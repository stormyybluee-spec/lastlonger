## L3.3 Epilogue. The promise: the story of money in ten questions (id rb-promise)

Kicker: Two chapters put money on trial with tables and tests. This one tells what they found as a story, through people who might have lived it. The people are imagined. The numbers are not.

[0 tldr]
- Money is a promise people make to each other about a future nobody has seen. Gold was one way to make the promise hard to break. Credit is the other way, and it is thousands of years old.
- Hard money keeps the promise scarce and rewards the saver, and it makes the borrower and the young wait. Money that stretches lets more people start, but it charges a quiet toll, the slow loss of what each coin can buy, and its gate opens most easily for those who already have money.
- No system has ended the argument. It is still about who pays, how much, who decides and who is let in.

[1 bridge] This story is built on two chapters
- rb-print: The first chapter asked whether printing money is a scam. It found a toll, not a scam, and it found that hard money has a toll of its own.
- rb-credit: The second chapter tested the claim that money made from promises is what lifted people out of poverty. It found that credit is a lever with a gate in front of it.

[2 callout] (How to read this chapter) This is a story, about 25 minutes long. When you see the word picture, the person is imagined. Every number comes from the two chapters before, where its source is given. A box at the end ties each scene to its question.

[3 callout] (Not advice) This chapter tells a story about how money works. It does not tell you to borrow, save or buy anything.


### Two angry voices

[5 p] Some people say that printing money is a scam. Governments and banks make new money from nothing, they say, and everyone who saved the old money is quietly robbed. They want hard money: money tied to something scarce, like gold, that nobody can make more of at will.

[6 p] Other people say the opposite. One of them, a reader of this site, wrote in to argue that money made from promises is the reason most of us are no longer poor farmers. Without it, the reader says, nobody could build anything before they had saved for it.

[7 p] Two chapters tested both voices with numbers. One word will come back again and again. It is the toll, like a fee paid on a road. Here the fee is the slow loss of what each coin can buy. This chapter walks through what they found, by way of the people who lived it.


### The field

[9 p] Picture a man in an English field in the year 1522. By one count about two of every three working men in his country farm as he does. He is not lazy and he is not a fool. He works from first light to dark, as his father did, and his father before him. Across those lives change came very slowly. What his country makes per person grows by about a fifth of one percent a year. No single life can feel that.

[10 p] He has few coins. But coins are not the thing he is missing most. A bad harvest means hunger no matter how many coins the king holds. What he lacks is a way to make more with the same two hands.

[11 p] And notice what he does when he needs seed before the harvest. He goes to a neighbour, and the neighbour remembers. A historian of early modern England found that most everyday buying and selling ran this way, on credit between households. One of the oldest written records of a loan is about 4,400 years old. So the oldest money is not gold. It is a sentence: I will pay you after the harvest.

[12 p] There is hope in that sentence, and there is fear in it too. If the harvest fails, the debt does not.


### The ships

[14 p] Now picture a boy on the docks of Spain in that same century. The ships are coming in from the Americas, low in the water with silver. By one historian's count about 210 million silver pesos landed in those hundred years. The boy must think his country can never be poor again.

[15 p] He is wrong, and it will hurt. He will grow old in a country where everything costs more. Across Western Europe prices rise three to four times in that century, by one estimate. The king of Spain, with all that silver, fails to pay his debts four times. Historians still argue about why. But the silver could buy things. It could not make them.

[16 p] Our own time has a harsher case. Zimbabwe printed money to pay the state's bills while its output was falling, and by late 2008 its prices were doubling about once a day, by one well-known estimate.

[17 p] Before the silver ships there had been the opposite fear. From the 1370s to the 1470s Europe ran short of silver, and the mints in Ghent and Bruges stopped making coins. Picture a trader there with good cloth to sell and neighbours who want it, and no coins to pay with. Too much money is one sickness. Too little is another. People have been afraid of both for a very long time.


### The king's promise

[19 p] London, 1694. The Bank of England is founded to lend the king 1.2 million pounds at about 8% interest. So the bank begins with a loan to the king. By 1815, after the long wars with Napoleon, Britain owes more than two years of everything it makes. It sounds like ruin.

[20 p] It was not. Britain led the industrial age, and by the early 1900s the debt had shrunk to about 30% of a year's income. The promise was kept while the country grew.

[21 p] The reader who wrote in calls credit a token we borrow from our own future. It is a fine picture, and it needs one gentle correction. Nobody can ship cement back from the year 2050. A bridge built this year is built with this year's hands. What travels through time is only the promise: who will be paid, later, out of what is made later.

[22 p] If the bridge makes the children richer, the promise is kept easily. If the money was used up, or the terms turn harsh, or the debt is owed to outsiders, the children pay for a meal they never tasted. Latin America's debt grew from 29 billion dollars in 1970 to 327 billion in 1982, much of it foreign loans. Then interest rates rose. Income per person fell by roughly a tenth in the 1980s, though not in every country, and about a third of the debt was later forgiven. Picture the parents behind that number, explaining to grown children why there was no work.


### The queue

[24 p] America, 1931. Picture a woman standing in a line outside a bank before dawn, in the cold, holding a small book that says how much she has saved. About 2,300 banks shut that year. There is no insurance. If her bank fails, the savings of her working life may go with it, and nothing she did caused it.

[25 p] Money is hard in her world. It is tied to gold, and nobody can simply make more. From 1930 to 1933 the supply of money falls by nearly 30%, and prices fall by about a quarter in those years. That makes debts about a third heavier, because a borrower still owes the same dollars and each dollar is harder to earn. Picture a farmer who borrowed honestly and never borrowed another cent. Each year he owes more in real things than the year before. Economists still argue whether the gold rules or the central bank's own choices did more harm. The farmer does not care which.

[26 p] Now picture another woman, a little further up the same line. The first woman's bank stays shut, but hers opens. Her book still says what it said. And because prices have fallen, her savings buy more bread than they did a year ago. Hard money kept its promise to her. She waited, and waiting paid. The same rule that crushed the farmer rewarded her. That is why savers have loved hard money, and why the argument never ends.

[27 p] After those years the law changed. Deposit insurance became law in America in 1933, and in 1934 only 61 banks failed. Other things helped too, and Canada got through the same years with no insurance and no failed banks. But from then on an American saver had a promise that her money would come back if her bank failed.

[28 p] Who wrote the rules of this system? Insiders had a large hand, and lawmakers passed them. Voters were seldom asked directly. Here is one time they were. Banks make new money when they lend. In 2018 Swiss voters were asked whether to stop them, and 75.7% said no. One vote cannot say why people chose as they did. But in that vote, the fear of a bank failing seems to have weighed more than anger at the toll of rising prices.


### The ruins

[30 p] 1945. Europe and Japan are in ruins. America holds most of the gold that governments have reported, and its share will peak at about 70% in 1948. Some ruined countries hold almost none. If gold had been the only way to begin again, the ruined countries would have faced a long wait.

[31 p] It took about five years. Western Europe's industry passed its level of 1938 by 1949, and Japan's real spending passed its pre-war level by 1951. Work and saving inside each country paid for most of it. Aid helped: on average it came to about 2.5% of the yearly income of the countries that received it. Credit between neighbours helped too.

[32 p] Picture a mother in West Germany in June 1948, holding a new kind of banknote. Her old savings have just been converted at 6.5 new marks for every 100 old ones. Most of what she saved through the war is gone, and nothing she did caused it either. Yet the rising prices stop, and trade begins to move again. Both things are true in the same summer. That is what a new start cost, and savers paid it.


### The weekend

[34 p] August 1971. An American president and fifteen advisers spend a weekend at Camp David. On the evening of 15 August he tells the country on television that the promise to swap dollars for gold is over. It is his decision. The last metal limit on money is gone. From now on the dollar is tied to no metal at all.

[35 p] The reader who wrote in, a real person and not one of our pictures, has a sharper way to say what changed. Before that weekend, promises leaned in the end on something scarce. After it, they lean only on laws, central banks and human judgment. That is close to right, and a later chapter will test how close. Whether the judgment has been good enough is the argument of our time.

[36 p] What followed was not a simple triumph. In the rich countries income per person had grown 3.5% a year from 1951 to 1973. From 1974 to 1989 it grew 2.1%. Ending gold did not speed things up there. These numbers cannot say whether it slowed them down.

[37 p] What it brought was room. In 1971 all the debts of American households, firms and government came to about one and a third years of what the country makes. By 2024 they came to about two and a half years. Room to build, and room to overreach, like a family that can now borrow far more than before. So two things followed that weekend. There was far more room to borrow. And growth did not clearly speed up.

[38 p] And the toll went on. A dollar today buys what 3 cents bought in 1913. A ringgit today buys only what 18 sen bought in 1971. There are 100 sen in a ringgit. Picture a grandmother in Malaysia who kept her ringgit in a tin. From 1971 to 2025 the tin lost about 82% of what it could buy, and nobody asked her. Money in a bank lost less in most of those decades. But the tin was not foolish. It was the safest place she knew, and the toll found her there.

[39 p] Now picture her granddaughter. Income per person in Malaysia grew about six times over those same years, after inflation. Pay and output grew faster than prices. The chapters do not show that the toll paid for the growth. They show only that both happened in the same years, to the same family. It is hard to hold both in one heart.


### The counter

[41 p] Today. Picture a young woman in Kuala Lumpur, in her first job, sitting at a bank counter. She wants a small flat. In 2019 the middle-priced house in Malaysia cost 4.1 times what the middle household earns in a year.

[42 p] The officer asks three questions. Do you have income? Do you have something valuable to put up in case you cannot pay, like a house? Do you have a record of paying on time? She notices what the three questions have in common. Each is easier to answer if you already have money.

[43 p] If the answer is yes, she gets a key and a debt. The middle new borrower in Malaysia pays 41% of income on loans. If the answer is no, there is the licensed moneylender, who may charge more. She is still luckier than many. Around the world 1.3 billion adults had no account at all in 2024.

[44 p] Think of a game of Monopoly that began long before she sat down. The streets were already bought. Hard money would tell her to save first. Under hard money, the coins she saves would buy a little more each year. But she must save first, and most of the money is already held by people who came before her. Money that stretches says she can start now, and she will owe. Neither gives her an even start. Saving means a long wait while others move ahead. Owing means she can start now, but she begins by paying. By one American count, about 58% of one older generation owned a home by the age of 30. Of a younger one, about 41% did.


### The lever

[46 p] Picture two families in America in 2007. Both have borrowed to buy a home. The first is like the typical owner. Count what the typical owner has after paying off all debts. It comes to $396,200. For the typical renter it is $10,400. Owners and renters differ in many ways, so the house is not the whole reason. But about half of that wealth is the home.

[47 p] The second family is like the poorest fifth of owners. For every 100 dollars of things they own, they owe about 80, so only about 20 is truly theirs. Then house prices fall. In 2007 they were worth about $30,000 after debts. By 2010 it is almost nothing.

[48 p] It was the same tool. A lever makes a small push big, in both directions. Here the loan is the lever. A rising house price becomes a big gain. A falling one becomes a big loss. In one home the loan is remembered as the best thing the family ever did. In the other, nobody talks about it.

[49 p] Malaysia recorded 5,977 bankruptcies in 2024, and 46% of them came from personal loans. Picture one of those people, learning to dread the post. The law is kinder than it was. England stopped jailing most debtors in 1869, and in Malaysia a bankrupt can be released after three years if the conditions are met. An old line from the book of Proverbs puts the fear in a few words: the borrower is servant to the lender. The evidence puts it another way. Credit multiplies what a borrower already is: their income, their judgment and their luck.


### The sword and the rocket

[51 p] Picture a collector who pays 100,000 ringgit for a hand-made sword. People sometimes say that money spent like this is gone. It is not gone. It became the blacksmith's income and went on moving, because one person's spending is another person's pay. That is the quiet wonder inside money. It is also why it is so frightening when many people stop spending at once.

[52 p] The largest hope of the reader who wrote in goes further. It is that promises about the future are how people attempt things no saver could pay for alone. The reader's example is a rocket. Picture the people who build it for years, paid every month, before it earns anything at all. Somebody's promise is paying them. The two chapters support one part of that hope. America's interstate highways, built from the 1950s, gave a large one-time lift to the output of the industries that used them.

[53 p] They do not prove the rest. The test for a rocket is the same as the test for a road. Does it leave the future richer than the debt it leaves behind? Some bets will. Some will not, as Latin America learned in the 1980s, and then somebody pays. Who that somebody is has been the argument all along, from the field to the counter.


### What the story says

[55 p] So here is the verdict again, this time with the people in it.

[56 p] Is printing money a scam? Not in the plain sense, because nothing about it is hidden. It is a toll, and the grandmother with the tin paid it. Hard money is not innocent. Ask the farmer of the 1930s, whose debt grew heavier every year he worked. Ask the first woman in the queue too. Her bank's promise broke, and tight money helped to break it. But hard money is not a villain either. Ask the second woman, whose savings bought more bread. And credit is not a gift. It is a lever with a gate in front of it, and the young woman at the counter is standing at the gate.

[57 p] Think of the field and the ships. Plenty comes from what people make. Spain's silver could buy plenty. It could not make it. But making takes time, and people must eat while they build. A promise lets them start.

[58 p] Again and again the promise won over the metal. Sometimes voters chose it, as the Swiss did. Sometimes a few people chose it for everyone, in one weekend. Either way, they were not simply fooled. The fear of the queue was real. So is the price: the toll and the gate. And the argument is still about who pays, how much, who decides and who is let in.

[59 p] The man in the field of 1522 could not have imagined the counter in Kuala Lumpur. He would have understood the promise, though. He made one every spring.

[61.0 p] The reader who wrote in has replied with four new points. Before 1971 credit rested on scarce gold, and after it on the judgment of governments and central banks alone. Output was scarce in the past because the whole chain of money, schooling, knowledge and central power was missing. Moving money was once dangerous, because a merchant on the road could be robbed. And new technology costs an enormous price before it pays, so someone must issue the promises that fund it. None of these has been tested yet. Later chapters will test them. Until then they are open questions, not findings.
