# Red team, round 13: rb-promise (Epilogue. The promise)

Scope: DRAFT_R13.md only. No other files, no web. Facts are left to the verifier. This review is about fairness, framing, craft and wording.

## 1. Blocking points, ranked

### RT-01. The queue is a failed bank promise, but the story bills it to hard money. Blocks [19], [46], [48]
What is wrong: The woman in 1931 loses her savings because her bank cannot keep its promise. That is a failure of credit, not of metal. The draft then uses her twice as a witness against hard money ("Hard money is not innocent. Ask the woman in the queue") and as a reason people chose "the promise over the metal". This is the most emotional scene in the chapter, and it is pointed at the wrong target. The farmer in [19] is a fair hard-money witness. The woman is a mixed case at best: tight money made the runs worse, but the thing that broke was a bank's promise. A hard-money reader will see this at once and stop trusting the rest.
Fix in [46]:
OLD: `Ask the woman in the queue and the farmer whose debt grew while he slept.`
NEW: `Ask the farmer whose debt grew heavier every year he worked. Ask the woman in the queue too. Her bank's promise broke, and tight money made it break faster.`
Fix in [48] is under RT-03.

### RT-02. Every saver hurt by a stretching money is consoled or corrected. Every person hurt by hard money is mourned. Blocks [25], [30]
What is wrong: The two tolls do not get equal human weight. The woman in the queue gets "nothing she did caused it" and no consolation. The grandmother with the tin gets one sentence of loss, then a quiet correction ("In a bank she would have done better"), then a consolation (her granddaughter, the schools, the roads). The German mother in 1948 also loses nearly all her savings, and the next line is "And the shops have bread". So the inflation victims are always balanced inside their own paragraph, while the hard-money victims are left standing in the cold. There is also no saver or pensioner whom hard money helped. The draft says in [35] that under hard money "the coin would gain value", but no person ever feels that gain. A missing scene in section 2 fills this gap with facts already in the draft.
Fix in [30], to remove the implied blame:
OLD: `In a bank she would have done better, since deposits beat inflation in most of those decades.`
NEW: `Money in a bank lost less, since deposits beat inflation in most of those decades. But the tin was not foolish. It was the safest place she knew, and the toll found her there.`

### RT-03. "People kept choosing the promise over the metal" claims more than the story shows. Block [48]
What is wrong: The story itself shows that the biggest step away from metal was taken by one president and fifteen advisers over one weekend ([27]: "It is his decision"). It also says the woman in the queue "was never asked" ([21]). The only real vote in the chapter is the Swiss vote of 2018. So "people kept choosing" contradicts the chapter's own scenes. "Many of them had stood in the queue" then turns an imagined person into a cause of history.
Fix:
OLD: `People kept choosing the promise over the metal. They were not simply fooled. Many of them had stood in the queue.`
NEW: `Again and again the promise won over the metal. Sometimes voters chose it, as the Swiss did. Sometimes a few people chose it for everyone, in one weekend. Either way, they were not simply fooled. The memory of the queue was real.`

### RT-04. The story says the reader is right on a point it later calls untested. Blocks [28], [51]
What is wrong: In [28] the narrator says of the reader's claim "That is right." In [51] the same claim ("Before 1971 credit rested on scarce gold, and after it on governance alone") is listed as one of four points that "None of these has been tested yet." Both cannot stand. This is also the clearest place where the chapter flatters the reader who made the pro-credit thesis.
Fix:
OLD: `That is right. Whether the judgment has been good enough is the argument of our time.`
NEW: `That is close to right, and a later chapter will test how close. Whether the judgment has been good enough is the argument of our time.`

### RT-05. "The same years that emptied the tin built the schools and the roads." Block [30]
What is wrong: The sentence makes the toll sound like the price of the schools. The chapters show the two happened in the same years. They do not show that one paid for the other. A story line that ties the grandmother's loss to her granddaughter's gain does by feeling what the evidence has not done.
Fix:
OLD: `The same years that emptied the tin built the schools and the roads.`
NEW: `The same years that emptied the tin also saw the schools and the roads built.`

### RT-06. Proverbs is used as the final authority in the lever scene. Block [39]
What is wrong: For a mixed-faith readership, mostly in Malaysia, one scripture is quoted and then endorsed with "still stands". It closes the scene with a religious verdict against borrowing. That is unfair to readers of other faiths and of none, and it reads close to advice. Quoting the line is fine. Endorsing it is not.
Fix:
OLD: `But an old line in the book of Proverbs still stands: the borrower is servant to the lender.`
NEW: `An old line from the book of Proverbs puts the fear in a few words: the borrower is servant to the lender.`

### RT-07. One Swiss vote is made to explain a whole century of feeling. Block [21]
What is wrong: "The fear of the queue has been stronger than the anger at the toll" is a strong claim about motives, built on a single vote in one country. The same paragraph uses the imagined woman as a historical witness ("The woman in the queue was never asked"), though the 1910 plan came twenty years before her queue. "Drawn up in secret" next to "duck hunt" also leans toward a plot story about insiders, which the site should avoid.
Fix:
OLD: `The fear of the queue has been stronger than the anger at the toll.`
NEW: `One vote cannot say why people chose as they did. But in that vote, the fear of a bank failing seems to have weighed more than anger at the toll.`

### RT-08. Two imagined families carry real group averages, and the house is given credit it has not earned. Block [37]
What is wrong: "Picture two families" marks them as imagined, but they are then given exact survey medians as if they were their own numbers. An 18-year-old could read "$396,200" as one family's real result. The comparison with renters also suggests the house made the owner rich. Owners and renters differ in age, income and much else. Then [38] says the first family "tells the story of the house that made them", which turns the median into a cause.
Fix:
OLD: `One is the typical owner, and by 2022 that owner's net worth is $396,200, against $10,400 for the typical renter.`
NEW: `One is like the typical owner. In 2022 the typical owner was worth $396,200, against $10,400 for the typical renter. Owners and renters differ in many ways, so the house is not the whole reason.`

### RT-09. Latin America is placed right after "the money was simply eaten", and an imagined scene is not marked. Block [16]
What is wrong: The order of sentences suggests the region ate its loans. The draft's own next sentence says the break came when interest rates rose. That is a quiet stereotype of a region. Then "Behind that number were parents explaining to grown children why there was no work" is an imagined scene with no "picture", against the chapter's own rule in [2].
Fix 1:
OLD: `If the money was simply eaten, the children pay for a meal they never tasted.`
NEW: `If the money was simply eaten, or the terms turn harsh, the children pay for a meal they never tasted.`
Fix 2:
OLD: `Behind that number were parents explaining to grown children why there was no work.`
NEW: `Picture the parents behind that number, explaining to grown children why there was no work.`

### RT-10. Other imagined details are not marked. Blocks [39], [41]
What is wrong: [39] "Behind each one is a person who learned to dread the post" is an imagined feeling given to 5,977 real people. [41] "Remember the collector who paid 100,000 ringgit for a sword" can be read as a real sale. It was a thought experiment in the first chapter.
Fix 1:
OLD: `Behind each one is a person who learned to dread the post.`
NEW: `Picture one of them, learning to dread the post.`
Fix 2:
OLD: `Remember the collector who paid 100,000 ringgit for a sword.`
NEW: `Remember the imagined collector from the first chapter, who paid 100,000 ringgit for a sword.`

### RT-11. The summary tilts toward credit. Block [0]
What is wrong: Hard money "makes the borrower and the young wait". Money that stretches "lets more people start". But the counter scene [33] to [35] shows that the gate opens most easily for people who already have money. The summary gives credit the hope and hard money the delay. It also never says that hard money rewards the saver.
Fix:
OLD: `Hard money keeps the promise scarce and safe, and it makes the borrower and the young wait. Money that stretches lets more people start, and it charges a quiet toll and guards a gate.`
NEW: `Hard money keeps the promise scarce and rewards the saver, and it makes the borrower and the young wait. Money that stretches lets more people start, but it charges a quiet toll, and its gate opens most easily for those who already have money.`

### RT-12. The last insider box is honest in one line and confusing and flattering in others. Block [51]
What is wrong: "None of these has been tested yet" is good and must stay. But "four sharper points" praises the reader before any test. "Each of the seven questions" is confusing: the chapter has ten questions and the reader has four points. A reader with English as a second language will not know which seven.
Fix 1:
OLD: `The reader who began all this has replied with four sharper points.`
NEW: `The reader who began all this has replied with four new points.`
Fix 2:
OLD: `Each of the seven questions will get a chapter of its own, and these points will be tested there.`
NEW: `Later chapters will test them. Until then, they are open questions, not findings.`

### RT-13. A counterfactual is stated as fact. Block [23]
What is wrong: "would have waited for generations" is a guess about a world that did not happen. The table in [50] says only that gold was not the engine.
Fix:
OLD: `If gold were the only way to begin again, the ruined countries would have waited for generations.`
NEW: `If gold had been the only way to begin again, the ruined countries would have faced a long wait.`

### RT-14. Lines that read as advice. Blocks [30], [39]
What is wrong: [30] "In a bank she would have done better" tells the reader where to keep money. RT-02 fixes it. [39] "Credit multiplies what you already are" speaks to the reader as a future borrower and sounds like a warning not to borrow.
Fix:
OLD: `Credit multiplies what you already are. Your income, your judgment and your luck.`
NEW: `Credit multiplies what a borrower already is. Their income, their judgment and their luck.`

### Checks that passed
- (f) The sword and the rocket scene tests the hope. It names the one part the chapters support and says plainly that they do not prove the rest. It could be sharper by naming a bet that failed (see section 2, edit 9).
- (g) Apart from RT-14, nothing reads as a push to borrow or not to borrow. The closing callout [52] is clear.
- (e) No people or faith is blamed. The Spanish boy, the German mother and the Malaysian grandmother are drawn with respect. Proverbs (RT-06) and Latin America (RT-09) are the only risks.
- [20] is a model of fairness: it gives the insurance its due and then names Canada.

## 2. The craft of the story

### Where it is alive
- The field [4] to [7]. Calm, concrete, patient. "It is a sentence: I will pay you after the harvest" is the best line in the chapter. "If the harvest fails, the debt does not" turns the hope into fear in eight words.
- The ships [9] and [10]. The boy who "must think his country can never be poor again", then "He is wrong, and it will hurt." That is real narration.
- The mother in 1948 [25]. "Both things are true on the same morning." This is the chapter at its best: two truths held in one scene, no lecture.
- The lever [38]. "It was the same tool." Short and hard.
- The ending [49]. It lands. The man in the field returns, and "He made one every spring" closes the circle without a moral.

### Where it falls back into report voice
- [29] reads as a table in prose: two growth rates, a hedge, then two credit ratios. The hedge "though these numbers cannot say it caused the slowdown" is honest but it stops the music.
- [34] stacks four numbers (41%, 12%, 18%, 1.3 billion) in four sentences. The young woman disappears while the numbers pass.
- [37] gives an imagined family a survey median to the dollar. The scene becomes a chart.
- [21] crowds a secret plan, a duck hunt, lawmakers, an imagined woman and a Swiss vote into one paragraph.
- [30] has too much to do: two price levels, a tin, a bank, a granddaughter, income growth, schools. The grandmother needs room.

### Weakest scene
The lever [37] to [39]. It has the most numbers, the least picture, an unmarked imagined feeling, and it ends on scripture. The two families never become people. One fix is to keep only one number per family and give each one a moment, as [25] does.

### Order of scenes
The order works. It is mostly by time, and the jump from 1694 to Latin America in 1982 is justified by the idea. Two small points. First, the queue [18] comes before the ruins [23], which is right by date, but it means the chapter's saddest hard-money scene sits in the middle with no saver to answer it. The missing scene below fixes that inside the same section. Second, the reader's voice appears in [15], [28], [42] and [51]. Four times is enough to feel like a second narrator. That is fine, if the narrator does not agree with him more than the evidence does (RT-04).

### The recurring image
The promise is used well. It is defined in [6], tested in [14] and [16], broken in [18], kept in [20], and becomes the verdict in [47] and [49]. The man in the field is used only at the start and the end. One short return in the middle would make him a thread, not a frame. Edit 10 below does this without new facts.

### Missing scene (fills the gap in RT-01 and RT-02)
Place at the end of [19], before [20]. 82 words. Uses only facts in the draft.

> Now picture another woman, a little further up the same line. Her bank opens. Her book still says what it said. And because prices have fallen by about a quarter, her savings buy more bread than they did a year ago. Hard money kept its promise to her. She waited, and waiting paid. The same rule that ruined the farmer rewarded her. That is why savers have loved hard money, and why the argument never ends.

## 3. Six readers

- Hard-money saver: the queue, a bank failure, is used as the case against hard money, and no saver ever feels the gain that hard money gave (RT-01, RT-02).
- Credit optimist: the rocket hope is answered in three short lines with "They do not prove the rest", while the bankruptcy scene gets Proverbs and dread, so the hope feels shut down before it is tested.
- Malaysian 18-year-old, English as a second language: [34] and [35] pack numbers and the "coin would gain value while she waits, in the hands of people who came before her" clause into hard sentences, and "the seven questions" in [51] makes no sense to them.
- Historian: "People kept choosing the promise over the metal" and "the fear of the queue has been stronger than the anger at the toll" turn one weekend and one Swiss vote into a story about whole peoples (RT-03, RT-07).
- Person who went bankrupt: "The other family does not tell the story at all" and "learned to dread the post" frame them as silent and ashamed, and "your judgment" in [39] hints that it was their fault.
- Central banker: [21] leans on "drawn up in secret" and a duck hunt, and [28] says promises now lean "only on laws, central banks and human judgment" with no word on why those rules were built, except the queue.
