## L3.3 The weekend that cut the anchor: what 1971 really changed (id rb-1971)

Kicker: One weekend in August 1971 ended the dollar's last link to gold. A reader says that weekend is the hinge of modern money: before it credit was limited, after it borrowing became unlimited, under stable and controlled governance. This chapter tests each word of that claim.

[0 tldr]
- Before 1971 gold did limit money, but loosely. It limited America's promise to foreign governments far more than it limited lending at home. Bank lending grew fast under the gold link, from a low post-war base, and rose far higher against income only after the link was cut.
- After 1971 borrowing became elastic, not unlimited. Three limits replaced gold: inflation, lenders, and the currency a country borrows in. The country that prints the world's main reserve money feels them least, but America still met the inflation limit in 1980 and again in 2022.
- The governance that replaced gold took about twenty years to appear. Prices then stayed calm for about 35 years, much as in the 1950s and 1960s, but currency crises came more often.
- Rich countries grew more slowly after 1973. Late developers such as Korea, Malaysia, China and India grew faster. The numbers do not show that the money system caused either.

### The claim on trial

[4 p] A reader of this site wrote in with a sharper version of the argument from chapter 02. Here it is in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. Before 1971 money was tied to gold, and gold is scarce. So all credit before 1971 rested on something limited.
- Part 2. After 1971 the system is tied to no hard asset. So unlimited borrowing is at last available.
- Part 3. The new system runs under stable, controlled governance.
- Part 4. Its purpose is to let productivity and technology advance, and that is what it has done.

[6 p] Each part can be tested. But first, the weekend itself.


### The weekend

[8 p] On Friday 13 August 1971 President Richard Nixon flew to Camp David, the presidential retreat. With him were fifteen advisers, among them Arthur Burns, the head of America's central bank, which sets interest rates, John Connally, the Treasury Secretary, and a senior Treasury official named Paul Volcker. They had a problem that could not wait.

[9 p] Since 1944 America had promised foreign governments one ounce of gold for every 35 dollars they handed in. The promise worked while America held more gold than foreign governments held in dollars. By 1971 that was no longer true.

[10 p] Why had the claims piled up? Part of the cause was built in. The world needed more dollars to trade and to save, and it could get them only if America spent more abroad than it earned. As early as 1960 the economist Robert Triffin warned that the more dollars flowed out, the less anyone would trust the gold behind them. Part was policy. From about 1965 prices in America rose faster, while the government paid for the Vietnam War and new programmes at home. And part lay with countries such as Germany, which piled up dollars and were slow to change their exchange rates. Historians still weigh each cause differently.

[11 table]
| Year | America's gold | Dollar claims held by foreign governments | Could the promise be kept? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1960 | $17.8 billion | $10.5 billion | Yes. More gold than claims |
| 1968 | $10.9 billion | $12.1 billion | Not in full |
| 1971 | $10.5 billion in June | $30.8 billion in June | No. Claims were about 2.9 times the gold |

[12 p] Picture a cloakroom that holds 10 coats but has handed out 30 tickets. If everyone rushes back at once, two in three go home without a coat. That rush is a run. In 1971 America was that cloakroom. It could have paid about one in three foreign governments. It had three ways out. It could raise interest rates so people kept their dollars, but that costs jobs, and America already had rising prices and too little work. It could make the dollar cheaper against gold and other money, which breaks part of the promise. Or it could stop paying out gold. That weekend Nixon would choose the third way, and in December the second would follow.

[13 p] By the summer of 1971 runs on the dollar had begun. On Sunday evening, 15 August, Nixon went on television. He closed the gold window, which meant foreign governments could no longer swap dollars for gold. He froze wages and prices for 90 days and put a 10% tax on imports.

[14 quote] "I am determined that the American dollar must never again be a hostage in the hands of international speculators." (President Nixon, 15 August 1971)

[15 p] He said the step was to "suspend temporarily" the swapping of dollars for gold. It was never undone. A new set of fixed exchange rates was agreed in December 1971 and lasted little more than a year. In March 1973 six European countries let their currencies float together against the dollar, and the fixed system ended. The anchor was gone.

[16 p] What does floating mean? Under the old system a government had to defend a fixed price for its money. Under floating, buyers and sellers set the price every day, so the market judges the money all the time. If people think a government prints or borrows too much, they sell its money and its price falls. Then everything bought from abroad, from fuel to machines, costs more. Gold checked a government only at the gold window. A floating price checks it every working day. In Malaysia, in December 1971, the ringgit's rate to the US dollar was reset, and in June 1973 Bank Negara let it float.


### Test 1. Did gold really limit credit before 1971?

[18 p] The reader says credit before 1971 rested on something limited. There is real evidence for that, and real evidence against.

[19 table]
| Evidence that gold did limit money | Evidence that the limit was loose |
|---|---|
| Until March 1968 America's central bank had to hold gold worth 25% of the banknotes it issued. In February 1968 Congress was told that of about 12 billion dollars of gold, only 1.3 billion was still free, meaning not already locked up as cover for banknotes | Congress removed the rule in two steps: for bank deposits in 1965, and for banknotes in 1968, when the free gold was about to run out. A limit that is lifted when it binds is a loose limit |
| America's gold could not cover its promises to foreign governments, as the table above shows. That is what forced the weekend at Camp David | The promise was to foreign governments only. No rule tied a home loan in Ohio to an ounce of gold |
| Bank lending in 18 rich countries was 53% of yearly output in 1970, lower than the 58% of 1913. By 2007 it was 111% | Lending grew fastest while the gold link was still there, though from a low post-war base: 9.0% a year after inflation from 1950 to 1970, against 5.8% from 1971 to 1990 and 3.3% from 1991 to 2019 |
| World debt of all kinds was about 97% of world output in the 1950s. In 2024 it was about 235%. The 1950s figure still carried debts left from the war | Even under the old gold standard, gold coin was only about 5% to 9% of the money in Britain in 1914, by one estimate. Most of the rest was bank deposits and notes |

[20 p] Put the two columns together. Gold was a real limit at the top of the system, on what America could promise other governments. It was a weak limit on lending inside each country, and it was loosened when it pulled tight.

[21 p] Rules on interest rates and on money crossing borders also held lending down. And in the 1960s dollar lending outside America, called eurodollars, grew fast beyond both America's rules and the gold rule. Credit grew fast under the gold link partly because it started from a low level after the war. But its level against income climbed far higher once the link was gone.

[22 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds in part. Money before 1971 did rest on something limited. But the limit was a rope, not a wall. It held back governments more than it held back banks, and it was let out when it pulled.


### Test 2. Is borrowing unlimited now?

[24 p] The reader says that after 1971 unlimited borrowing is at last available. The record says something different. Borrowing became elastic. It can stretch a long way. If unlimited means only that no metal now caps it, the reader is right. If it means no limit at all, the record disagrees. Three limits took the place of gold, and each has forced a government to change course.

[25 p] The first limit is inflation. Through the 1970s American prices rose in two waves, to above 12% around 1974 and above 14% in 1980. The central bank, now led by Paul Volcker, the same man who had been at Camp David, pushed interest rates to nearly 20% in 1981. At that price few people borrow to buy houses or cars, and prices stop racing. That broke the inflation, and it broke a great deal else. Unemployment reached 10.8% at the end of 1982. Among house builders it was 22%, and among car makers 24%. Builders mailed bricks and pieces of building timber to the central bank, the Associated Press later reported, and car dealers mailed the keys of cars they could not sell.

[26 p] The second limit is the lender. In September 2022 Britain's government announced large tax cuts paid for by borrowing. Within days the interest rate on its 30-year bonds, which are loans investors make to the government, jumped by 1.6 percentage points. Pension funds that had borrowed to hold these bonds had to sell as prices fell, which made the fall worse, until the Bank of England stepped in to buy bonds for a short time. Most of the tax cuts were withdrawn within about a month, and the prime minister resigned after 44 days in office, press reports say.

[27 p] The third limit is the currency. A country that borrows in money its own central bank issues can always print to repay, at the cost of inflation. So, some economists stress, the real limit is how fast prices rise, not how big the debt is. A country that borrows in someone else's money cannot. Mexico told American officials and the IMF in August 1982 that it could not pay what was due on about 80 billion dollars of debt. In 1997 a similar trap closed on much of East Asia, though not on every country in the same way.

The table shows its debt in the money it earned. The firm did nothing new. Its debt grew because its currency fell. Bank Negara could print ringgit, but not dollars. That is a limit printing cannot fix.

[32 p] Malaysia's road out was its own. It took no IMF loan. By one study using IMF data, its short-term foreign debt was smaller than its reserves, while Thailand's and Korea's were larger. A study by Ethan Kaplan and Dani Rodrik found that after its controls Malaysia recovered faster, with smaller job losses, than the countries that took IMF loans. How much the controls themselves helped is still argued. In 2012 the IMF said such controls can be useful in some cases.

[34 p] Today Malaysia's government borrows almost wholly in ringgit, though in 1997 the weak point was short-term foreign borrowing by banks and firms. At the end of 2022, 97.3% of its federal government debt was in ringgit, and a law caps that debt at 65% of yearly output. It stood at 60.4%.

[35 p] So who can borrow with the fewest limits? The country whose money everyone else saves in and trades in. In mid 2026 the US dollar was 56.7% of the world's official currency reserves. Other countries need dollars to trade and to save, so America can usually sell its debt more easily. Even so, it met the first limit in 1980, and again in 2022.

[36 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 does not hold as stated. Borrowing after 1971 is elastic, not unlimited. Gold was replaced by three other limits: inflation, lenders and the currency of the debt. They bind a small country much harder than they bind America.

[37 check] Q: A friend says a government that prints its own money can never run out, so its borrowing has no limit. What three limits would you name?
A: Inflation: print too much and prices rise until the central bank must raise rates, as America did in 1981. Lenders: they can demand higher interest, as Britain found in 2022. And the currency: a country that borrowed in dollars cannot print dollars, as Asia found in 1997.


### Test 3. Is the governance stable and controlled?

[39 p] The reader says the new system runs under stable, controlled governance. Something did replace gold as the anchor. It is a promise by a central bank to keep inflation near a target, which in America is 2%. But that promise was not ready in 1971. New Zealand's came about twenty years later, in 1990. America announced its own in 2012, forty-one years after 1971.

[40 table]
| When | What happened | Inflation in America |
|---|---|---|
| 1971 to 1982 | A weakened anchor of fixed rates until 1973, then none. In October 1979 the central bank began to target the supply of money | 7.8% a year on average |
| 1990 | New Zealand became the first country to give its central bank an inflation target. By 2010, 26 countries had one | No single figure |
| 1983 to 2019 | The long calm. America announced its own 2% target in January 2012 | 2.7% a year on average, close to the 2.5% of 1949 to 1970 |
| 2007 to 2009 | The financial crisis. American output fell 4.3% and unemployment reached 10%. Congress approved up to 700 billion dollars to buy bad assets and support banks and other firms | No single figure |
| 2022 | The target was badly missed | 9.1% in the year to June, as reported. The euro area is reported to have reached 10.6% in October. Prices then cooled, and in 2024 American prices rose 2.9% |

[41 p] So after a decade of fast-rising prices, American inflation stayed near 2 to 3% a year for about 35 years, much as in the gold-linked years after the war. Crises are a second test, counted by Michael Bordo, Barry Eichengreen and two colleagues in 21 countries.

[42 table]
| Era | The money rule | Crises started per 100 country-years, 21 countries | Banking crises per 100 country-years, 18 rich countries |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1880 to 1913 | Gold standard | 6.4 | 4.2 |
| 1919 to 1939 | Between the wars: gold restored, then given up | 13.4 | 7.1 |
| 1945 to 1971 | Dollar tied to gold | 7.9 | 0 |
| 1973 to 1997 | After Bretton Woods | 10.7 | 3.1 (1973 to 2017, a longer span) |

[43 p] A country-year is one country in one year. The worst era was between the wars, when many countries went back to gold and then left it in the Great Depression. After 1973 crises came more often than under either gold-linked system, though not clearly longer, mostly because of currency crises. Banking crises in rich countries did not rise. None is recorded from 1945 to 1971. Those were also years of tight rules on banks and on money crossing borders, so gold may not deserve the credit.

[44 p] Both sides have a strong voice here. Friedrich Hayek, a hard-money economist, wrote in his book Denationalisation of Money that "history is largely a history of inflation, and usually of inflations engineered by governments". Lewis Lehrman, a campaigner for gold, has argued in the press that gold would force governments to balance their budgets. Ben Bernanke, later head of America's central bank, pointed in a 2004 speech to "the substantial decline in macroeconomic volatility" and gave better policy part of the credit. The historian Barry Eichengreen, in his book Golden Fetters, called the gold standard "a central factor in the worldwide Depression".

[45 p] And who controls it? Not voters, directly. America's central bank governors are named by the President and confirmed by the Senate, for terms of 14 years. In Malaysia the Agong appoints Bank Negara's governor, the bank says. When Swiss voters were asked in 2014 to make their central bank hold at least a fifth of its assets in gold and never sell it, about 77% said no, swissinfo reported.

[46 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds in part. There is governance, and for about 35 years prices stayed calm. But it arrived late, it missed badly in 2022, and it did not stop the banking crisis of 2008. Crises came more often than after the war, though less often than between the wars. Voters control it only at one remove, through the leaders who appoint its officials.


### Test 4. Did it let productivity and technology advance?

[48 p] This is the reader's largest claim: that a money system free of gold exists so that progress can be paid for before it pays back. Start with growth in income per person, after inflation.

[49 table]
| Growth per person, a year | 1950 to 1973 | 1973 to 1990 | 1990 to 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | 2.5% | 2.0% | 1.5% |
| 19 rich countries, our estimate | 3.7% | 2.2% | 1.5% |
| South Korea | 6.0% | 7.9% | 3.6% |
| Malaysia | 2.2% | 4.2% | 4.1% |
| China | 2.8% | 4.1% | 6.2% |
| India | 1.4% | 2.6% | 4.4% |

[50 p] Read across the rows and two stories appear. The rich countries grew fastest before 1973, and more slowly in each period after. The late developers did the opposite. Korea, Malaysia, China and India all grew faster from 1973 to 1990 than before. Malaysia, China and India kept up that pace after 1990, while Korea slowed as it grew rich.

[51 p] Neither story proves a cause. Until about 1973 the rich countries were catching up after a war, and that probably explains much of their fast growth. The oil shocks also began in 1973. Dearer oil makes almost everything cost more to make and move, so prices rise while factories slow. And Japan, the star late developer of the gold-linked years, grew about 8% a year per person from 1950 to 1973.

[52 p] The late developers changed many things at once: schools, factories, trade rules. Malaysia passed a Free Trade Zone Act in 1971, and electronics factories soon rose in Penang. World trade rose from about a quarter of world output in 1970 to more than half in 2024.

[53 p] A world of floating money and freer movement of capital may have helped them. But Korea, China and Malaysia often grew while managing their exchange rates and limiting money crossing borders. The numbers cannot show which mattered more, because so many things changed at the same time. Productivity, the most direct test, slowed in America too.

[54 table]
| America | 1948 to 1973 | 1973 to 1995 |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity, meaning what workers and machines make together, growth a year (Brookings) | about 2.1% | about 0.5% |

[55 p] Productivity picked up again after 1995, in the computer years, by the same study. What about research? America's spending on research was 2.79% of its output in 1964, when the gold link still held. It fell to 2.07% by 1978 and rose to 3.43% by 2022. Research money did not wait for the anchor to be cut, and its low point came seven years after the cut.

[56 p] For a Malaysian reader the picture is mixed in a familiar way. Prices rose 0.9% a year in the 1960s, 6.3% a year from 1971 to 1982, and 2.5% a year from 1983 to 2019, by World Bank figures. Growth per person rose from 2.2% a year before 1973 to about 4% after. The cost of rising prices, the toll, rose for a decade and then fell back, while income grew faster.

[57 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 is not shown. For late developers, Malaysia among them, the years after 1973 were their fastest since 1950. For the rich countries they were slower than the years before, and American productivity slowed too. The evidence does not show that cutting the gold link caused either. Research spending was high under gold too.

[60 p] Here is the claim again, in a form the record can carry.

[61 steps]
- Before 1971 gold tied the hands of governments more than of banks. The tie was loosened when it pulled. After years of policy that let dollars pile up abroad, one weekend it was cut.
- After 1971 money became elastic. It can stretch a long way, as in 2008. It is not unlimited. Inflation, lenders and foreign-currency debt now set the limits, on borrowers rather than on a promise to foreign governments, and they can bite hard, as in 1981 and 1997.
- A new anchor, the inflation target, took about twenty years to appear, and forty-one to reach America. It has held most of the time and missed badly some of the time. It is run by officials whom elected leaders appoint.
- The years since have been the fastest since 1950 for late developers and slower for the rich world. Whether elastic money caused either is not shown.

[62 p] One number speaks for the hard-money side. An ounce of gold cost 35 dollars on that weekend. On 16 November 2026 it cost about 3,900 dollars, by one price chart. Measured in gold, the dollar has lost about 99% of its value since the anchor was cut. But gold is a poor yardstick because its own price jumps around, from about 850 dollars in January 1980 to about 5,590 in January 2026, by price charts. Measured in what people buy, the loss is smaller, as the table below shows. One number speaks for the other side. In those same years income per person in Malaysia, after inflation, grew about 4% a year, as the growth table shows. Neither number proves the case for its side.

[63 table]

|---|---|---|

[64 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that the slowdown in rich countries after 1973 was caused by the end of the gold link, and not by the end of post-war catching up. Second, that late developers could have grown as fast under a gold-linked dollar. Third, that the three new limits bind as tightly as gold did, in which case elastic money is not very elastic at all.

[65 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): How governments fought to save the gold price before 1971
[65.0 p] From November 1961 America and seven European central banks ran the London Gold Pool. They sold their own gold on the open market to hold the price at 35 dollars. From December 1967 to March 1968 the pool lost about 3 billion dollars of gold, and America's share was 2.2 billion. In March 1968 the pool was closed and a two-tier market began: one price for central banks, a free price for everyone else. It lasted until the weekend at Camp David.

[65.1 p] Some economists argue the system could have been saved. Michael Bordo and Robert McCauley point out that America's gold position in the 1960s was no worse than Britain's around 1900, when the Bank of England held very little gold against what it owed, under 5% by one account. On this view the end came from policy choices, not from a shortage of metal.

[67 callout] (Not advice) This chapter weighs an argument about how money works. It does not tell you to buy gold, hold dollars or borrow. Every number has a date and will change.
