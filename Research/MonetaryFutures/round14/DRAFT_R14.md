## L3.3 The weekend that cut the anchor: what 1971 really changed (id rb-1971)

Kicker: One weekend in August 1971 ended the dollar's last link to gold. A reader says that weekend is the hinge of modern money: before it credit was limited, after it borrowing became unlimited, under stable and controlled governance. This chapter tests each word of that claim.

[0 tldr]
- Before 1971 gold did limit money, but loosely. It limited America's promise to foreign governments far more than it limited lending at home. Bank lending grew fastest while the gold link was still in place.
- After 1971 borrowing became elastic, not unlimited. Three limits replaced gold: inflation, lenders, and the currency a country borrows in. Only the country that prints the world's main reserve money escapes most of them.
- The governance that replaced gold took about twenty years to build. It then gave about 35 years of low inflation, with more frequent crises than the gold-linked years before.
- Rich countries grew more slowly after 1971. Late developers such as Korea, Malaysia, China and India grew faster. The numbers cannot say the money system caused either.

### The claim on trial

[4 p] A reader of this site wrote in with a sharper version of the argument from chapter 02. Here it is in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. Before 1971 money was tied to gold, and gold is scarce. So all credit before 1971 rested on something limited.
- Part 2. After 1971 the system is tied to no hard asset. So unlimited borrowing is at last available.
- Part 3. The new system runs under stable, controlled governance.
- Part 4. Its purpose is to let productivity and technology advance, and that is what it has done.

[6 p] Each part can be tested. But first, the weekend itself.


### The weekend

[8 p] On Friday 13 August 1971 President Richard Nixon flew to Camp David, a retreat in the hills north of Washington. With him were fifteen advisers, among them Arthur Burns, the head of the central bank, John Connally, the Treasury Secretary, and a Treasury official named Paul Volcker. They had a problem that could not wait.

[9 p] Since 1944 America had promised foreign governments one ounce of gold for every 35 dollars they handed in. The promise worked while America held more gold than foreigners held dollars. By 1971 that was no longer true.

[10 table]
| Year | America's gold | Dollar claims held by foreign governments | Could the promise be kept? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1960 | $17.8 billion | $10.5 billion | Yes. More gold than claims |
| 1968 | $10.9 billion | $12.1 billion | Not in full |
| 1971 | $10.2 billion | $30.8 billion in June | No. Claims were about 2.9 times the gold |

[11 p] If every foreign government had asked for its gold, America could have paid about one in three. Runs on the dollar had begun. On Sunday evening, 15 August, Nixon went on television. He closed the gold window, which meant foreign governments could no longer swap dollars for gold. He froze wages and prices for 90 days and put a 10% tax on imports.

[12 quote] "The American dollar must never again be a hostage in the hands of international speculators." (President Nixon, 15 August 1971)

[13 p] He said the step was to "suspend temporarily" the swapping of dollars for gold. It was never undone. A new set of fixed exchange rates was agreed in December 1971 and lasted little more than a year. By March 1973 the main currencies floated against each other, their prices set each day by buyers and sellers. The anchor was gone.


### Test 1. Did gold really limit credit before 1971?

[15 p] The reader says credit before 1971 rested on something limited. There is real evidence for that, and real evidence against.

[16 table]
| Evidence that gold did limit money | Evidence that the limit was loose |
|---|---|
| Until March 1968 America's central bank had to hold gold worth 25% of the banknotes it issued. In February 1968 its chairman told Congress that of 12 billion dollars of gold, only 1.3 billion was still free | When the rule began to pinch, Congress removed it: for bank deposits in 1965, and for banknotes in 1968. A limit that is lifted each time it binds is a loose limit |
| America's gold could not cover its promises to foreign governments, as the table above shows. That is what forced the weekend at Camp David | The promise was to foreign governments only. No rule tied a home loan in Ohio to an ounce of gold |
| Bank lending in 18 rich countries was 53% of yearly output in 1970, lower than the 58% of 1913. By 2007 it was 111% | Lending grew fastest while the gold link was still there: 9.0% a year after inflation from 1950 to 1970, against 5.8% from 1971 to 1990 and 3.3% from 1991 to 2019 |
| World debt of all kinds was about 97% of world output in the 1950s. In 2024 it was about 235% | Even under the old gold standard, gold coin was only about 5% to 9% of the money in Britain in 1914, by one estimate. The rest was bank money |

[17 p] Put the two columns together. Gold was a real limit at the top of the system, on what America could promise other governments. It was a weak limit on lending inside each country, and it was loosened whenever it pulled tight. Credit grew fast under the gold link because it started from a low level after the war. But its level against income climbed far higher once the link was gone.

[18 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds in part. Money before 1971 did rest on something limited. But the limit was a rope, not a wall. It held back governments more than it held back banks, and it was let out whenever it pulled.


### Test 2. Is borrowing unlimited now?

[20 p] The reader says that after 1971 unlimited borrowing is at last available. The record says something different. Borrowing became elastic. It can stretch a long way. But three limits took the place of gold, and each has stopped a government in its tracks.

[21 p] The first limit is inflation. Through the 1970s American prices rose faster and faster, and in 1980 inflation passed 14%. The central bank, now led by Paul Volcker, the same man who had been at Camp David, pushed interest rates to nearly 20% in 1981. That broke the inflation, and it broke a great deal else. Unemployment reached 10.8% at the end of 1982. Among house builders it was 22%, and among car makers 24%. Builders are reported to have mailed bricks and blocks of wood to the central bank, and car dealers the keys of cars they could not sell.

[22 p] The second limit is the lender. In September 2022 Britain's government announced large tax cuts paid for by borrowing. Within days the interest rate on its 30-year bonds jumped by 1.6 percentage points, and the Bank of England had to step in to calm the market. The plan was reversed within about a month, and the prime minister resigned.

[23 p] The third limit is the currency. A country that borrows in its own money can always print to repay, at the cost of inflation. A country that borrows in someone else's money cannot. Mexico told its lenders in August 1982 that it could not service about 80 billion dollars of debt. In 1997 the same trap closed on Asia.

[24 table]
| Asia, 1997 to 1998 | What happened |
|---|---|
| The weakness | Banks and firms had borrowed dollars for short periods, without protection against a fall in their own currency (IMF) |
| Malaysia | The ringgit fell from about 2.50 to the dollar in mid 1997 to about 4.88 in January 1998. On 1 September 1998 Bank Negara put controls on money leaving the country and fixed the ringgit at 3.80 to the dollar. The peg lasted until 2005 |
| South Korea | In January 1998 people queued to sell their gold rings and necklaces so the gold could be exported for dollars. In the first three days they brought in 17 tonnes |
| The rescues | The International Monetary Fund lent Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea tens of billions of dollars, with hard conditions |

[25 quote] "I did not realize how much gold I have in my closet until this campaign." (Nam Kwang-woo, 24, in the queue in Seoul (Reuters, 7 January 1998))

[26 p] Malaysia learned from that year. At the end of 2022, 97.3% of its federal government debt was in ringgit, and a law caps that debt at 65% of yearly output. It stood at 60.4%.

[27 p] So who can borrow almost without limit? The country whose money everyone else saves in. In mid 2026 the US dollar was 56.7% of the world's official currency reserves. Other countries need dollars, so America can sell its debt more easily than anyone. Even America met the first limit, in 1980.

[28 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 does not hold as stated. Borrowing after 1971 is elastic, not unlimited. Gold was replaced by three other limits: inflation, lenders and the currency of the debt. They bind a small country much harder than they bind America.

[29 check] Q: A friend says a government that prints its own money can never run out, so its borrowing has no limit. What three limits would you name?
A: Inflation: print too much and prices rise until the central bank must raise rates, as America did in 1981. Lenders: they can demand higher interest, as Britain found in 2022. And the currency: a country that borrowed in dollars cannot print dollars, as Asia found in 1997.


### Test 3. Is the governance stable and controlled?

[31 p] The reader says the new system runs under stable, controlled governance. Something did replace gold as the anchor. It is a promise by each central bank to keep inflation near a target, usually 2%. But that promise was not ready in 1971. It took about twenty years to build.

[32 table]
| When | What happened | Inflation in America |
|---|---|---|
| 1971 to 1982 | No anchor. In October 1979 the central bank began to target the supply of money | 7.8% a year on average |
| 1990 | New Zealand became the first country to give its central bank an inflation target. By 2010, 26 countries had one |  |
| 1983 to 2019 | The long calm. America announced its own 2% target in January 2012 | 2.7% a year on average |
| 2008 | The financial crisis. American output fell 4.3% and unemployment reached 10%. Congress approved 700 billion dollars to rescue banks |  |
| 2022 | The target was badly missed | 9.1% in the year to June, as reported. The euro area is reported to have reached 10.6% |

[33 p] So the new anchor worked for about 35 years, after a decade with no anchor at all. Now look at crises. One well-known count measures how often a country was in a banking or currency crisis.

[34 table]
| Era | The money rule | Share of country-years in crisis |
|---|---|---|
| 1880 to 1913 | Gold standard | 6.4% |
| 1945 to 1971 | Dollar tied to gold | 7.9% |
| 1973 to 1997 | Floating paper money | 10.7% |

[35 p] Most of the rise came from currency crises. And in 18 rich countries, no country-wide banking crisis is recorded at all between 1945 and 1971. Those were also years of tight rules on banks and on money crossing borders, so gold may not deserve the credit.

[36 p] Both sides have a strong voice here. Friedrich Hayek, a hard-money economist, wrote that "history is largely a history of inflation, and usually of inflations engineered by governments". Ben Bernanke, later head of America's central bank, pointed in 2004 to "a substantial decline in macroeconomic volatility" and gave better policy part of the credit. The historian Barry Eichengreen called the gold standard "a central factor in the worldwide Depression".

[37 p] And who controls it? Not voters, directly. America's central bank governors are named by the President and confirmed by the Senate, for terms of 14 years. When Swiss voters were asked in 2014 to tie their central bank to gold, about 77% are reported to have said no.

[38 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds in part. There is governance, and for 35 years it kept inflation low. But it arrived late, it has failed badly at least three times, and crises became more frequent, not less. It is controlled by appointed officials, not by the people who pay the toll.


### Test 4. Did it let productivity and technology advance?

[40 p] This is the reader's largest claim: that a money system free of gold exists so that progress can be paid for before it pays back. Start with growth in income per person, after inflation.

[41 table]
| Growth per person, a year | 1950 to 1973 | 1973 to 1990 | 1990 to 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | 2.5% | 2.0% | 1.5% |
| 19 rich countries, average | 3.7% | 2.2% | 1.5% |
| South Korea | 6.0% | 7.9% | 3.6% |
| Malaysia | 2.2% | 4.2% | 4.1% |
| China | 2.8% | 4.1% | 6.2% |
| India | 1.4% | 2.6% | 4.4% |

[42 p] The table tells two stories. The rich countries grew fastest while the gold link was in place, and more slowly in each period after. The late developers did the opposite. Korea, Malaysia, China and India all grew faster after 1973 than before.

[43 p] Neither story proves a cause. The rich countries were catching up after a war until about 1973, and that could not last. The late developers changed many things at once: schools, factories, trade rules. World trade rose from 25.8% of world output in 1970 to 56.7% in 2024. A world of floating money and freer movement of capital may have helped them. The numbers here cannot show it.

[44 p] What about technology? America's spending on research was 2.79% of its output in 1964, when the gold link still held and the country was racing to the moon. It fell to 2.07% by 1978 and rose to 3.43% by 2022. So research was high before 1971, dipped in the decade after, and is higher now.

[45 p] For a Malaysian reader the picture is mixed in a familiar way. Prices rose 0.9% a year in the 1960s, 6.3% a year from 1971 to 1982, and 2.6% a year from 1983 to 2019. Growth per person roughly doubled its pace. The toll went up, and so did the income.

[46 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 holds in part. For late developers, Malaysia among them, the years after 1971 were the best on record. For the rich countries they were slower than the years before. The evidence does not show that cutting the gold link caused either. Research spending was high under gold too.

[49 p] Here is the claim again, rebuilt so the evidence can carry it.

[50 steps]
- Before 1971 gold tied the hands of governments more than of banks. The tie was loosened each time it pulled, until one weekend it was cut.
- After 1971 money became elastic. It can stretch to meet a war, a crisis or a new industry. It is not unlimited. Inflation, lenders and foreign-currency debt now do the work gold once did, and they do it more roughly.
- A new anchor, the inflation target, took about twenty years to build. It has held most of the time and failed badly some of the time. It is run by appointed officials.
- The years since have been the best on record for late developers and slower for the rich world. Whether elastic money caused either is not shown.

[51 p] One number speaks for the hard-money side. An ounce of gold cost 35 dollars on that weekend. In November 2026 it cost about 3,900 dollars, by one price chart. Measured in gold, the dollar has lost about 99% of its value since the anchor was cut. One number speaks for the other side. In those same years income per person in Malaysia, after inflation, grew faster than it ever had before.

[52 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that the slowdown in rich countries after 1973 was caused by the end of the gold link, and not by the end of post-war catching up. Second, that late developers could have grown as fast under a gold-linked dollar. Third, that the three new limits bind as tightly as gold did, in which case elastic money is not very elastic at all.

[53 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): How governments fought to save the gold price before 1971
[53.0 p] From November 1961 America and seven European central banks ran the London Gold Pool. They sold their own gold on the open market to hold the price at 35 dollars. From December 1967 to March 1968 the pool lost about 3 billion dollars of gold, and America's share was 2.2 billion. In March 1968 the pool was closed and a two-tier market began: one price for central banks, a free price for everyone else. It lasted until the weekend at Camp David.

[53.1 p] Some economists argue the system could have been saved. Michael Bordo and Robert McCauley point out that America's gold position in the 1960s was no worse than Britain's around 1900, when the Bank of England held gold worth under 5% of what it owed. On this view the end came from policy choices, not from a shortage of metal.
