Short quotes from Nixon's address of 15 August 1971 (American Presidency Project, verified):
- "suspend temporarily the convertibility of the dollar into gold or other reserve assets"
- "I am today ordering a freeze on all prices and wages throughout the United States for a period of 90 days."
- "I am today imposing an additional tax of 10 percent on goods imported into the United States."
- "the American dollar must never again be a hostage in the hands of international speculators"
- "The strength of a nation's currency is based on the strength of that nation's economy"

Note for narration: the word "temporarily" matters. The suspension was framed as temporary and was never reversed.

Reading for H3: the replacement for gold came late and piecemeal. From 1971 to roughly 1990 there was no agreed anchor. The rule-based anchor (an inflation target set in public, run by an independent central bank) took about 20 years to arrive in New Zealand and 41 years to become explicit in the United States (1971 to 2012). So "controlled governance" is a later achievement, not something 1971 itself delivered.

## Evidence for and against H2 and H3

H2 (after 1971, unlimited borrowing is available?)

For:
- No metal limit remains. The US suspended gold convertibility in 1971 and never restored it (R14B-03, R14B-10).
- The issuer of the main reserve currency borrows in its own money, and foreigners still hold 56.7% of reserves in dollars (R14B-29).
- Central banks can create money on a scale gold never allowed (Fed losses of $114.3bn in 2023 with no effect on its ability to pay, R14B-37). The US debt limit has been raised or suspended many times (R14B-26).
- Malaysia now borrows 97.3% in ringgit, so even a mid-sized economy has escaped much of 1997's original sin at the federal level (R14B-25).

Against:
- Inflation is a limit. US inflation passed 14% in 1980 and was stopped only by rates near 20% and unemployment of 10.8% (R14B-11 to R14B-14).
- Lenders are a limit. The UK changed course within about three weeks in 2022 (R14B-15 to R14B-17) and borrowed from the IMF in 1976 (R14B-18).
- Foreign-currency debt is a hard limit for most countries: Mexico 1982, Asia 1997 to 1998, Argentina 2001 (R14B-19 to R14B-24).
- Written rules remain: Basel capital ratios, the EU's 3% and 60%, Malaysia's 65% statutory ceiling (R14B-25 to R14B-28).
- The privilege is shrinking: the dollar's reserve share fell from about 71% (2001) to 56.7% (2026Q2) (R14B-28, R14B-29).
- Verdict to test in the chapter: borrowing became elastic, not unlimited. The limit moved from a metal to inflation, markets and law. It binds the United States least and Malaysia more.

H3 (the new system runs under stable, controlled governance?)

For:
- An anchor was rebuilt: money targets (1979), inflation targeting from New Zealand (1990) to 26 countries by 2010 and about 40 by 2017, the Fed 2% target (2012) (R14B-12, R14B-31 to R14B-33).
- Independence was set in law: Bank of England 1997 to 1998, Bank Negara's Act of 2009 (R14B-34, R14B-35).
- The Great Moderation: a "substantial decline in macroeconomic volatility" credited partly to policy (R14B-41). Eichengreen's evidence that gold itself was destabilising in the 1930s (R14B-42).
- Where citizens voted (Switzerland 2014 and 2018), large majorities kept the existing system (R14B-45, R14B-46).

Against:
- No anchor for about two decades after 1971, with the Great Inflation (R14B-11, R14B-12).
- Serious breakdowns: Latin America 1982, Asia 1997, 2008 (4.3% output fall, $700bn TARP), and inflation of 9.1% (US) and 10.6% (euro area) in 2022 against 2% targets (R14B-19, R14B-36, R14B-38, R14B-39).
- Central banks took large losses (Fed deferred asset $133bn end 2023, SNB loss about 17% of GDP in 2022) (R14B-37, R14B-40).
- The hard-money critics: Hayek on a history of government-made inflation, Shelton on savers (R14B-43, R14B-44).
- Control is by appointed officials. Citizens almost never vote on the money system directly (R14B-47, R14B-48).
- Verdict to test in the chapter: "controlled" holds. "Stable" holds for some decades (about 1985 to 2007, and the 2010s) but not for the whole period. Governance learned by failing, and each failure fell hardest on borrowers in foreign currency.
