## 1. Answers

### WQ1. What if China opens its capital account fully (full convertibility of the yuan)?

**Answer.** China is very unlikely to do it, and if it did, the first effect would probably help the dollar, not hurt it. Today China runs a deliberately half-open system: citizens may buy only $50,000 of foreign currency a year, foreigners come in through licensed channels (QFII, Stock Connect, Bond Connect), residents go out only through rationed ones (QDII quotas, Southbound Bond Connect at 800 billion yuan a year), and the PBoC sets a daily fixing. That is the "impossible trinity" choice: Beijing keeps control of its own interest rates and a managed yuan, so it cannot also let money move freely. The one real test, 2015 to 2016, went badly: after a small devaluation on 11 August 2015, reserves fell $512.66B in 2015 alone and from about $3.99T to under $3T by January 2017, and controls were tightened again. Full opening today would point money the same way: Chinese households hold about $25T of deposits earning little, while US 10-year Treasuries pay 5.13%. If 5% to 10% of household deposits went abroad, that is $1.25T to $2.5T, or 37% to 74% of China's $3.40T reserves. So the likely sequence is outflows and a weaker yuan first, Chinese private money buying dollar assets (Treasuries included) while the PBoC sells some reserves, and only later, if the rules and courts are trusted, steady foreign inflows and a reserve share for the yuan in yen or pound territory (4% to 6%, perhaps 10% at best by the 2040s) against the dollar's 56.7%. The official plan for 2026 to 2030 says "high-quality" and "autonomous" capital account opening, which in practice means more quotas, not freedom. Odds: full convertibility by 2030 is 3 in 100, by 2035 8, by 2050 25. A large partial step (the $50,000 quota lifted or abolished, or about 80% of capital items convertible) is 25 by 2030 and 40 by 2035. It changes today's scenario odds by less than half a point, because it is unlikely and far away. If it did happen, the desk would move about 4 points from S1 to S2 in H1 and 5 points in H2.

- **Status:** Plausible (the "no full opening" call rests on T1 policy text, T0 reserve and quota data and the 2015 to 2016 record. The effect sequence is a mechanism inference from T0 history).
- **Confidence:** 75.
- **Horizon:** H1 (2028 to 2035) for any large step. H2 for full convertibility.
- **Three views:**
  - Governance: For Beijing, full opening means giving up the cheap captive deposits that fund state banks, local governments and industrial policy, and accepting outside judgment of its currency, its courts and its data. The Party has chosen control every time the trade-off was sharp, so expect more quotas and pilots (Shanghai, Hainan, Hong Kong), not a big bang. For Washington, a sudden opening is not a threat at first: it would push Chinese savings into US assets.
  - Business: Treat China's opening as quota news, not regime change. The investable signals are the $50,000 quota, QDII and Southbound Connect quotas, the width of the daily band and whether the PBoC stops leaning on the fixing. A real opening would first mean a weaker yuan, a flood of Chinese buying in Hong Kong, Singapore and US assets, and later index weight increases for Chinese bonds.
  - Ordinary person: Nothing here changes the dollar in your pocket soon. If China did open, the first thing you might notice is Chinese money chasing US and Asian homes, stocks and bonds, and cheaper Chinese goods if the yuan fell. A yuan you could hold in a US bank account as easily as euros is a 2040s question at the earliest.

### WQ2. Is Kobyakov's claim one form of a real path: a one-time US revaluation that clears 50% to 80% of the debt and starts a new yardstick?

**Answer.** No. It is the least probable of the paths, and the most probable path is the quiet one Kobyakov partly described: slow erosion by inflation, with captive buyers (now including stablecoins) holding part of the debt at low yields. "Revaluation" can mean five things for a dollar debtor: marking up gold, devaluing the dollar against gold or other currencies, inflation above coupons, swapping into a new unit (Germany 1923, Brazil 1994) or an explicit haircut (Argentina 2005). The arithmetic rules out a one-time 50% to 80% clearance. To clear 50% of debt held by the public with the US gold stock, gold would have to be revalued to about $62,300 an ounce, and to about $99,700 for 80% (15 to 24 times today's $4,078). The gain would arrive as $16T to $26T of new central bank money, three to five times today's base money, which would either cause the inflation it was meant to avoid or cost the same interest as the debt it replaced. Halving the real debt by inflation alone needs about 15% a year for 5 years or 7% for 10 years, and that is only if no bill or note were ever rolled over at a higher rate. With bills at 22% of the stock, 71-month average maturity and a 2.9% primary deficit, even 10 years of 20% inflation only takes the debt ratio from 100% to about 84% unless the Fed also caps yields. And the people who would bear a devaluation are mostly Americans: foreigners hold only 28.6% of debt held by the public and 23% of gross debt, while Social Security benefits, tax brackets and $2.17T of TIPS are indexed to inflation. Kobyakov's stablecoin channel is real only in its small part: holders get zero interest by law and many are foreign, but the tokens hold 93-day bills that reprice every quarter, and they are 0.9% of the debt. The verdict by probability: one-time 50% to 80% clearance by revaluation by 2035: 1. Price-level erosion of 20% or more of today's debt stock by 2035 (needs average inflation of about 2.5%): 55. The strict version, 20% to 40% erased net of coupons (real yields of minus 2.4% to minus 5.4% a year for nine years, the 1946 to 1955 template): 10. A new US unit of account replacing the dollar by 2040: 2. A new international yardstick at accord level (a new reserve unit or a gold-linked settlement layer) by 2040: 12. The closest probable path is therefore gradual erosion plus financial repression, the S4-lite that DCC already maps.

- **Status:** Plausible (the arithmetic is T0 inputs plus a model, the history is T0 and peer-reviewed T4, the probabilities are judgment).
- **Confidence:** 75.
- **Horizon:** H1 for erosion and repression, H2 for any new yardstick.
- **Three views:**
  - Governance: The real choice is not "reset or no reset" but how visible the loss is and who carries it. A one-time revaluation is the most visible and least effective tool. Inflation above coupons plus captive buyers is the least visible and is already partly built (GENIUS bill rule, leverage relief). The binding constraints are the bond market, the indexation of spending and a Fed that is currently hiking.
  - Business: Price the slow path. It rewards owners of real assets and pricing power and fixed-rate borrowers, and it punishes long nominal creditors. A gold revaluation would be a headline, not a balance-sheet event, unless it came with yield caps.
  - Ordinary person: Nobody is going to wake up to a new dollar with half the debt gone. The realistic risk is a decade in which prices rise faster than the interest on your savings. Indexed income (Social Security, TIPS, I bonds) and owned real assets protect you. Cash and stablecoins do not, because they pay you nothing or little while prices rise.

### 3.1 What the controls are today (T0 and T1)

**The impossible trinity.** A country can have at most two of three: a fixed or managed exchange rate, free capital movement, and its own interest rate policy. The US and euro area pick free capital plus their own rates and let the currency float. Hong Kong picks a peg plus free capital and imports US rates. China picks a managed yuan plus its own rates (and a state-directed credit system) and gives up free capital movement. Full convertibility forces China to float or to give up control of rates and credit. That is the real price, not a technicality.

### 3.3 What full opening would require
1. **A floating yuan**: no daily fixing guidance, no countercyclical factor, and tolerance for a 10% to 20% move in a year.
2. **Giving up some control of rates and credit**: state banks could no longer rely on low deposit rates (household deposits RMB 167T) because savers could leave. Local government and property debt would be refinanced at market prices.
3. **Deep, open bond markets with hedging**: foreign holdings are only about RMB 3.2T (about $480B), roughly 2% of the market by an older BNP estimate (WIF-09, WIF-10). A reserve currency needs foreigners to hold far more and to exit freely.
4. **Rule of law and predictable policy**: independent courts for foreign creditors, no sudden exit bans, data access. This is the hardest condition and the one investors weight most (Prasad).
5. **Willingness to run deficits or two-way flows**: as Pettis puts it, the world can accumulate yuan only if China runs a current account deficit or if inflows are matched by Chinese outflows. A $938B nine-month surplus means the second route is the only one, and that route is exactly the open capital account (WIF-11).

**The counter-case.** If China opened into strength (record surplus, yuan at a four-year high, global investors underweight China), the first wave could be inflows. That is why Beijing releases outflow quotas when the yuan is strong (QDII in July 2026). It is also why any real opening is more likely in a strong-yuan year than in a crisis.

### 3.5 Who wins and who loses (if opened)
- **Win:** Chinese households and private firms (diversification, higher returns), global asset managers and Hong Kong and Singapore wealth businesses, US asset markets in the outflow phase, foreign holders of Chinese bonds in the long run (hedging, liquidity), exporters during the initial yuan fall.
- **Lose:** state banks and local government financing vehicles that live on captive deposits, the PBoC's control of the exchange rate and credit, Hong Kong's privileged role as the only gate (CNH loses its niche), exporters later if the yuan rises once it is trusted, and in H2 the dollar's reserve share.
- **US position:** a short-run gain (capital inflow, stronger dollar, buyers for Treasuries) and a long-run loss of share. Washington's 2026 posture (welcoming a strong yuan, R1 L5-19) is consistent with wanting more flexibility, not full opening.

## 4. WQ2 in depth: can the US clear its debt by revaluation?

### 4.1 What "revaluation" can mean for a dollar debtor

| Form | Mechanism | Who pays | Can it clear 50% to 80%? | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Gold revaluation | Raise the statutory price from $42.2222, issue gold certificates to the Fed, credit the Treasury (DCD 6.1) | Holders of dollars, through the new base money | Only at $62,300 to $99,700 per ounce, and the proceeds are new money (see 4.2) | One day, then inflation follows |
| B. Devaluation against gold or other currencies | Officially or by policy push the dollar down (1933 to 1934, 1971 to 1973, a "Mar-a-Lago accord") | Foreign holders in their own currency, Americans through import prices | No. Debt is in dollars, so a cheaper dollar does not cut it unless domestic prices rise | Months |
| C. Inflation above coupons (financial repression) | Prices and nominal GDP grow faster than the interest paid, helped by caps and captive buyers | Holders of nominal debt and cash, mostly American | 20% to 40% over a decade is possible only with yield caps. 50% needs years of high inflation | Years |
| D. New unit swap | Replace the currency at a fixed rate (Rentenmark 1923 at 1 for 1 trillion paper marks, Brazil URV to real at 2,750 cruzeiros reais in 1994) | Whoever held the old unit, but the loss happened earlier through hyperinflation | The swap does not clear debt. It ends an inflation that already cleared it | Months, after the damage |
| E. Restructuring | Haircut or forced term-out (Argentina 2005: about 75% to 77% NPV loss, Greece 2012: 53.5% face) | Bondholders directly | Yes, arithmetically. Politically and legally it is a default on the world's collateral | Weeks, then decades of litigation |

### 4.2 The arithmetic

**Gold (A).** Gain = (P minus $42.22) x 261.5M oz.

| Target | Size | Gold price needed | Times today ($4,078) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% of debt held by the public | $16.29T | $62,318 | 15.3 |
| 80% of debt held by the public | $26.06T | $99,683 | 24.4 |
| 50% of gross debt | $20.22T | $77,366 | 19.0 |
| 80% of gross debt | $32.35T | $123,760 | 30.3 |

The proceeds would be $16T to $26T of new reserves, 3.0 to 4.7 times today's $5.51T of base money. If the Fed pays interest on reserves near the bill rate, swapping Treasuries for reserves saves almost nothing (DCD-25). If it stops paying, banks bear a large tax (repression). Either way the "one-time" revaluation becomes form C.

**Inflation (C), pure stock version.** If no debt ever repriced and there were no new deficits, cutting the real debt by 50% needs the price level to double: 14.9% a year for 5 years or 7.2% for 10. Cutting it by 80% needs a fivefold price level: 38.0% a year for 5 years or 17.5% for 10.

**Inflation (C), realistic version (model).** Debt ratio starts at 100% of GDP, real growth 2%, primary deficit 2.9% of GDP, average coupon 3.44%, 30% of the stock reprices each year at inflation plus a real rate.

| Debt ratio after 10 years | 2.5% inflation | 4% | 6% | 8% | 10% | 15% | 20% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New debt at real rate +1.5% (market) | 122 | 119 | 114 | 110 | 107 | 99 | 92 |
| New debt at real rate 0% | 110 | 108 | 104 | 101 | 97 | 90 | 84 |
| New debt at real rate -2% (yield caps) | 97 | 94 | 91 | 89 | 86 | 80 | 76 |
| Real rate 0% and primary balance zero | 84 | 81 | 78 | 74 | 72 | 65 | 59 |

Over 5 years the same rows end at 111 to 85 (market rate), 106 to 82 (0% real), 100 to 78 (-2% real). Reading: inflation alone barely moves the ratio, because the debt is short and the deficit keeps adding. Even 20% inflation for 10 years with a balanced primary budget cuts the ratio by only about 41%. A 50% to 80% cut through inflation needs Weimar or Argentina-scale inflation, which would destroy the bond market the US depends on.

**Real erosion of today's stock (the soft measure).** By end 2035 (9.2 years) the price level erases 19% of today's debt's purchasing power at 2.37% inflation (the 10y breakeven), 24% at 3%, 30% at 4%, 36% at 5%. Erasing 20% needs average inflation of about 2.5%, erasing 40% needs about 5.7%. "Net of coupons" (holders actually losing 20% to 40% after interest) needs average real yields of minus 2.4% to minus 5.4% for nine years.

**Who bears it.** Of $32.57T held by the public: foreign 28.6% ($9.33T, CRS put it at 31% at end 2025), Fed 14.0%, domestic private about 57%. Of gross debt, foreigners hold 23% and federal trust funds (mostly Social Security) 19.5%. A 50% real devaluation would cost foreigners about $4.7T and Americans about $11.6T (outside the Fed) plus the trust funds. The Social Security trust fund would lose real assets while benefits rise with the COLA, so it would run dry sooner.

### 4.4 The stablecoin channel Kobyakov describes

His words (2025-09-06, Vladivostok): "Over time, when part of the US national debt is placed in stablecoins, the country's authorities will devalue this debt" (L5-24). He gave no mechanism (Cointelegraph). Decoded, there are four possible channels:
1. **Zero yield to holders.** True by law (GENIUS bans issuer yield). But the Treasury still pays the market bill rate, to the issuer. The Treasury saves nothing directly. The holder pays, and the issuer keeps the spread (DCD 3.5).
2. **Captive bill bid.** True but small: stablecoins hold about 4.27% of bills and are about 0.9% of debt held by the public. It lowers bill yields at the margin.
3. **Inflating it away.** Stablecoins hold bills of 93 days or less, the debt that reprices fastest. Inflation erodes long fixed coupons, not 3-month bills, unless the Fed also holds bill rates below inflation, which would hit every bill holder, not stablecoin holders in particular.
4. **Export of the loss.** Partly true: much of the demand is foreign (R3 R3-1), so if real rates go negative, part of the inflation tax falls on foreigners holding zero-yield tokens. At $2T of supply and a 2% inflation gap, that is about $40B a year, about 0.1% of GDP.

Verdict: the claim is half-right on capture and wrong on "devalue this debt". Stablecoins are a captive-demand tool (D1 elsewhere on this desk) and a way to make holders, many foreign, carry inflation. They are not a channel that can clear 50% to 80% of anything (D3 as a plan, 25).

### 4.5 Constraints
- **Debt held mostly by Americans** (about 71% of debt held by the public, 77% of gross). A deliberate devaluation is a tax on US pensions, banks, money funds and households.
- **Indexation.** Social Security COLAs, Medicare costs, tax brackets and $2.17T of TIPS rise with prices, so inflation raises spending as well as nominal GDP. Japan and the US in the 1940s had far less indexation.
- **Short debt.** Bills are 22.4% of the stock, FRNs another $0.71T, average maturity 71 months. Surprise inflation is passed into new coupons within a few years.
- **The bond market.** The 10y is 5.13% with a 2.76% real yield. A visible plan to inflate would raise yields immediately, as the 2022 UK gilt episode showed (DCC).
- **The Fed.** Warsh hiked to 3.75% to 4.00% and the Supreme Court kept Governor Cook in place (DCA). A yield cap needs a captured or frightened Fed. DCC puts that at 25 by 2035.
- **Law.** A gold revaluation needs Congress (31 U.S.C. 5117). A restructuring would break the collateral role of Treasuries worldwide, including inside GENIUS stablecoins.

| Path | Probability | Note |

**Most probable path:** gradual erosion plus repression lite. Inflation a little above the old 2% target, a Fed pushed toward capping long yields in a stress episode, and captive buyers (stablecoins, banks with leverage relief, money funds) holding a growing share of short debt. This erases about a fifth of today's debt's purchasing power by 2035 but does not shrink the debt ratio unless the deficit also falls. Kobyakov's sentence describes a piece of this path (captive holders bearing inflation) and dresses it as a one-time devaluation that the arithmetic does not allow.

## 5. Key findings, Say/Do gaps, Mechanism chains, Open questions

### 5.1 Key findings
1. China's opening is asymmetric by design: inflows are welcome, outflows are rationed. The $50,000 quota is unchanged and QDII stalled in Q2 2026 (WIF-06, WIF-07).
2. The 2015 to 2016 episode cost about $1T of reserves under a partial opening. A full opening today faces a larger pool ($25T of household deposits) and a wider rate gap (WIF-05, WIF-12).
3. A Chinese opening helps the dollar first and hurts its reserve share only over one to two decades (WIF-D03, WIF-D04).
4. Full convertibility: 3 by 2030, 8 by 2035, 25 by 2050. No change to S1 to S4 today.
5. No form of revaluation clears 50% to 80% of US debt at once without becoming money printing or default. Gold would need $62,300 to $99,700 (WIF-14).
6. Inflation alone barely cuts the US debt ratio because the debt is short and deficits continue. Even 20% for 10 years leaves the ratio at about 84% without yield caps (WIF-13).
7. The best US precedent, 1946 to 1974, cut about 80 points over 28 years with about 40 from rate distortions and about 30 from primary surpluses (WIF-15).
8. Foreigners hold under a third of the debt, so any devaluation is mostly a tax on Americans (WIF-21).
9. Kobyakov's stablecoin channel: half-right on capture, wrong on devaluation. It works on the shortest debt and on 0.9% of the stock.
10. The most probable path is slow erosion with repression lite (55 for at least 20% price-level erosion by 2035), not a reset (1).

### 5.3 Mechanism chains
**Chain WIF-A (China opens, H1, conditional).** SAFE lifts the quota and the PBoC floats → households move 5% to 10% of $25T abroad → yuan falls 10% or more → PBoC sells reserves, some Treasuries → private Chinese buyers take dollar assets, dollar up → Beijing either re-closes (2017 replay, likeliest) or accepts the fall → if it holds, index inclusion raises foreign holdings of Chinese bonds → yuan reserve share 4% to 6% by the 2040s. Test: SAFE quota text, monthly reserves, COFER CNY share.

**Chain WIF-B (gold revaluation becomes inflation, Speculative).** Crisis → Congress marks gold to $62,300 → $16T TGA credit → spent or used to retire debt → reserves triple → either IOR is paid (no saving) or it is not (bank tax and inflation) → dollar falls, gold market price rises toward the new mark → the debt cut turns out to be a transfer from money holders. Test: any bill touching 31 U.S.C. 5117.

**Chain WIF-C (slow erosion, Plausible).** Oil shock and deficits → inflation above 2.5% → Fed under pressure, a market-functioning episode (30 by 2028) → long-end buying or yield language → real yields fall below zero while captive holders (stablecoins, banks, money funds) take short bills → debt's real value erodes 20% or more by 2035 while the nominal debt keeps rising. Test: 10y breakeven above 2.75%, Fed purchase descriptions, stablecoins above 10% of bills, the TIPS share of issuance (a cut while inflation rises would be a tell of intent).
