## Episode notes

### Episode 1. Money vs Currency (published 2013-02-26)

Summary. Maloney opens in Egypt and draws a line between "currency" (a medium of exchange and unit of account) and "money" (all of that plus a store of value over long periods). He argues that gold and silver are the best money because they are durable, divisible, fungible, portable and limited in supply, while paper currencies can be expanded without limit and so leak purchasing power. He adopts a monetarist definition of inflation as growth in the currency supply, with rising prices as the symptom. He shows charts of US base money jumping after the 2008 bailouts and QE, and says the same thing is happening in Canada, Australia, Russia, India and China. He says the US "exported" its inflation because foreigners hold many dollars, and warns that those dollars could flow back. He links QE2 to a rise in world food prices and the Arab Spring. He ends with the claim that gold and silver always "do an accounting" of currency expansion by rising until their value meets or exceeds all the currency in circulation.

Quote: "Inflation is an expansion of the currency supply."

Main claims:
- Money and currency are different, and only gold and silver have held purchasing power for 5,000 years.
- Every fiat currency in history has gone to zero (citing a Daily Reckoning tally of about 600 currencies in the letters A and B).
- The dollar has lost about 95% of its purchasing power since the Fed was created in 1913.
- The Federal Reserve is a private corporation, not really part of the government.
- QE is currency created out of thin air and handed to banks.
- Inflation is the expansion of the currency supply, rising prices are the symptom.
- Gold will rise until it accounts for all currency created.

### Episode 2. USA's Seven Stages of Empire (published 2013-08-13)

Summary. The episode tells the story of Athens: coinage became true money in Lydia around 680 to 630 BC, Athens prospered with free markets and sound coins, then debased its coinage with copper to pay for the Peloponnesian War and public works. Maloney presents this as the first example of a cycle he calls the Seven Stages of Empire: sound money, public works, a big military, war, debasement, loss of faith, and a rush into gold. He then compresses 140 years of monetary systems into a London talk: the classical gold standard from 1873, suspension in World War I, the gold exchange standard with a 40% gold cover under the 1913 Federal Reserve Act, Bretton Woods in 1944 at $35 an ounce, de Gaulle's gold demands in the 1960s, and Nixon closing the gold window on 1971-08-15. He notes Gresham's law and the 1965 switch from silver to clad coins. He claims a new world monetary system arrives every 30 to 40 years and predicts a deflationary crash, then attempts to print out of it, then hyperinflation and a return to gold "in this decade" (the 2010s). He says the US is in stage six and entering stage seven.

Quote: "It always ends with gold delivering a knockout blow to debased currencies."

Main claims:
- Debasement for war and public works is a repeating seven-stage cycle.
- The 1913 Act allowed $50 of notes against $20 of gold (a 40% reserve).
- The US lost about 50% of its gold from 1959 to 1971, with about 12 times more dollars than gold by 1971.
- A new monetary system comes roughly every 40 years, and the next one would arrive in the 2010s.

### Episode 3. The Death of the U.S. Dollar Standard (published 2013-09-24)

Summary. Filmed around a talk in Singapore, this episode argues that the dollar's reserve role is ending. Maloney lists "nails in the coffin": countries trading outside the dollar, Iran's exclusion from SWIFT, gold repatriation by Germany and Venezuela, Utah making gold and silver legal tender, China's gold accumulation, and the Fed's QE3 at $85 billion a month. Jim Rickards lays out four possible next systems (multiple reserve currencies, the SDR, gold, or chaos). Maloney says gold standards fail because governments print more claims than gold, but gold itself does not, and that gold could be used electronically. He shows gold "accounting" for base money in 1934 and 1980, adds credit card balances to the currency supply because a card charge creates new currency, and projects gold at $13,400 to $47,000 an ounce. He expects this within "a couple of years".

Quote: "When you charge something on a credit card, you create currency."

Main claims:
- The dollar standard is ending fast and a chaotic new system is inevitable.
- Credit card borrowing creates currency, and the public adds to the money supply by borrowing.
- Gold has twice risen to cover the US currency base (1934 and 1980) and will do so again, implying $13,400 to $47,000 an ounce.

### Episode 4. The IOU Machine: How Currency Is Created (published 2013-10-15)

Summary. This is the series' core lesson on money creation and its most viewed video. Politicians promise more than taxes cover, so the Treasury sells bonds (IOUs), big banks buy them at auction, and through open market operations the banks sell some to the Fed, which pays with a check drawn on nothing. Maloney quotes the Boston Fed saying the Fed creates money when it writes a check, and describes base money as a pile of bonds at the Fed matched by currency at the Treasury. The government spends, the recipients deposit their pay, and fractional reserve lending with a 10% ratio turns $100 into up to $1,000 of bank credit, which he says is where 92 to 96% of money comes from. Taxes then pay interest on bonds bought by the Fed, the debt can never be repaid because interest is owed on every dollar, repayment destroys money, and so debt must grow forever. He adds that the Fed has stockholders who receive a 6% dividend, guesses they are the primary dealers, ties the income tax of 1913 to the Fed's founding, and calls the whole system "the biggest scam in the history of mankind".

Quote: "The Federal Reserve and the Treasury are just swapping I.O.U.s, using the banks as middlemen."

Main claims:
- Deficits are financed by bonds that banks buy at auction and partly resell to the Fed, which pays with newly created money.
- Banks multiply each deposit through fractional reserves (10%, 3% or 0% ratios), and 92 to 96% of money is bank credit.
- Taxes largely go to interest on bonds the Fed bought with nothing.
- Because interest is owed on every dollar, there is never enough money to repay debt, so debt must keep growing.
- Repaying debt destroys money.
- The Fed is privately owned, probably by the primary dealers, who get a 6% dividend and interest on reserves.
- The income tax exists because of the Fed.

### Episode 5. When Money Is Corrupted (published 2013-11-26)

Summary. Maloney tours the Bundesbank Money Museum in Frankfurt, from barter and commodity currencies to the first Lydian coins and on to paper. He criticises the museum's wording, saying central bankers confuse currency with money. He covers Ming paper money, the Continental dollar and the Weimar hyperinflation, quoting a museum panel that money lost half its value or more per day in 1923. He argues that hyperinflation wipes out the middle class and opens the door to dictators, using Hitler's 1923 putsch and later rise as the example, and that crises push societies from capitalism toward collectivism, using divided Berlin. He shows an ECB display that teaches the 10% reserve multiplier and treats it as proof of Episode 4. He predicts a short deflation followed by big inflation or hyperinflation.

Quote: "Default on their debt or inflate it away."

Main claims:
- Indebted nations end up choosing default or inflation, and Germany chose inflation in 1923.
- Hyperinflation destroys the middle class and helps dictators rise.
- Central banks teach the money multiplier themselves (the ECB museum panel).
- A short deflation will come first, then big inflation or hyperinflation.

### Episode 6. The Convergence of Cycles (published 2015-11-17)

Summary. Maloney explains why he expects deflation before inflation. He shows base money rising after 2008 while most of it sits as excess reserves at the Fed, which pays interest to keep banks liquid after the 2008 interbank freeze. He argues that the response to 2008 built a new credit and asset bubble (stocks, collector cars, art, housing affordability) and that all bubbles pop. With Harry Dent he presents demographic arguments (the baby boom passing its peak spending years), the wealth-distribution cycle, stock valuation cycles, the Kondratiev long wave, an East-West 500-year cycle, household debt cycles and the shift in world monetary systems. He says all these cycles have peaked together, which is deflationary, and that central banks will then over-print. He notes that the Fed controls base money only and steers the rest with interest rates.

Quote: "Most of this currency does not circulate."

Main claims:
- Most new base money after 2008 sits as excess reserves earning interest and does not circulate.
- The Fed controls only base money and influences the rest through rates.
- Several cycles have peaked together, so deflation comes before inflation.

### Episode 7. Velocity of Money (published 2016-10-05)

Summary. The episode explains velocity, the number of times each dollar changes hands, with a waiter, cab driver and gas station example and a ten-person economy. Maloney reads from his book on Weimar Germany: the money supply quadrupled in World War I with little price effect because people saved, then prices caught up when confidence returned, and again when people learned to spend money as fast as they got it. He argues that US velocity has fallen to offset money creation since 2008 and that a change in psychology will make velocity rise and trigger inflation or hyperinflation. Jim Rickards adds that the Fed controls money supply but not velocity, and that once inflation expectations shift they are hard to reverse. Maloney reads his pre-2008 forecast of a boomer-driven crash, a failed helicopter drop, and then tax-rebate "bombers" that set off hyperinflation.

Quote: "When people save their currency, it stops circulating."

Main claims:
- Prices depend on money times velocity, and velocity is psychological.
- Falling velocity explains why post-2008 money creation did not cause high inflation.
- A rebound in velocity will bring big inflation or hyperinflation.

### Episode 8. The New System That Could Replace Banks (published 2017-12-18)

Summary. The longest episode moves to cryptocurrency. Filmed at a Bitcoin conference and in Washington, it explains distributed ledgers through the Byzantine Generals problem, then walks through Bitcoin mining, blocks and the 21 million coin limit. Maloney and guest Chris Ellis raise concerns that a fixed-supply coin may be hoarded in a deflation and that miner rewards end. Patrick Byrne of Overstock explains why accepting Bitcoin can save card fees for low-margin retailers. Most of the second half promotes Hedera Hashgraph, with its inventor describing a gossip-about-gossip consensus method and claims of very high transaction throughput. Maloney says he will put some capital into crypto but will not sell his gold and silver, and calls Bitcoin an experiment that could fail.

Quote: "It's called full consensus distributed ledger technology."

Main claims:
- Distributed ledgers could replace banks, much of Wall Street and some government functions.
- Bitcoin is an experiment with economic weaknesses (deflationary hoarding, miner incentives).
- Hashgraph solves consensus at far higher speed than Bitcoin.

### Episode 9. Why Empires Collapse: Rome, America and the Money Printing Trap (published 2018-10-29)

Summary. The first of a Rome double episode builds a timeline comparing the Roman Republic and Empire with the United States. Rome had stable prices with full-weight coins, then debased its coinage for the Second Punic War, clipped and restamped coins, and kept cutting silver content to pay for wars and public works. The episode compares this with US debasement from World War I to Nixon's 1971 decision, and with modern "shrinkflation". Steve Forbes argues money should be a fixed measure like an inch or a minute. Maloney presents Diocletian's Edict of Prices (301 AD), the French Law of the Maximum and Nixon's 1971 wage and price freeze as proof that price controls fail. He praises the "Depression of 1921" as a sharp deflation that healed fast because the Fed and government did not intervene, and blames Greenspan's post-2000 rate cuts for the housing bubble.

Quote: "wage and price controls do not work"

Main claims:
- Rome and the US follow the same path of debasement for war and public works.
- Price controls fail every time (Diocletian, the French Revolution, Nixon).
- The 1921 depression healed in 18 months because the Fed did not intervene.

### Episode 10. Empire or Freedom? The Final Warning from Rome (published 2018-10-30)

Summary. The second Rome episode adds bread and circuses (grain handed to about a fifth of Rome's population), heavier taxes, bureaucracy, cronyism and military overreach, each paired with a modern US parallel. A segment with a Roman restaurant owner and Maloney's own business move out of California illustrates regulatory burden. Chris Martenson explains exponential growth with a doubling-water-drop thought experiment. Ron Paul, Max Keiser, Grant Williams and Steve Forbes give short views, and Eisenhower's 1961 warning on the military-industrial complex is shown. The episode credits the Eastern Roman Empire's long survival largely to the gold solidus, kept pure for centuries until debasement after 1034. It closes by asking Americans to choose sound money, restrained government and freedom over debasement and empire.

Quote: "the quality of a society is directly proportional to the quality of its money"

Main claims:
- Welfare, bureaucracy, cronyism and war spending, paid for with debasement and taxes, brought Rome down.
- The Byzantine solidus shows that sound money sustains a society for centuries.
- There have been no free markets since 1913, because the FOMC sets the price and quantity of money.

Main claims:
- Total US government spending is about $7 trillion, about $21,212 per person.
- 1970s price controls caused waste and shortages.
- The 1921 depression was the largest US deflation and ended without intervention.

## Where the series' money-creation teaching differs from central banks today

1. Order of events. Ep 4 starts with deposits and has banks lend them out. Central banks describe the reverse: the loan creates the deposit, and reserves follow (BoE 2014). Ep 3's credit card line already had the modern view, so the series is internally inconsistent.
2. The multiplier. Ep 4 and the ECB display in Ep 5 teach a fixed 10x multiplier. In practice the central bank sets the price of reserves and supplies the quantity the system demands. Since 2008 reserves have been abundant, so they cannot be the binding limit. Since 2020-03-26 the required ratio is zero. The real limits are capital rules, profitable demand, liquidity rules and the policy rate.
3. What QE does. The series treats QE as money handed to banks to lend. The Fed and BoE describe QE as an asset swap whose deposit effect depends on the seller: purchases from nonbanks add deposits, purchases from banks add only reserves (FEDS Note 2022, BoE 2014).
4. The Fed and the Treasury. Ep 4 puts the Fed between the auction and the Treasury's cash. The Fed is barred from buying new Treasuries from the Treasury (section 14(b), "only in the open market", since 1935, with a capped wartime exception from 1942 that lapsed in 1981). The Fed's purchases help deficits indirectly by holding down yields and by replacing bonds in private hands with reserves and deposits.
5. Where interest goes. Ep 4 says taxes pay interest to the Fed's owners. Coupons paid to the Fed go back to the Treasury as remittances when the Fed is profitable. The flow that now matters is the reverse: the Fed paying interest on reserves to banks, which since 2022 has run above its income.

What became outdated after 2008: interest on reserves (from 2008-10-01), abundant excess reserves (reserves rose from tens of billions to trillions), and the move to a floor system confirmed in January 2019. After this, the quantity of reserves stopped constraining lending, and the Fed sets rates by paying interest instead of by rationing reserves. The Ep 6 description of reserves "sitting" at the Fed is accurate for this new world.

What became outdated after 2020: reserve requirements went to zero on 2020-03-26, so the 10% rule in Ep 4 and the "vault cash" framing no longer exist in law. The 2020 to 2021 episode also showed money creation through the fiscal channel at a scale the series did not model: the Treasury spent deposits directly into household accounts while the Fed and banks absorbed most of the new debt, and M2 rose $6.3T (41%) in two years.

## What the series gets right

- Most money is created as debt, and repayment destroys it (Ep 3 and Ep 4).
- Debasement is an old and recurring way to pay for war and public works (Ep 2, 9, 10), and the 1913 to 1971 history is told accurately in outline.
- The Fed's Treasury purchases finance deficits indirectly (Ep 4), and on a large scale in 2020 to 2021.
- After 2008 much base money sat idle as interest-bearing reserves and the Fed steers by rates (Ep 6).
- Money growth and inflation are linked over time, and expectations and velocity matter (Ep 1 and Ep 7).
