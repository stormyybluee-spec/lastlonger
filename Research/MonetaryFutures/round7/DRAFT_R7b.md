## L2.1 Bonds: a ticket you can resell, and where it touches your life (id rb-bonds)

Kicker: A bond is a ticket you can resell: you pay RM100, it pays you RM4 a year, and you get RM100 back at the end. This chapter builds everything from that one ticket, then shows where bonds touch your own life, how to check your own exposure once a month, and how to weigh scary bond headlines before you feel them.

[0 tldr]
- A bond is a ticket: you pay RM100, get RM4 a year, and get your RM100 back at the end.
- You can keep it or resell it through a dealer. That resale market is the secondary market.
- Yields up, prices down. A single bond held to the end still pays back its RM100. A bond fund never ends, so its dips are real when you sell.
- Bonds touch you through EPF, loans, the ringgit, prices and taxes, even if you never buy one.
- Rising yields hurt people holding long bonds today and help people who will save for years, if yields stay above inflation.

### 2. A bond is a ticket

[11 p] Forget finance for a moment. The government sells you a ticket for RM100. It pays you RM4 every year. In ten years the government buys it back for RM100.

[12 diagram] rm-ticket: One ticket carries the whole chapter: RM100 in, RM4 a year, RM100 back.

[13 p] That ticket is a bond. You lent the government RM100, so it owes you, and RM4 a year is your reward for waiting. Two limits of the picture: a concert ticket is worthless after the show, but a bond ends with your RM100 back. And a bond fund holds many tickets and never ends.

[14 cards] Five words to start with
- Bond: A ticket that pays a fixed amount each year and your money back on a set date.
- Face value: The amount paid back at the end. RM100 in our ticket.
- Coupon: The fixed yearly payment. RM4 in our ticket, or 4%.
- Maturity: The end date, when the ticket is bought back.
- Yield: What a ticket earns per year if you buy at today's price and hold to the end. New tickets are sold at the going yield.

[15 insider] (Malaysia note) About half of Malaysia's government debt is Islamic paper, mostly GII. Most GII is built on a deferred sale of a commodity to the government, so holders earn profit, not interest. The ticket picture still works.


### 3. The seesaw: why your ticket's price moves

[17 p] A month later, new tickets pay RM5 a year: the going yield has risen from 4% to 5%. Nobody will pay RM100 for your RM4 ticket now. Its price drops until a buyer also gets a yield of about 5%.

[18 steps]
- Worked example. Your ticket pays RM4 and has 10 years left. New tickets yield 5%. A buyer pays about RM92.28. They get RM4 a year plus a RM7.72 gain at the end, a yield of about 5%.
- Your turn, half done. New tickets yield 3% instead. Is your RM4 ticket worth more or less than RM100? (More, about RM108.53, because it pays more than new tickets.)
- Your turn. A ticket with only 2 years left, when new tickets yield 5%. Does it fall more or less than the 10-year ticket? (Much less, about 1.9%, because only two years of too-small payments are left.)

[19 diagram] seesaw: Yields and prices sit on a seesaw. When yields rise, old tickets are worth less, and the other way round.

[20 widget] bond-lab: Try it. Drag the yield on new bonds and watch three $1,000 bonds reprice.

[21 p] Two rules fall out of the seesaw. Yields up, prices down. And the longer the ticket, the bigger the swing. A point means one percentage point, say from 4% to 5%. For a one-point rise, a 2-year ticket falls about 2% and a 30-year ticket about 15% (a model). A bond's duration tells you its swing: a fund with a duration of 6 falls about 6% per one-point rise. Fund factsheets print it.

[22 myth] Myth: Government bonds are safe, so you cannot lose money on them.
Reality: They are safe from default, not from price. In 2022 the main US bond index lost 13.01%, its worst year since the index began in 1976. Hold a single bond to the end and you get every ringgit back. Sell a fund's units after a dip and the loss is real.


### 4. The resale stall: the secondary market

[24 p] There are two markets. The first sale is the government's auction, a ticket booth. In Malaysia, Bank Negara runs it and 20 principal dealers must bid. Every later sale is at a resale stall, the secondary market: one investor sells to another through a dealer. The government gets nothing from resales, but the price shows what its next auction will cost.

[25 diagram] resale-stall: The booth sells new tickets. Old tickets change hands at the resale stall, and the dealer earns the gap.

[26 steps]
- To sell, you contact a dealer: a bank's trading desk or a broker.
- The dealer shows a buy price and a sell price, say RM99.50 and RM99.60. The difference is the dealer's gap, its fee for standing ready. Ordinary buyers usually pay a wider gap than big investors, so ask for both prices.
- The price shown is the clean price. The buyer also pays you the interest earned since the last payment.

[27 p] The dealer's gap is what you pay to be able to sell quickly. It widens when everyone wants out. In March 2020 even US government bonds were hard to sell, and the Fed bought $1.27 trillion in five weeks.

[41 p] Rising yields in the news usually means the 10-year government bond yield. Here is what a rise does to a normal person.

[43 table]

[46 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): What makes yields move: seven forces, and 2026 as an example
[46.0 p] Why would new tickets suddenly pay RM5 instead of RM4? Think of a tug of war. The central bank's own rate, expected inflation, how many tickets the government must sell, how many buyers turn up, how fast the economy grows, and how much people trust the borrower all pull on the price of a new ticket.

[46.1 diagram] yield-tug: Seven forces pull on the yield of a new ticket.

[46.2 table]
| Force | What it means | Which way it pushes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The Fed's rate path | Short yields track where the Fed sets overnight rates now and where markets expect it to go | Expected hikes push yields up, expected cuts pull them down |
| 2. Expected inflation | Lenders want compensation for prices rising while they wait | More expected inflation means higher yields |
| 3. Term premium | Extra pay for locking money up for years when the future is uncertain | More uncertainty about deficits, inflation or policy pushes long yields up |
| 4. Supply | How much the Treasury must borrow and in what lengths | Bigger deficits and more long bonds push yields up |
| 5. Demand | Who must or wants to buy: foreign central banks, banks, money funds, pensions, stablecoins | Captive or eager buyers pull yields down, a big seller pushes them up |
| 6. Growth | A strong economy competes for money, a weak one frees it up | Booms raise yields, recessions lower them |
| 7. Trust | Confidence in repayment and in the value of the currency repaid | Any doubt raises yields, a crisis elsewhere can lower them as money flees to safety |

[46.3 diagram] yield-parts: Split the 10-year yield in two. The gold part pays you back for expected inflation. The blue part is the real reward for waiting.

[46.4 p] You can see several forces at once in 2026. The Fed hiked in September (force 1). Oil prices kept inflation worries alive, and the bond market expected about 2.4% a year of inflation over ten years (force 2). A $1.99T deficit meant heavy issuance (force 4). Japan's recorded holdings of Treasuries fell $171B from February to August while it spent a similar sum defending the yen (force 5). Meanwhile French stress pushed some European money into Treasuries for safety (force 7 in reverse). The result: a 10-year yield of 5.13% on 21 October 2026, after a peak of 5.31% on 5 October.

### 7. Hidden risks, and what protects you

[48 table]
| Risk | How it reaches you | A real number | What reduces it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price risk | Your bond fund falls when yields rise | The main US bond index lost 13.01% in 2022 | Holding a single bond to the end. Shorter bonds |
| Inflation risk | Your fixed RM4 buys less | US bonds bought at 0.52% in 2020 paid far less than 2022's 9.1% inflation | Inflation-linked bonds. Short bonds that reset quickly |
| Forced-seller risk | You must sell at the worst moment | UK pension funds, September 2022: the Bank of England bought £19.3 billion of bonds to stop a spiral | Not borrowing to hold bonds. Emergency cash |

[50 p] Why the difference? 2008 was a recession: people fled to safety, yields fell and bond prices rose. 2022 was an inflation shock: yields rose fast and bonds fell with shares. Higher yields today also mean more income tomorrow: in October 2026 a US inflation-linked bond paid about 2.6 points above inflation, against about minus 1 point at the end of 2021.

### What is left after inflation

[53 p] Every yield you see is before inflation, Level 1's rubber ruler. Malaysian individuals generally pay no income tax on interest and profit from government bonds and approved sukuk, so the real return is roughly the yield minus inflation: about 3.95% minus 1.8%, or about 2.1 points (October 2026). In the US, take tax off first.

[54 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The US real-return walk, with tax
[54.0 p] On 21 October 2026 the US 10-year yielded 5.13% and the 10-year inflation-linked Treasury 2.76%, so about 2.4 points of the 5.13% was pay for expected inflation.

[54.1 steps]
- Start with the nominal yield: 5.13% on a 10-year Treasury in October 2026, or $51.30 a year on $1,000.
- Take out tax. In a 24% federal bracket about $39 is left. Treasury interest is free of state income tax, but not of federal tax.
- Take out inflation. If prices rise 2.4% a year, about what the bond market expects, $24 of that $39 only keeps up with prices. Your real gain is about 1.5% a year.
- If inflation turns out higher than expected, the real return drops below zero, even though every payment arrives on time.

[55.2 p] Two models, both using the Fed's H.15 daily closes. The first column reprices a 10-year bond as if the whole jump in yields hit it at once. The second follows a real holder who bought a new 10-year at par at the low, still owned it at the high with fewer years left, and collected the coupons along the way (coupons not reinvested, computed for this chapter). Notice the one gain: in 2008 Treasuries did their job as everything else fell. Notice too that 2022 was different. Stocks and bonds fell together, which broke the old idea that one always rescues the other.

[55.3 p] How can a bank fail holding bonds that never missed a payment? Walk through a bank shaped like Silicon Valley Bank.

[55.4 table]
| Step | The bank owns | The bank owes |
|---|---|---|
| 2021: deposits flood in | $100 of 10-year bonds bought at 1.5%, plus $8 of cash and loans | $100 of deposits, and $8 of owners' money |
| 2022: yields rise to 4.5% | The bonds are now worth about $78, plus the $8 | Still $100 of deposits. Marked to market, owners' money is -$14 |
| 2023: depositors leave | Bonds must be sold to pay them | Many deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit, so they left fast. The hidden loss becomes real. Owners' money is gone |

[55.5 p] Accounting rules let the loss stay hidden while the bank promised to hold the bonds to the end. A run forced a sale, and the paper loss became real in a day. The bonds were never in default. SVB's held-to-maturity losses were about $15.1B, close to all of its owners' money. Two days after SVB closed, the Fed opened the Bank Term Funding Program, which lent to banks against Treasuries at their face value, not their market value. A bond's price can fall, but in that panic its value as collateral was held at par by policy.

[55.6 curiosity]
- Q: How much could a risk-free 10-year Treasury lose?
  A: On price alone, about 32% for someone who bought a new 10-year at 8.76% in July 1979 and still held it in September 1981, when yields hit 15.84%. Counting the coupons collected, the loss was about 14%. From 2021 to 2023 a holder lost about 24% on price and about 21% with coupons (models, Fed H.15 data). Risk-free means free of default, not free of price swings. (builds on rb-13)
- Q: Someone bought a 10-year Treasury at 0.625% in 2020. What is it worth now, and did they lose money?
  A: About $84 per $100 at today's yields with four years left (model). They lose nothing in dollars if they hold to 2030, but they lose buying power: inflation peaked at 9.1% in 2022. (builds on rb-10)
- Q: A bank failed in 2023 holding bonds that never missed a payment. How?
  A: Its bonds had lost value as yields rose, about $15.1B at SVB, close to its owners' money, and US banks together carried $620B of such losses at the end of 2022 (FDIC). When depositors ran, the paper loss became real. (builds on rb-07)

[56 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): How governments quietly shrink debt with inflation
[56.0 p] This is not theory. In the 1940s the Fed held long-term yields near 2.5% while inflation ran in double digits after the war, quietly shrinking the war debt in real terms. In the 1970s inflation often ran above bond yields. And anyone who bought a 10-year Treasury at its record low closing yield of 0.52% in August 2020 earned about half a percent a year, while US consumer inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022.

[56.1 insider] This is the quietest way governments shrink debt. Keep the nominal yield below inflation for long enough and the real value of the debt melts, paid for by bondholders and savers who only look at the nominal number. Level 1's "The seasons of money" called it financial repression. The cure is the split in step 3: always separate a yield into the part that only keeps up with inflation and the part that is truly yours.

### 10. Go deeper: the government's side and the machinery

[78 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The government's side, in one page
[78.0 p] Why do governments sell tickets? Much of each year's new borrowing repays old tickets that are ending. The rest covers the gap when spending is more than taxes. For a government that issues its own money, borrowing is also a rule it sets for itself: in the US the Fed may not buy new Treasuries straight from the government (Level 1, "How money is born today"), and safe bonds help a central bank steer interest rates. Banks, pension funds and central banks also need a safe ticket to hold. Each new ticket is sold at an auction where dealers must bid. The resale price then works like a vote that never stops: if buyers doubt a government's plans, yields rise and its interest bill grows.

[78.1 p] That bill is already large. Malaysia's debt service is projected at RM57.3 billion in 2026, 15.8% of revenue, above the 15% limit Malaysia set itself in its 2023 fiscal law (Act 850). In the US, net interest passed $1 trillion in fiscal 2026, more than defense.

[79 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Why the whole system rests on bonds (the backbone test)
[79.0 diagram] bond-tower: Picture a champagne tower at a wedding. Government bonds are the bottom row of glasses. Every glass above rests on them.

[79.1 list]
- The Fed's money rests on them. The Fed creates bank reserves and cash, and the main asset it holds against them is Treasuries: $4.57T of its $6.47T of securities, about 71% (21 October 2026).
- They are the financial system's pawnshop ticket. In repo markets, banks and funds hand over Treasuries to borrow cash, often overnight. About $12.6T of US repo was outstanding on an average day in the third quarter of 2025 (Office of Financial Research).
- Banks and money funds park cash in them, because rules treat them as the safest liquid asset.
- Stablecoins must hold them. Under the GENIUS Act, compliant dollar tokens must be backed by cash-like reserves: deposits, central bank money, government money funds, repo, or Treasuries with 93 days or less left. Short bills are the dominant choice.
- Foreign central banks keep their savings in them. That is how the world holds dollars without holding cash.
- They set the base price of money. Mortgages, car loans and company loans are priced as the Treasury yield plus a margin for risk.

[82 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Repo: how a bond is pawned overnight
[82.0 p] Repo is a loan secured by a bond. You sell a bond for cash today and agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The difference is the interest. The lender keeps a cushion called a haircut, for example lending $98 against $100 of bonds. Here a hedge fund buys a $100 Treasury and funds it in repo through a dealer, and a money market fund supplies the cash.

[82.1 table]
| Who | What they now own | What they now owe | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hedge fund | $100 Treasury (pledged) | $98 repo loan, plus $2 of its own money | It holds a $100 bond with only $2 of its own money. In practice this is usually one leg of a hedged trade (the cash-futures basis trade), not a bare bet |
| Dealer (a bank's arm) | $98 loan to the fund | $98 borrowed from the money fund | It is the middleman and nets the two |
| Money market fund | $98 repo loan, safe and overnight | nothing new | It swapped a bank deposit for a loan backed by a Treasury |
| The two floors | Reserves move between banks | Deposits move from the fund to the bond's seller | Public money only moved. Nothing was created |

[82.2 p] The US repo market had about $12.6T of loans outstanding on an average day in the third quarter of 2025 (Office of Financial Research). That is why a Treasury is worth more than its coupons: it can be turned into cash overnight at a small haircut. It is also where the system breaks. When haircuts jump or dealers run out of room, funds must sell bonds, prices fall, haircuts rise again. That loop drove March 2020.

[83 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Duration and convexity: the bond's lever
[83.0 p] Duration is the number that tells you how hard a bond swings. Roughly, a bond's price moves by its duration, in percent, for each one point move in yields, the other way. It is also about the average time until you get your money back, with near payments counting more than far ones. A 2-year Treasury has a duration near 2, a 10-year near 8, a 30-year near 15.

[84 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The yield curve, and the bond market as a voter
[84.0 diagram] bond-ripple: One move in the 10-year yield, four ripples.

[84.1 p] Take a $400,000 mortgage over 30 years. At 6.40% the monthly payment is about $2,502. At 7.40%, one point higher, it is about $2,770. One point on the bond market costs that family about $267 a month, about $3,210 a year, for thirty years.

[84.2 diagram] yield-curve: The yield curve: yields lined up from short to long. Normal curves slope up. An inverted curve, drawn here as an example, often warns of a slowdown.

[84.3 p] Line up yields from short to long and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes up, because waiting longer deserves more pay. In October 2026 it does: about 4.81% at 2 years, 5.13% at 10 years and 5.42% at 30 years. When short yields rise above long ones (an inverted curve), markets are betting the Fed will have to cut, usually because a slowdown is coming. Inversions have come before many US recessions, though the timing is loose. The longest inversion of the 2s and 10s on record, 541 trading days from April 2022 to September 2024, has not been followed by a recession so far, and by 22 October 2026 the curve sloped up again by 0.31 points.

[87.0 list]
- A concert ticket is worthless after the show. A bond ticket ends with a RM100 buy-back.

### Part 2 of 4. Who can really print money


### 3. Three locks on the printing press

[23 p] Here printing means a state and its central bank creating the money that the state's debts are owed in. Banks are the other printer, which Level 2 chapter 2 covers. To print freely, a country must open three locks.

[24 steps]
- Lock 1, the law. Does it issue its own currency? Italy, Ecuador and Panama do not. They use the euro or the US dollar, made by someone else.
- Lock 2, the debt. Does the state owe its debt in money it can create? Sri Lanka could print rupees, but owed dollars, and stopped paying on 12 April 2022.
- Lock 3, the market. Can it print a lot in a crisis without a currency crash or runaway prices? That depends on whether the world wants its money, how open its markets are, and its inflation record. Turkey and Argentina had their own money but owed a large part of their debt in dollars, and saw inflation reach 72% (2022) and 220% (2024). They failed lock 3 and partly lock 2.

[25 p] Each lock is needed and none is enough alone. Passing locks 1 and 2 lets a country pay in its own money, if its law allows and its parliament does not block payment, as the US debt-limit fights of 2011 and 2023 threatened. It cannot choose what that money will buy.

### Part 4 of 4. The game the world plays


### 6. Game theory in five minutes

[52 p] Game theory asks: what is each player's best move, given what the others might do? Each game below is a box with two players, two moves each, and what you get in each cell, from best to worst.

[53 table]
| Game | The shape | Where it settles |
|---|---|---|
| Stag hunt (coordination) | Everyone gains most if all act. Acting alone is costly | All act or none. Each acts only if it expects the others to |
| Prisoner's dilemma | Each gains by cheating whatever the other does | Both cheat and both end up worse off |
| Free rider | A shared good is paid for by some and enjoyed by all | Each player's best move is to let others pay |
| Focal point | Many choices would work, so players pick the one everyone expects | The habit wins, even if others are as good |
| Chicken | Two players drive at each other | Each tries to look more committed so the other swerves |
| Run | Lenders who expect others to stay, stay. Those who expect others to flee, flee first | The same borrower can be safe or bust depending on belief. Big reserves or a backstop make staying the best move |

[54 callout] (What game theory leaves out) Domestic politics (a leader facing an election may choose the worse national outcome), ideology, accidents and plain error. Use the games as a lens on incentives, not as proof that every move was planned.


### 7. Five questions that open the game board

[56 p] Guess each answer before you open it. Each one opens a game, and each game is a real case.

[57 curiosity]
- Q: Why did the G20 move within months in 2008 to 2009, but take years on poor countries' debts?
  A: Two games. 2009 was a stag hunt (box 1): the shared fear of collapse made each member expect the others to act, and out came a $1.1 trillion package. Debt relief is a free-rider game (box 2): relief from one lender frees cash to pay the others. The 2020 pause covered $12.9 billion, but only one private lender joined, and Ethiopia's deal took more than five years to reach agreement in principle. (builds on rb-leader)
- Q: If every country wants a tariff edge or a cheaper currency, what happens when they all try at once?
  A: Tariffs are the clean prisoner's dilemma (box 3): all lose when all raise them, as in the tariff war of the early 1930s. Currencies are murkier: in the 1930s devaluing helped the countries that did it, and historians find that devaluing together would have sped recovery (Eichengreen and Sachs, 1985). Exits have been deals like the 1985 Plaza Accord, or truces like the October 2025 US and China pause, which can lapse. Two giants trading threats are playing chicken. (builds on rb-leader)
- Q: Why does the world keep saving in US dollars even while complaining about the US?
  A: A focal point (box 4). Money is useful because others use it. The dollar was 56.7% of world reserves in mid 2026, nearly three times the euro. The yuan was 2.1%, largely because China keeps capital controls. (builds on rb-04)
- Q: Why did Malaysia and its neighbours pile up dollars after 1998 instead of spending them?
  A: To change the payoffs of a run on their currency (box 5). In 1997 to 1998 the ringgit fell from about 2.50 to about 4.88, and Thailand, Indonesia and Korea had to accept IMF conditions. Malaysia's reserves rose from $21.5 billion in 1997 to $132.6 billion in June 2026. Big reserves make attacking a losing bet, and mean the country need not accept the IMF's terms. (builds on rb-em)
- Q: Why can Japan owe about twice its economy and borrow cheaply, while Sri Lanka defaulted?
  A: A run on the debt (box 6): who holds it, and in which money, largely decides it. Japan owes yen mostly to itself: its central bank holds about 43% and foreigners about 13% (end 2025). Sri Lanka owed dollars to foreigners and stopped paying on 12 April 2022. Malaysia borrows in ringgit, but foreigners hold about a fifth of its bonds, so its risk is a weaker ringgit, not default. (builds on rb-worldbs)

### 8. Read any headline as a game

[71 steps]
- Players: who can act? Include the quiet ones.
- Moves: what can each do? Two moves each if you can.
- Payoffs: what does each get from each pair of moves? Best, good, bad or worst.
- Order and information: who moves first, and who sees what?
- Then ask what the game leaves out: elections, ideology, accidents and plain error.

[75 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): A sixth game: the race to the bottom on tax
[75.0 p] Countries gain by undercutting each other's tax rates to attract company profits, and all lose revenue if all undercut. That is a prisoner's dilemma, and the 2021 deal set a 15% floor. Malaysia applied it from 1 January 2025. The US did not enact it, and in January 2026 the OECD package shielded US-based groups from the main top-up rules. The largest player took the defector's payoff, and the others accepted it to keep the rest of the deal. The US argues its own minimum taxes do a similar job, which is a real debate, not only defection.

[76 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Three balance-sheet walks: the dollar privilege, defending the ringgit, a euro member borrowing
[76.0 p] The dollar privilege. A US household buys Malaysian-made chips. The exporter sells the dollars to its bank, which sells them to Bank Negara, which buys US Treasury bills. No dollars were created abroad. Dollars moved from a US household to a foreign central bank, which lent them back to the US government. The US got goods for IOUs in its own money. Bank Negara created ringgit to buy the dollars, and usually soaks them back up later.

[76.1 p] Defending the ringgit. A foreign fund sells Malaysian bonds and wants dollars. Bank Negara sells dollars from its reserves and receives ringgit, so banks' ringgit reserves fall. It then usually tops them back up to keep its interest rate at 2.75%. The cost is dollar reserves, which is why the 0.9 times ratio matters. A defence that lasts too long runs reserves down, as in 1997 to 1998.

[76.2 p] A euro member borrowing. Italy spends more than it taxes. It must sell bonds to banks, funds, households and foreigners. The ECB can create euros and buy Italian bonds on the resale market, but only by a decision taken for the whole euro area. In a panic, deposits can move to banks in another euro country, as in Greece, where about EUR 41 billion left before the June 2015 controls.
