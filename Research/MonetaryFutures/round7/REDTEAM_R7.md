## 4. Malaysian household voices

### 4.1 EPF member who has never bought a bond
Most important miss: the chapter never mentions the EPF dividend, the one bond-linked number every member knows (6.15% for 2025, per the lane file, R7A-11). It also never says how bond prices reach that dividend. EPF says it pays dividends only from realised income, so unrealised mark-to-market moves are not paid out (EPF statements reported by the press). So the EPF row in [45], "Old bonds dip on paper", is true but misleading for a member: the dip does not cut this year's dividend directly. The real link is slower: new money and maturing bonds are reinvested at the new yield.
Blocking: yes. EPF is the main touch point for the readers.
Fix: add to the EPF row and to [41]: "EPF pays its dividend from income it has actually received, so a paper dip in bond prices does not cut your dividend directly. Higher yields lift future income as old bonds mature and new money is invested." Also add a Malaysian retiree household (framer 3.7 asked for a 60-year-old drawing EPF). Linda in the US does not cover the Malaysian at 55 or 60 who moves a lump sum into an FD or a fund.

### 4.2 Malaysian bond dealer
Most important misstatement: "98.8% of it is owed at home" (L2.4 [41], [46], [53], [59]) and "Malaysia's federal debt is 98.8% domestic" (L2.1 [53]). The Ministry of Finance's "domestic debt" means debt raised in ringgit in the local market. It includes MGS and GII held by foreigners. The lane says so itself: 98.8% domestic, and offshore loans of RM16.7 billion (R7B lane, line 212). So "owed at home" is wrong and makes the next sentence ("a fifth held by foreigners") look like a contradiction. Both cold readers tripped on it.
Blocking: yes.
Fix: "98.8% is borrowed in ringgit in Malaysia's own market. About a fifth of the government bonds in that market are held by foreigners." Use one foreign-share figure with one date across both chapters (21.4% [44], 21.5% [30], 21.6% [90.0] today).

Second misstatement: "A sukuk gives you a share in an asset or its rent, not a loan" ([15], repeated in [92.0]). That describes an ijarah sukuk. GII issued since 22 July 2013 uses murabahah: Bank Negara buys a commodity (often palm oil) for the bidders and sells it to the government at a mark-up paid later, and that payment obligation is the GII. It is a certificate of debt from a deferred sale, not a share of an asset. Before 2013 GII used bai al-inah. In 2024 Bank Negara's Shariah Advisory Council allowed wakalah as an alternative (Bank Muamalat GII note and search summary, see sources). Blocking: yes, it is a factual error on the main path.
Fix for [15]: "About half of Malaysia's government paper is GII, an Islamic instrument. Most GII is built on a sale: the government buys a commodity and pays for it later at a mark-up, so you earn a profit rather than interest. The ticket picture still works for price and resale."

Third point: the retail cost in [27] ("RM99.50 and RM99.60, about RM10 on RM10,000") is an interdealer-sized gap. A retail buyer of a seasoned bond or a thinly traded Bursa bond usually pays a wider gap. Non-blocking. Fix: "Big investors pay gaps this small. Ordinary buyers usually pay more, so ask for both prices before you trade."

### 4.3 Malaysian personal-finance writer
Most important miss: the main-path real-return walk [59] and the final check [99] teach a tax step that does not apply to most Malaysian readers. Interest paid to individuals on government bonds, and on bonds and sukuk approved by or lodged with the Securities Commission, is generally exempt from Malaysian income tax (PwC Malaysian Tax Booklet, SC incentives page, Income Tax Act Schedule 6). The chapter gives only a US federal bracket.
Blocking: yes, small fix. A Malaysian reader leaves with a wrong rule.
Fix: make [59] Malaysian first: "A Malaysian individual usually pays no income tax on interest from government bonds and approved bonds and sukuk, so for you the real return is roughly the yield minus inflation. In the US, interest is taxed, so take tax off first." Keep the US line as a second row. Ask a tax source to confirm the current wording.

Other gaps for the Malaysian lens (non-blocking):
- Home loans: the SBR rule ([45], [47], [64]) covers floating loans taken from 1 August 2022. Older loans stay on the base rate (BR), which banks set. Islamic home financing, a large share of new home financing, often states a ceiling rate. Say "loans taken since August 2022" and add one line on older and Islamic loans.
- The touch-point table lacks PTPTN and Tabung Haji, both in the lane file (R7A lines 222 and 228).

### Economic historian
Most important misstatement: L2.4 [53] Q2 and the bridge [1] say currency devaluation is a prisoner's dilemma: "each gains alone, all lose together, as in the 1930s". The standard study, Eichengreen and Sachs (1985), found that devaluing helped the countries that did it, that individual devaluations did hurt trading partners, but that wider, coordinated devaluation would have sped recovery from the Depression. So for 1930s exchange rates "all lose together" is not the finding. The tariff war of the 1930s fits a prisoner's dilemma much better. The owner asked for "factual" games.
Blocking: yes, for the "factual" ask.
Fix: "Tariffs are the clean prisoner's dilemma: each gains alone, all lose when all raise them, as in the early 1930s. Devaluation is murkier: in the 1930s it helped the countries that did it and hurt their neighbours, but historians find that if all had devalued together, recovery would have come sooner." Keep the Mantega quote and the Plaza Accord.

## 7. RT3 Schools and readers

### MMT critic
Most important miss: [81] says governments sell bonds "because they spend more than they collect in taxes". For a currency issuer like the US or Malaysia this is a legal and operating rule (the treasury may not overdraw at the central bank, and bonds help the central bank hold its interest rate), not a household-style need to find money. L2.4 [32] states the MMT view fairly, but L2.1 teaches the household view as fact.
Blocking: no.
Fix: add to [81]: "For a government that issues its own money, borrowing is also a rule it sets for itself. The law in the US and Malaysia makes the treasury borrow rather than draw on the central bank, and safe bonds help the central bank steer interest rates."

### Hard-money critic
Most important miss: the household cost of rung-1 printing, slow loss of buying power, is in a Go deeper box ([62], financial repression). On the main path of L2.4, "The US can print forever: partly true" [31] gives no number for what dollars lost.
Blocking: no.
Fix: one sourced line in [31]: what a 1971 dollar buys today, from the US consumer price index (BLS). Do not use a number without fetching it.

### Ordinary reader who takes headlines literally
Most important misstatement: the summary line [0] "A young saver with decades ahead mostly gains" and [46] "on the winning side ... even on days the headlines call it a crash". A literal reader hears "rising rates are good for me". That holds only for new money, and only if yields rise more than inflation. If yields rise because inflation rises, the young saver's real return can fall. A young person who puts a lump sum into a long bond fund loses on the day. And the young also face higher loan rates for a first home.
Blocking: yes. It is the Level 1 headline.
Fix for [0]: "The same rise in yields hurts people holding long bonds today and helps people who will add new savings for years, as long as yields stay above inflation." Same fix in [46] and [73].
Second literal-reader trap: "A price dip only costs you if you sell" in the Rules [100], [22] and [23]. True for one bond held to the end. Not true for a bond fund, which never ends [40], and not true for buying power. The Rules line will be repeated without its conditions. Fix: "For a single bond held to the end, a price dip costs you only if you sell. A bond fund never ends, so its dips are real when you sell units."
Third: [31] "It can always pay in dollars" on the US. The US debt limit (2011, 2023) is a legal route to missed payments that printing power does not remove. Add "unless Congress blocks it through the debt limit".

## 8. L2.4 specialists

### Game theorist
Most important miss: the games are named but not specified. The framer's spec 4.5 asked for players, moves, payoffs (in numbers where possible), the game type, a documented case, what game theory misses in that case, and a tripwire. The draft has players, cases and tripwires [54], but no payoff box for any game, and only one general "what game theory leaves out" box [50]. Three specification errors:
1. [16] and [20] say that in a crisis "acting together is each member's best move". That describes a game with a dominant strategy (harmony), not a coordination game. In the primer [49] coordination is correctly "gain most if all act, acting alone is costly". The point of 2009 is that a shared shock made each member expect the others to act. Even then there was free riding (some members resisted more fiscal stimulus), which the lane itself notes as stimulus leaking abroad through imports (R7B line 273).
2. "Self-insurance" is listed as a game in [0] and Q4 [53] but is not in the primer [49] and has no second player. It is a game only against speculators (a currency attack is a run game, and reserves change the payoff of attacking) or against the IMF (reserves improve the outside option in bargaining over conditions).
3. The 1930s case in Q2 is misread (see the economic historian).
Blocking: yes. This is the owner's main ask for L2.4.
Fix: give each game a 2 by 2 box with plain ordinal payoffs (best, good, bad, worst), name the equilibrium in one line, and add one line per case on what the game misses. Recast Q4 as a run game against speculators, with the IMF as the outside option, and add "run" or "self-insurance against a run" to the primer. Then add a four-step routine the owner asked for ("a mental framework for everyone"): who are the players, what can each do, what does each gain or lose, who moves first and who can see what. Show one worked headline and one faded headline.

### G20 sherpa
Most important miss: the scorecard [14] picks nine promises and grades them "2 delivered, 4 partly, 2 not, 1 stalled", then concludes "half right" [15]. The list leaves out items that were delivered and that a sherpa would name first: the G20-backed $650 billion SDR allocation of 2021 (the lane mentions the 2009 and 2021 allocations, R7B line 64), the automatic exchange of tax account information that the G20 endorsed in 2014 (Malaysia takes part, to verify), central clearing of standard derivatives after 2009, and the 2010 IMF quota reform (delivered late, in 2016, to verify). "Global subsidies were still very large" has no number (the lane has "above $1 trillion in 2022, IEA, to verify").
Blocking: yes, because the verdict sentence depends on which promises were picked.
Fix: state the selection rule first (for example, every leaders' communiqué headline pledge from 2008 to 2026 with a measurable target), add the delivered items above with T0 or T1 sources, and give the subsidy number. The "fast in crises, slow in calm" base rate will probably survive, which makes it stronger.
