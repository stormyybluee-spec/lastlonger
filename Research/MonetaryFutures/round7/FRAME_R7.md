## 3. Answer spec for Q1: the L2.1 Bonds rewrite

An expert answer is complete only when every item below is present, sourced and tested. The court's completeness auditor marks each item present, partial or missing.

### 3.1 The reader and the reading level
- Reader: an 18-year-old with no finance background, possibly reading in a second language (many readers are in Malaysia). Also their parents, who hold EPF savings and loans.
- Levels 1 and 2 of the depth ladder: Flesch-Kincaid grade 8 to 9. Average sentence 15 words or fewer. At most one new term per paragraph, defined in plain words at first use. Every number shown in ringgit or dollars that a person can picture, before any percentage.
- Levels 3 and 4: grade 10 to 11 allowed, still one term per paragraph.
- The whole Level 1 path (headline sentence, the seesaw, the resale market in one picture, "your eight touch points", the gauge) must read on its own in about 10 minutes (about 1,500 words).
- Glossary with Malay equivalents where they help (bon, sukuk, KWSP for EPF, kadar faedah). The lane checks the Malay terms with a native speaker voice, never by guess.
- Readability is measured, not asserted: the court runs a readability count on the draft (a script in the scratchpad) and reports grade per part.

### 3.2 Teaching method: evidence grading of the "biohacking methods"
The chapter must use the techniques with strong evidence, name the popular ones that lack it, and say so in one short box for readers who search "biohacking learning". Lane B must source each grade from the primary reviews. The grades below are the framer's starting hypotheses, from memory, to verify.

|---|---|---|---|

| Spacing (coming back later) | Strong | Dunlosky 2013, Cepeda and others 2006 | "Come back in 3 days" prompts and a revisit card that links back from L2.4 |

Pass test for the method: every part of the chapter uses at least two strong techniques, no weak technique is presented as evidence-based, and the cold reader test (section 3.10) is run.

### 3.3 Core mechanism items (the machinery)
1. What a bond is: a loan you can sell. Face value, coupon, maturity, issuer. Government, company and sukuk versions, with the sukuk difference explained as profit or rent from an asset rather than interest (Malaysia's GII), checked against a Shariah-structure source.
2. The price and yield seesaw with one worked example in ringgit (for example a RM100 bond paying RM5 a year when new bonds start paying RM6), then the rule of thumb that a bond's price moves by about its duration times the change in yield, labelled as an approximation.
3. Duration explained physically (how long your money is locked to the old rate), with a table of price change for a 1 point rise in yields at 2, 10 and 30 years, computed in a script and labelled as a model.
4. Real yield: yield minus inflation, with Malaysia and US numbers for at least two regimes.
5. Holding to maturity versus selling early: a paper loss is real if you must sell, and is not lost if you hold a government bond that pays in full (default aside). This is where the "safe" claim is true and where it is not.
6. The yield curve in one picture and what an inverted curve has and has not predicted, with a base rate.
7. Credit risk and spreads: why companies and weaker governments pay more.
8. Own-currency versus foreign-currency bonds, as the bridge to L2.4.
9. Who buys at the auction and who holds afterwards (the government lens kept, but compressed to one part): for the US and for Malaysia, holder tables with shares and dates from T0 sources (US Treasury and Fed for the US, Bank Negara Malaysia for Malaysia, including EPF, banks, insurers, foreign holders and the central bank).
10. Ledger check, tied to Case 2: when a bond is bought by a bank, by EPF, by a foreign fund or by the central bank, which balance sheets change and whether public money rises, falls or only moves. One small table, both floors.

### 3.4 Secondary market items
1. Primary (auction) versus secondary (resale) market, in one picture.
2. How resale works: dealers quote a buy price and a sell price, the gap is their fee (bid-ask spread). Over the counter, not mostly on an exchange. Clean price and accrued interest explained in one line each.
3. Who trades: banks, pension funds (EPF, US pension funds), insurers, asset managers, foreign funds, central banks, hedge funds, and the dealers in the middle (US primary dealers, Malaysia's principal dealers appointed by Bank Negara, to verify the current list).
4. Why prices move day to day: expected policy rates, inflation news, supply from new auctions, foreign flows and the currency, flight to safety, forced sellers (UK pension funds in 2022, Silicon Valley Bank in 2023).
5. Liquidity: why a US Treasury can be sold in seconds and a small corporate bond may not sell at a fair price. The March 2020 "dash for cash" when even Treasuries froze, and the Fed's purchases.
6. How an ordinary person buys and sells:
   - US: TreasuryDirect (buy at auction, hold, transfer to a broker to sell before maturity, to verify the rules), a brokerage account buying in the secondary market, bond funds and ETFs, I bonds and savings bonds that cannot be resold.
   - Malaysia: what retail access exists (exchange-traded bonds and sukuk on Bursa Malaysia, retail bond offerings through banks, retail sukuk issues such as past government savings bonds, bond and sukuk unit trust funds, and EPF as the indirect route most people already use). Every route must be checked against Bank Negara, Securities Commission or Bursa pages, with minimum amounts and costs. Malaysians buying US Treasuries through a foreign broker: what the foreign exchange policy allows, to verify with Bank Negara.
   - A cost box: spread, fees, tax on interest in each country, and the currency cost for a Malaysian buying a US bond.
7. Pass: a reader can trace a bond from auction to their EPF statement, and knows the three cheapest legal routes to own bonds in their country.

### 3.5 The normal person's lens (must be a sizeable part, not a sidebar)
Every channel by which bonds reach an ordinary person, each with a mechanism line, a Malaysia number, a US number and a source. Minimum list:

| # | Touch point | Malaysia angle (to verify) | US angle (to verify) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Retirement savings | EPF is a large holder of MGS and GII. Share of EPF assets in fixed income and its dividend history | 401(k) and pension funds, target-date funds |
| 2 | Bank deposits | Banks hold government bonds as safe liquid assets. Deposit insurance by PIDM (limit per depositor per bank, to verify) | FDIC limit. Silicon Valley Bank 2023 as the case where bond losses sank a bank |
| 3 | Home loans | Most Malaysian home loans float with the bank's base rate, which follows the policy rate (OPR) more than bond yields | The 30-year fixed mortgage is priced off the 10-year Treasury yield |
| 4 | Car loans and other borrowing | Hire purchase in Malaysia is mostly fixed rate. PTPTN student loans | Auto loans, student loans, credit cards |
| 5 | Jobs and business | Company bond costs and bank lending rates. Government room to spend | Same |
| 6 | The currency | Foreign sales of MGS can weaken the ringgit (2013 taper tantrum, 2015, 2018, 2022, to verify with flows), and a weaker ringgit raises import prices | The dollar is the other side of that trade |
| 7 | Prices and inflation | Fixed coupons lose value when inflation rises. Real return on savings | Same, with 2021 to 2023 as the case |
| 8 | Taxes and public services | Government interest costs as a share of revenue, and what they squeeze (subsidies, schools, health) | Net interest above defence spending (to verify the year) |
| 9 | Insurance and takaful | Insurers and takaful operators hold bonds to pay future claims | Life insurers and annuities |
| 10 | Islamic savings | Tabung Haji and Islamic funds hold sukuk | Not applicable |

For each touch point the chapter answers: does a rise in bond yields help or hurt me, now and in 10 years? Young savers and borrowers get opposite answers, which is the most useful single insight for an 18-year-old.

### 3.6 Hidden risks a normal person carries
A table with the risk, how it reaches the person, a real episode and what (if anything) protects them: interest-rate risk in "safe" funds (2022 bond losses, to verify size), inflation risk, liquidity risk, credit or default risk (Malaysian corporate bond defaults, to verify cases), currency risk on foreign bonds, reinvestment risk when rates fall, concentration risk (one fund holding most of a nation's savings, and that fund holding much of the nation's debt), bank and sovereign linkage (banks holding their own government's bonds), fiscal risk (who pays when debt rises: taxes, inflation or cuts) and sukuk structure risk. Overcorrection guard: the same table names the risks bonds reduce (shares falling in a recession, deposit safety, pension income stability).

### 3.7 The personal gauge
A worksheet the reader runs once a month in about 10 minutes, with public dials and a self-check. Labelled as a learning tool, not advice. Minimum content:
- Public dials (each with where to look it up, free): the 10-year government yield (Malaysia and US) and its change over 12 months, the real yield (10-year minus inflation), the curve slope (10-year minus the policy rate), a credit spread, the foreign share of Malaysian government bonds and USD/MYR, and government interest cost as a share of revenue.
- Self-check: what share of my savings is in bonds (EPF counts), roughly how long their duration is, what currency they are in, which of my loans float and by how much my monthly payment moves if rates rise by 1 point (a worked example), and whether I would be a forced seller in a crisis.
- Three colours per dial with thresholds marked as a model with stated inputs, tested on three made-up households (an 18-year-old student, a 35-year-old with a home loan, a 60-year-old retiree drawing EPF).
- Pass: the gauge gives different answers for the three households, and every dial has a free public source.

### 3.8 The headline filter (rage-bait method)
A short checklist, possibly with a mnemonic (the one place a mnemonic is useful), that turns a bond headline into numbers. Minimum questions: compared with what (per GDP, per revenue, per person)? A level or a change? Nominal or real (nominal debt sets a "record" almost every year by arithmetic)? Who holds it (domestic or foreign, own or foreign currency)? Since when (base period chosen to look dramatic)? In basis points, how big is the "spike" and what does it do to a RM300,000 loan or a bond fund? Who gains from me believing this (the outlet, the speaker, a seller)? What is left out? Then three worked examples using real headlines, at least one from Malaysia and one from the US, and at least one alarming headline that turned out right. Overcorrection guard as in H5.

Deliverables scorecard for the G20 hypothesis (H8), each graded delivered, partial or not delivered, with T0 or T1 evidence: the 2008 to 2009 coordination (fiscal stimulus, IMF resources, SDR allocation, the Financial Stability Board, Basel III), the 2009 pledge on fossil fuel subsidies (a likely say/do gap), the 2014 growth target, BEPS and the 15% global minimum tax (and the 2025 G7 "side-by-side" carve-out for US companies, to verify), the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (anomaly check: $12.9 billion suspended, 48 of 73 eligible countries, one private creditor), the Common Framework (how many cases, how many years each took), and the split over Russia since 2022.
6. Base rate: clubs coordinate fast in crises and slowly in calm years. The answer must say which regime the owner's impression comes from.

### 4.3 Printing power: a ladder, not a yes or no
A matrix of 15 to 25 example countries scored on at least these tests, each from a primary source:
- A. Issues its own currency (legal power). Fails: euro members individually (France, Italy, and Bulgaria if it joined in 2026, to verify), dollarized economies (Ecuador, El Salvador, Panama), CFA franc users.
- B. Exchange-rate regime, de jure and de facto (IMF AREAER): free floating, floating, managed or other, pegs, currency boards (Hong Kong), no separate legal tender.
- C. Share of government debt owed in its own currency, and the share held by foreigners ("original sin", Eichengreen and Hausmann).
- D. Reserve-currency status (IMF COFER shares): the dollar, euro, yen, pound, renminbi and others.
- E. Capital controls and convertibility (China, Malaysia's offshore ringgit rules).
- F. Central bank independence and the law on direct lending to the government.
- G. Inflation record, the practical limit.
Then three levels of answer: can print by law, can pay its own debts by printing, can print without a currency or inflation crisis. Worked contrasts: the US (reserve issuer), Japan (very high debt, own currency, mostly domestic holders, the central bank owns a large share, to verify), Italy (high debt, no own printer, the ECB backstop through its transmission tool), Argentina or Sri Lanka or Lebanon (own currency but foreign-currency debt, default or collapse), Saudi Arabia and Hong Kong (pegs by choice), Ecuador (dollarized), Switzerland (prints to keep its currency down), Malaysia.
Tie to Case 2: "printing" here means the state and central bank creating the money its debts are owed in. Bank lending is the other printer and is covered in L2.2.

### 4.4 Malaysia as the outlier

Malaysia's other seats: ASEAN, ASEAN+3 and the Chiang Mai Initiative Multilateralisation, BRICS partner country since January 2025 (to verify), IMF member, BIS shareholder (to verify), Islamic Financial Services Board host.
2. Own currency, own central bank. Bank Negara's own words since 2005 are "managed float". Both are true and the chapter must explain the difference.
3. History: the 1997 to 1998 crisis, the September 1998 capital controls and the peg at 3.80 per dollar until July 2005 (to verify dates), the 2016 measures against offshore ringgit trading (to verify), and what the IMF said then versus later.
4. Who holds Malaysian government debt: foreign holders about a fifth (anomaly check: RM283.10 billion of RM1.309 trillion in March 2026, about 21.6%, denominator to verify), EPF, banks, insurers, Bank Negara. EPF's size relative to the economy and to the bond market.
5. The statutory debt limit and the share of debt in ringgit (to verify).
6. Rule-taker: Malaysia applies rules made in clubs it does not sit in (Basel III, the 15% global minimum tax from 2025, to verify). This is the strongest bridge to the game board.
7. Comparison with peers (Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, Chile, Poland) to test H9: where Malaysia is typical and where it is the outlier.

- Holding to maturity versus selling early (paper loss versus realized loss)
- Government versus corporate versus sukuk (Malaysia's GII is a large share)
- Own-currency versus foreign-currency bonds
- Fixed-rate versus floating-rate loans (Malaysian home loans float with the base rate, US mortgages are mostly fixed for 30 years)
- Direct holding versus indirect (most ordinary people hold bonds only through EPF, banks, insurers or funds)
- Young saver versus borrower versus retiree (opposite effects of the same yield change)
- Normal years versus stress years: 1998 and 2008 and 2013 and 2020 and 2022 for Malaysia, 2008 and 2020 and 2022 and 2023 for the US
- Upstairs versus downstairs (central bank buying bonds versus the public buying them, Case 2)
- Domestic versus foreign holders
- The US versus Malaysia market structure, retail access law and tax
- Individual risk versus system risk (one saver versus a pension fund forced to sell)
- Accurate alarm versus rage-bait (the filter must catch both kinds of error)
- Explanation quality versus true difficulty (some parts should be cut, not simplified into errors)

## 7. Proposed curiosity questions

### 7.1 For Q1 (10 proposed, the chapter may use 8 to 10)
Each one opens a part of the chapter and is asked before the answer (prediction before reveal).
1. You lend the government RM100. A month later new loans pay more. Why is your old loan now worth less, and when does that loss stop mattering?
2. Most of your EPF savings may be lent to the government. Is that safe, and safe from what?
3. Why did "safe" government bond funds lose so much in 2022 (size to verify), when most people think bonds cannot fall?
4. Who really sets your home loan rate: Bank Negara, the bond market or your bank? And why is the answer different in the US?
5. When foreign funds sell Malaysian bonds, why can your phone, your petrol or your holiday get more expensive?
6. A headline says "yields spike 30 basis points". How much is that in ringgit on a RM300,000 loan, and on a bond fund?
7. Can a government that issues its own money ever go broke? (Bridge to L2.4)
8. Why would anyone buy a bond that pays less than inflation?
9. When you sell a bond before it ends, who is on the other side, and why might they pay you less than you paid?
10. How can a bank fail just from owning government bonds, the "safest" asset there is?

### 7.2 For Q2 (8 proposed, the chapter uses 5)
Framer's recommended five are marked with a star. Each names its game.
1. (star) Why did the G20 move within months in 2008 to 2009, but take years on poor countries' debts? (Coordination under a shared threat versus a free-rider problem. Case: 2009 London summit versus the DSSI and Common Framework, where private creditors stayed out)
2. (star) If every country wants a cheaper currency to sell more abroad, what happens when they all try at once? (Prisoner's dilemma. Cases: the 1930s devaluations, the 1985 Plaza Accord as coordination, the 2026 US and Japan joint yen action from Case 1)
3. (star) Why does the world keep saving in US dollars even while complaining about the US? (Focal point and network effects. Cases: dollar share of reserves and trade invoicing, the Triffin dilemma, what has and has not moved since 2022)
4. (star) Why did Asian countries, Malaysia among them, pile up foreign reserves after 1998 instead of spending them? (Repeated game and self-insurance against the outside option of an IMF programme. Case: 1997 to 1998, Malaysia's capital controls versus Thailand's and Indonesia's IMF programmes, reserves since)
5. (star) Why can Japan owe more than twice its economy and borrow cheaply, while Sri Lanka defaulted? (Who holds the debt and in what currency decides who has the power. A bargaining game with outside options. Cases: Japan's domestic and central bank holders, Sri Lanka's 2022 default on foreign-currency debt)
6. Who blinks first when a country cannot pay: the borrower, the old lenders or China? (Chicken, holdouts. Cases: Zambia's restructuring, Argentina's holdout creditors)
7. Why does Malaysia follow rules made in clubs it is not part of, such as the 15% minimum tax and Basel III? (Rule-taker in a repeated game, reputation and market access)
8. When foreigners own a slice of your bonds, who has the power, you or them? ("If you owe the bank RM100 it is your problem, if you owe RM100 million it is the bank's problem". Case: foreign holdings of MGS and the ringgit in 2013 and 2022, to verify)

Pass test for the chosen five: together they cover at least four distinct game types, at least one involves Malaysia directly, and each has a T0 to T2 case and a dated tripwire.
