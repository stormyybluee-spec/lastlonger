## L2.1 Bonds: a ticket you can resell, and where it touches your life (id rb-bonds)

Kicker: A bond is a ticket you can resell: you pay RM100, it pays you RM4 a year, and you get RM100 back at the end. This chapter builds everything from that one ticket, then shows where bonds touch your own life, how to check your own exposure once a month, and how to weigh scary bond headlines before you feel them.

[0 tldr]
- A bond is a ticket. You pay RM100. It pays you RM4 every year. At the end you get your RM100 back.
- You can keep the ticket to the end, or resell it. Resale happens on the secondary market, through dealers.
- When new tickets pay more, old tickets are worth less. Rates up, prices down. If you hold to the end, the dip disappears.
- Bonds touch you even if you never buy one: your EPF, your bank, your loans, the ringgit, prices and taxes.
- The same rise in rates hurts some people and helps others. A young saver with decades ahead mostly gains.

### 2. A bond is a ticket

[11 p] Forget finance for a moment. The government sells you a ticket for RM100. The ticket says three things. It pays you RM4 every year. It ends on a fixed date, say in ten years. On that date the government buys it back from you for RM100.

[12 diagram] rm-ticket: One ticket carries the whole chapter. You lend RM100, collect RM4 a year, and get RM100 back at the end.

[13 p] That ticket is a bond. You lent the government RM100. The RM4 a year is your reward for waiting. A company can sell the same kind of ticket. So can a state, or a bank.

[14 cards] Five words to start with
- Bond: The ticket: a promise to pay you a fixed amount each year and your money back on a set date.
- Face value: The amount paid back at the end. RM100 in our ticket.
- Coupon: The fixed yearly payment. RM4 in our ticket, or 4%.
- Maturity: The end date, when the ticket is bought back.
- Yield: What the ticket really earns you per year at the price you pay today.

[15 insider] (Malaysia note) About half of Malaysia's government paper is sukuk (GII). A sukuk gives you a share in an asset or its rent, not a loan. The ticket picture still works for price and resale. Only the word loan does not fit.


### 3. The seesaw: why your ticket's price moves

[17 p] A month after you buy, the government sells new tickets that pay RM5 a year. Now try to resell yours. Nobody will pay RM100 for a ticket paying RM4 when a new one pays RM5. So your price drops until a buyer earns about the same as on a new ticket.

[18 steps]
- Worked example. Your ticket pays RM4, has 10 years left, and new tickets pay RM5. A buyer will pay about RM92.28. They get RM4 a year plus a RM7.72 gain at the end, which works out to about 5% a year.
- Your turn, half done. New tickets pay RM3 instead. Is your RM4 ticket worth more or less than RM100? (It is worth more, about RM108.53, because it pays more than the new ones.)
- Your turn. A ticket with only 2 years left, when new tickets pay RM5. Does it fall more or less than the 10-year ticket? (Much less, about 1.9%, because only two years of too little pay are left.)

[19 diagram] seesaw: Rates and prices sit on a seesaw. When new tickets pay more, old tickets are worth less, and the other way round.

[20 widget] bond-lab: Try it. Three bonds, all bought for $1,000 and all paying $50 a year. Drag the rate new bonds pay and watch their prices. The black line is the $1,000 you paid.

[21 p] Two rules fall out of the seesaw. Rates up, prices down. And the longer the ticket, the bigger the swing: a 30-year ticket falls about 15% when new tickets pay one point more, a 2-year ticket about 2%. These figures are a model worked out for this chapter.

[22 myth] Myth: If a bond's price falls, the government must be in trouble.
Reality: Usually it only means new tickets pay more. The government still owes you every RM4 and your RM100. The price matters only if you sell before the end.

[23 curiosity]
- Q: You pay RM100 for a 10-year ticket paying RM4. Next month new tickets pay RM5. What can you sell yours for, and when does that loss stop mattering?
  A: About RM92.28 (model). If you hold to the end, you still get every RM4 and your RM100 back, so the dip only matters if you sell early. (builds on rb-01)


### 4. The resale stall: the secondary market

[25 p] There are two markets. The first sale happens at a ticket booth: the government's auction. Money goes to the government. Every sale after that happens at a resale stall, the secondary market. One investor sells to another, usually through a dealer. The government gets nothing from these resales. It still watches the price closely, because the resale price tells it what its next auction will cost.

[26 diagram] resale-stall: The booth sells new tickets. The resale stall is where old tickets change hands, and the dealer earns the gap between its two prices.

[27 steps]
- You want to sell. You contact a dealer, usually a bank's trading desk or a broker.
- The dealer shows two prices. It buys at RM99.50 and sells at RM99.60. The gap is its fee for always being ready. On RM10,000 of tickets, buying and selling costs you about RM10.
- Most bond trades happen between dealers and clients, not on a public screen like shares. You usually see a quote, not the whole market.
- The buyer also pays you the interest you earned since the last coupon. Then the ticket and the cash swap accounts, normally within a day or two.

[29 p] The gap between the two prices is the price of being able to leave. It is tiny in calm years. It jumps exactly when everyone wants out. In March 2020 even the safest US bonds were hard to sell, and the Fed bought $1.27 trillion of them in five weeks to calm the market.

### 6. Twelve places bonds touch you

[43 p] When people say yields are rising, they usually mean the 10-year government bond. Here is what that does to a normal person, place by place.

[44 diagram] touch-map: Each box is a place in your life where bonds reach you. The colour says whether a rise in yields tends to help, hurt, do both, or change little.

[45 table]
| Where | How it works | If yields rise |
|---|---|---|
| Your EPF | It holds a giant drawer of government bonds for you | Old bonds dip on paper. New money earns more. Young members gain over time |
| Bank deposits | Banks hold bonds as safe, sellable assets | Little change for you. PIDM insures RM250,000 per depositor per bank |
| Fixed deposits | FD rates follow the OPR (2.75%), not the 10-year | Help, if the OPR rises too |
| Home loan in Malaysia | Since August 2022 floating loans follow a base rate that moves only with the OPR | Only through the OPR. One 0.25 point step adds about RM60 a month on RM400,000 over 35 years |
| Home loan in the US | 30-year fixed rates follow the 10-year bond | Hurt new buyers. Existing fixed loans are protected |
| Car loans | Mostly fixed when you sign | Little change |
| Your job | Companies borrow at the government rate plus a margin | Hurt slowly if high rates last: fewer projects, fewer jobs |
| The ringgit | Foreigners hold about a fifth of Malaysian government bonds | Can weaken, raising import prices. Not automatic: in 2026 it held near 4.08 |
| Prices | A fixed RM4 buys less when prices rise | Rising inflation hurts holders of fixed coupons |
| Taxes and services | Interest on government debt competes with schools and hospitals | Hurt slowly as old debt is refinanced. Malaysia's debt service is about 16% of revenue |
| Insurance and takaful | Insurers hold bonds to pay future claims | Mixed: new policies price better, old bonds dip |
| Bond funds | They hold many tickets and sell units daily | Fall now, earn more later |

[46 insider] (The one idea to keep) The same rise in yields hurts people who hold long bonds or must borrow today, and helps people who will save for years. An 18-year-old paying into EPF is mostly on the winning side over a working life, even on days the headlines call it a crash.

[47 curiosity]
- Q: Does the 10-year bond set your Malaysian home loan rate?
  A: No. Since 1 August 2022 new floating home loans follow the Standardised Base Rate, which moves only with the OPR. In the US, the 30-year fixed mortgage does track the 10-year Treasury. (builds on rb-06)
- Q: When US yields jump, does the ringgit always fall?
  A: No. In 2022 the US 10-year rose from 1.52% to 3.88% and the ringgit fell about 12%. In 2026 the 10-year rose 1.12 points and the ringgit stayed between 3.89 and 4.15 (Fed data). (builds on rb-04)


### 7. What makes new tickets pay more or less

[49 p] Why would new tickets suddenly pay RM5 instead of RM4? Think of a tug of war. The central bank's own rate, expected inflation, how many tickets the government must sell, how many buyers turn up, how fast the economy grows, and how much people trust the borrower all pull on the price of a new ticket.

[50 diagram] yield-tug: Seven forces pull on the yield of a new ticket.

[51 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The seven forces in detail, and 2026 as an example
[51.0 table]
| Force | What it means | Which way it pushes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The Fed's rate path | Short yields track where the Fed sets overnight rates now and where markets expect it to go | Expected hikes push yields up, expected cuts pull them down |
| 2. Expected inflation | Lenders want compensation for prices rising while they wait | More expected inflation means higher yields |
| 3. Term premium | Extra pay for locking money up for years when the future is uncertain | More uncertainty about deficits, inflation or policy pushes long yields up |
| 4. Supply | How much the Treasury must borrow and in what lengths | Bigger deficits and more long bonds push yields up |
| 5. Demand | Who must or wants to buy: foreign central banks, banks, money funds, pensions, stablecoins | Captive or eager buyers pull yields down, a big seller pushes them up |
| 6. Growth | A strong economy competes for money, a weak one frees it up | Booms raise yields, recessions lower them |
| 7. Trust | Confidence in repayment and in the value of the currency repaid | Any doubt raises yields, a crisis elsewhere can lower them as money flees to safety |

[51.1 diagram] yield-parts: Split the 10-year yield in two. The gold part pays you back for expected inflation. The blue part is the real reward for waiting.

[51.2 p] You can see several forces at once in 2026. The Fed hiked in September (force 1). Oil prices kept inflation worries alive, and the bond market expected about 2.4% a year of inflation over ten years (force 2). A $1.99T deficit meant heavy issuance (force 4). Japan's recorded holdings of Treasuries fell $171B from February to August while it spent a similar sum defending the yen (force 5). Meanwhile French stress pushed some European money into Treasuries for safety (force 7 in reverse). The result: a 10-year yield of 5.13% on 21 October 2026, after a peak of 5.31% on 5 October.

### 8. Six hidden risks, and what protects you

[53 table]

|---|---|---|---|
| Price risk | Your bond or bond fund falls when yields rise | The main US bond index lost 13.01% in 2022, its worst year since 1976 | Holding to the end. Shorter bonds swing less |

[55 p] So bonds are not simply safe, and not simply dangerous. They protected savers in 2008 and hurt them in 2022. Higher yields today also mean higher income tomorrow: in October 2026 a 10-year US inflation-linked bond paid about 2.6 points above inflation, against about minus 1 point at the end of 2021.

[56 curiosity]
- Q: Why did safe US bond funds lose 13% in 2022?
  A: Inflation pushed yields up fast, so old tickets were repriced down. The main US bond index returned -13.01%, its worst year since 1976. The previous worst was -2.92% in 1994. (builds on rb-08)
- Q: In 2008 shares crashed. What did bonds do?
  A: The main US bond index rose 5.24% while the S&P 500 fell 37.0%. Bonds cushion recessions. They failed to in 2022 because the shock was inflation. (builds on rb-buckets)


### The real return: what is left after tax and inflation

[58 p] Every yield you see quoted, 5.13% on the 10-year or 4.81% on the 2-year (21 October 2026), is nominal. It counts dollars, not what dollars buy. Level 1's "The yardstick problem" called this the rubber ruler. Your real return is what is left after inflation and, for most savers, after tax. The market publishes its own split: on 21 October 2026 the 10-year inflation-linked Treasury yielded 2.76%, so about 2.4 points of the 5.13% was pay for expected inflation.

[59 steps]
- Start with the nominal yield: 5.13% on a 10-year Treasury in October 2026, or $51.30 a year on $1,000.
- Take out tax. In a 24% federal bracket about $39 is left. Treasury interest is free of state income tax, but not of federal tax.
- Take out inflation. If prices rise 2.4% a year, about what the bond market expects, $24 of that $39 only keeps up with prices. Your real gain is about 1.5% a year.
- If inflation turns out higher than expected, the real return drops below zero, even though every payment arrives on time.

[61.2 p] Two models, both using the Fed's H.15 daily closes. The first column reprices a 10-year bond as if the whole jump in yields hit it at once. The second follows a real holder who bought a new 10-year at par at the low, still owned it at the high with fewer years left, and collected the coupons along the way (coupons not reinvested, computed for this chapter). Notice the one gain: in 2008 Treasuries did their job as everything else fell. Notice too that 2022 was different. Stocks and bonds fell together, which broke the old idea that one always rescues the other.

[61.3 p] How can a bank fail holding bonds that never missed a payment? Walk through a bank shaped like Silicon Valley Bank.

[61.4 table]
| Step | The bank owns | The bank owes |
|---|---|---|
| 2021: deposits flood in | $100 of 10-year bonds bought at 1.5%, plus $8 of cash and loans | $100 of deposits, and $8 of owners' money |
| 2022: yields rise to 4.5% | The bonds are now worth about $78, plus the $8 | Still $100 of deposits. Marked to market, owners' money is -$14 |
| 2023: depositors leave | Bonds must be sold to pay them | Many deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit, so they left fast. The hidden loss becomes real. Owners' money is gone |

[61.5 p] Accounting rules let the loss stay hidden while the bank promised to hold the bonds to the end. A run forced a sale, and the paper loss became real in a day. The bonds were never in default. SVB's held-to-maturity losses were about $15.1B, close to all of its owners' money. Two days after SVB closed, the Fed opened the Bank Term Funding Program, which lent to banks against Treasuries at their face value, not their market value. A bond's price can fall, but in that panic its value as collateral was held at par by policy. That is strong evidence for this chapter's title.

[61.6 curiosity]
- Q: How much could a risk-free 10-year Treasury lose?
  A: On price alone, about 32% for someone who bought a new 10-year at 8.76% in July 1979 and still held it in September 1981, when yields hit 15.84%. Counting the coupons collected, the loss was about 14%. From 2021 to 2023 a holder lost about 24% on price and about 21% with coupons (models, Fed H.15 data). Risk-free means free of default, not free of price swings. (builds on rb-13)
- Q: Someone bought a 10-year Treasury at 0.625% in 2020. What is it worth now, and did they lose money?
  A: About $84 per $100 at today's yields with four years left (model). They lose nothing in dollars if they hold to 2030, but they lose buying power: inflation peaked at 9.1% in 2022. (builds on rb-10)
- Q: A bank failed in 2023 holding bonds that never missed a payment. How?
  A: Its bonds had lost value as yields rose, about $15.1B at SVB, close to its owners' money, and US banks together carried $620B of such losses at the end of 2022 (FDIC). When depositors ran, the paper loss became real. (builds on rb-07)

[62 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): How governments quietly shrink debt with inflation
[62.0 p] This is not theory. In the 1940s the Fed held long-term yields near 2.5% while inflation ran in double digits after the war, quietly shrinking the war debt in real terms. In the 1970s inflation often ran above bond yields. And anyone who bought a 10-year Treasury at its record low closing yield of 0.52% in August 2020 earned about half a percent a year, while US consumer inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022.

[62.1 insider] This is the quietest way governments shrink debt. Keep the nominal yield below inflation for long enough and the real value of the debt melts, paid for by bondholders and savers who only look at the nominal number. Level 1's "The seasons of money" called it financial repression. The cure is the split in step 3: always separate a yield into the part that only keeps up with inflation and the part that is truly yours.

### 11. The government's side, in one page

[81 p] Why do governments sell tickets at all? Because they spend more than they collect in taxes, and because a safe ticket is useful to everyone else: banks, pension funds and central banks all need something safe to hold. Each new ticket is sold at an auction, where dealers must bid so that every sale is covered. The resale price then works like a vote that never stops. If buyers doubt a government's plans, they pay less for its tickets, yields rise, and the government's own interest bill grows.

[82 p] That bill is already large. Malaysia's debt service was RM57.3 billion in 2026, about 15.8% of revenue. In the US, net interest passed $1 trillion in fiscal 2026, more than defense.

[84 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Why the whole system rests on bonds (the backbone test)
[84.0 diagram] bond-tower: Picture a champagne tower at a wedding. Government bonds are the bottom row of glasses. Every glass above rests on them.

[84.1 list]
- The Fed's money rests on them. The Fed creates bank reserves and cash, and the main asset it holds against them is Treasuries: $4.57T of its $6.47T of securities, about 71% (21 October 2026).
- They are the financial system's pawnshop ticket. In repo markets, banks and funds hand over Treasuries to borrow cash, often overnight. About $12.6T of US repo was outstanding on an average day in the third quarter of 2025 (Office of Financial Research).
- Banks and money funds park cash in them, because rules treat them as the safest liquid asset.
- Stablecoins must hold them. Under the GENIUS Act, compliant dollar tokens must be backed by cash-like reserves: deposits, central bank money, government money funds, repo, or Treasuries with 93 days or less left. Short bills are the dominant choice.
- Foreign central banks keep their savings in them. That is how the world holds dollars without holding cash.
- They set the base price of money. Mortgages, car loans and company loans are priced as the Treasury yield plus a margin for risk.

[87 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Repo: how a bond is pawned overnight
[87.0 p] Repo is a loan secured by a bond. You sell a bond for cash today and agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The difference is the interest. The lender keeps a cushion called a haircut, for example lending $98 against $100 of bonds. Here a hedge fund buys a $100 Treasury and funds it in repo through a dealer, and a money market fund supplies the cash.

[87.1 table]
| Who | What they now own | What they now owe | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hedge fund | $100 Treasury (pledged) | $98 repo loan, plus $2 of its own money | It holds a $100 bond with only $2 of its own money. In practice this is usually one leg of a hedged trade (the cash-futures basis trade), not a bare bet |
| Dealer (a bank's arm) | $98 loan to the fund | $98 borrowed from the money fund | It is the middleman and nets the two |
| Money market fund | $98 repo loan, safe and overnight | nothing new | It swapped a bank deposit for a loan backed by a Treasury |
| The two floors | Reserves move between banks | Deposits move from the fund to the bond's seller | Public money only moved. Nothing was created |

[87.2 p] The US repo market had about $12.6T of loans outstanding on an average day in the third quarter of 2025 (Office of Financial Research). That is why a Treasury is worth more than its coupons: it can be turned into cash overnight at a small haircut. It is also where the system breaks. When haircuts jump or dealers run out of room, funds must sell bonds, prices fall, haircuts rise again. That loop drove March 2020.

- Q: Why is a Treasury worth more than the interest it pays?
  A: Because it is a pawnshop ticket. About $12.6T was outstanding in US repo on an average day in late 2025, mostly against Treasuries. Being able to turn a bond into cash overnight is worth something, so investors accept a slightly lower yield for it. (builds on rb-01)

[88 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Duration and convexity: the bond's lever
[88.0 p] Duration is the number that tells you how hard a bond swings. Roughly, a bond's price moves by its duration, in percent, for each one point move in yields, the other way. [89 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The yield curve, and the bond market as a voter
[89.0 diagram] bond-ripple: One move in the 10-year yield, four ripples.

[89.1 p] Take a $400,000 mortgage over 30 years. At 6.40% the monthly payment is about $2,502. At 7.40%, one point higher, it is about $2,770. One point on the bond market costs that family about $267 a month, about $3,210 a year, for thirty years.

[89.2 diagram] yield-curve: The yield curve: yields lined up from short to long. Normal curves slope up. An inverted curve, drawn here as an example, often warns of a slowdown.

[89.3 p] Line up yields from short to long and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes up, because waiting longer deserves more pay. In October 2026 it does: about 4.81% at 2 years, 5.13% at 10 years and 5.42% at 30 years. When short yields rise above long ones (an inverted curve), markets are betting the Fed will have to cut, usually because a slowdown is coming. Inversions have come before many US recessions, though the timing is loose. The longest inversion of the 2s and 10s on record, 541 trading days from April 2022 to September 2024, has not been followed by a recession so far, and by 22 October 2026 the curve sloped up again by 0.31 points.

[89.4 myth] Myth: Government bonds are safe, so you cannot lose money on them.
Reality: They are safe from default, not from price changes. In 2022 bonds had one of their worst years on record. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank failed largely because the long Treasuries and mortgage bonds it held had fallen in price as rates rose. Safe means the promise will be kept, not that the price will hold.

[92.0 list]

### Part 2 of 3. Who can really print money


### 3. Three locks on the printing press

[23 p] Here printing means a state and its central bank creating the money that the state's debts are owed in. Banks are the other printer, which Level 2 chapter 2 covers. To print freely, a country must open three locks.

[24 steps]
- Lock 1, the law. Does it issue its own currency through its own central bank? Italy, Ecuador and Panama do not. Their money is the euro or the US dollar, made by someone else.
- Lock 2, the debt. Does the state owe its debt in the money it can create? A state that owes dollars cannot print dollars. Sri Lanka could print rupees, but it owed dollars, and stopped paying on 12 April 2022.
- Lock 3, the market. Can it print a lot in a crisis without its currency crashing or prices taking off? That depends on whether the world wants to hold its money, how open its markets are, and its record on inflation. Turkey and Argentina had their own money and owed much of their debt at home, and still saw inflation reach 72% (2022) and 220% (2024).

[25 p] Each lock is needed and none is enough on its own. A country that passes lock 1 can always pay a debt in its own money. It cannot choose what that money will buy.


### 4. The ladder

[27 diagram] print-ladder: Six rungs of printing power. Malaysia sits on rung 3, highlighted, near the top of it.

[28 table]
| Rung | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Reserve issuer | Opens all three locks with room. In a crisis the world buys its money | US by far, then the euro area as a whole, Japan, the UK |
| 2. Sovereign printer | Opens all three, but the world holds little of its money. Printing costs some inflation and a weaker currency | Canada, Australia, Switzerland, Korea |

### 6. Game theory in five minutes

[48 p] Game theory asks a simple question: what is each player's best move, given what the others might do? Six shapes of game explain a lot of what countries do.

[49 table]
| Game | The shape | Payoff in one line |
|---|---|---|
| Coordination | Everyone gains most if all act. Acting alone is costly | Fear decides whether all act or none |
| Prisoner's dilemma | Each gains by cheating whatever the other does | So both cheat and both lose |
| Free rider | A shared good is paid for by some and enjoyed by all | Each small player's best move is to let others pay |
| Focal point | Many choices would work, so players pick the one everyone expects | The habit wins, even if others are as good |
| Chicken | Two players drive at each other | Each tries to look more committed so the other swerves |
| Run | Lenders who expect others to stay, stay. Those who expect others to flee, flee first | The same borrower can be safe or bust depending on belief |

[50 callout] (What game theory leaves out) Domestic politics (a leader facing an election may choose the worse national outcome), ideology, accidents and plain error. Use the games as a lens on incentives, not as proof that every move was planned.


### 7. Five questions that open the game board

[52 p] Guess each answer before you open it. Each one opens a game, and each game is a real case.

[53 curiosity]
- Q: Why did the G20 move within months in 2008 to 2009, but take years on poor countries' debts?
  A: Two different games. In 2008 every member faced the same collapse, so acting together was each one's best move: $1.1 trillion and a $5 trillion spending push in one statement (coordination). Debt relief is a free-rider game: relief given by one lender frees cash to pay the others. The 2020 pause covered $12.9 billion, but only one private lender joined, and Ethiopia's deal took more than five years. (builds on rb-leader)
- Q: If every country wants a cheaper currency or a tariff edge, what happens when they all try at once?
  A: A prisoner's dilemma: each gains alone, all lose together, as in the 1930s. In 2010 Brazil's finance minister said the world was in an international currency war. The exits have been deals like the 1985 Plaza Accord, or truces like the October 2025 US and China pause, which can lapse. When two giants face off with threats, it becomes chicken. (builds on rb-leader)
- Q: Why does the world keep saving in US dollars even while complaining about the US?
  A: A focal point. Money is useful because others use it. The dollar was still 56.7% of world reserves in mid 2026, nearly three times the euro, while the Chinese yuan was 2.1% because China keeps capital controls. Switching only pays if many switch at once. (builds on rb-04)
- Q: Why did Malaysia and its neighbours pile up dollars after 1998 instead of spending them?
  A: Self-insurance. In 1997 to 1998 the ringgit fell from about 2.50 to about 4.88, and Thailand, Indonesia and Korea had to accept IMF conditions. Malaysia chose controls and a peg instead. Its reserves rose from $21.5 billion in 1997 to $132.6 billion in June 2026. Insurance you hold yourself is expensive, but nobody can attach conditions to it. (builds on rb-em)
- Q: Why can Japan owe about twice its economy and borrow cheaply, while Sri Lanka defaulted, and where does Malaysia sit?
  A: The run game: who holds the debt, and in which money, decides it. Japan owes yen mostly to itself, with its central bank holding about 41% and foreigners about 13%. Sri Lanka owed dollars to foreigners and stopped paying on 12 April 2022. Malaysia owes ringgit, 98.8% at home, but a fifth is held by foreigners, so its risk is a weaker ringgit, not default. (builds on rb-worldbs)

[56 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Three balance-sheet walks: the dollar privilege, defending the ringgit, a euro member borrowing
[56.0 p] The dollar privilege. A US household buys Malaysian-made chips. The exporter sells the dollars to its bank, which sells them to Bank Negara, which buys US Treasury bills. No dollars were created abroad. Dollars moved from a US household to a foreign central bank, which lent them back to the US government. The US got goods for IOUs in its own money. Bank Negara created ringgit to buy the dollars, and usually soaks them back up later.

[56.1 p] Defending the ringgit. A foreign fund sells Malaysian bonds and wants dollars. Bank Negara sells dollars from its reserves and receives ringgit, so banks' ringgit reserves fall. It then usually tops them back up to keep its interest rate at 2.75%. The cost is dollar reserves, which is why the 0.9 times ratio matters. A defence that lasts too long runs reserves down, as in 1997 to 1998.

[56.2 p] A euro member borrowing. Italy spends more than it taxes. It must sell bonds to banks, funds, households and foreigners. The ECB can create euros and buy Italian bonds on the resale market, but only by a decision taken for the whole euro area. In a panic, deposits can move to banks in another euro country, as in Greece, where about EUR 41 billion left before the June 2015 controls.
