## Evidence for and against the owner's thesis

Thesis part 1: no credit, no abundance.
FOR:
- Bank lending to GDP in advanced economies rose from about 30 percent (1945) to about 120 percent (2008), and income per person is far above 1820 levels (about three quarters of people in extreme poverty then, about 8 percent now).
- The Interstate case: debt-financed public capital gave a large one-time productivity boost.
- Bank of England was founded to lend to the state (1694), and Britain paid for a world war on debt (over 200 percent of GDP in 1815) and then grew.
- Colonial paper money, per the Cleveland Fed, mostly gave a stable medium that helped commerce.
AGAINST:
- Britain's income per person doubled 1270 to 1700 and again 1700 to 1870 with little bank credit at first. Growth was 1.3 percent a year under the gold standard.
- Growth per person was fastest in 1951 to 1973 (3.5 percent) when private credit to GDP was low, and slower after 1973 (2.1 percent) as credit rose. Credit to GDP rose mainly after the mid 1980s.
- Latin America 1980s: debt grew from 29 to 327 billion dollars and income per person fell.
- Spain had a huge inflow of money and still defaulted four times under Philip II.

Thesis part 2: scarce money means no plenty, so peasants were poor.
FOR:
- Bullion famines of the 1370s to 1470s closed mints, and England had about a tenth of its earlier silver (Spufford estimate).
- Small change was short for centuries (Sargent and Velde), and shortages hurt trade.
- Colonial America had coin drain, and paper helped.
AGAINST:
- Spain's silver (about 210 million pesos in the 1500s) raised prices 3 to 4 times, not output, and Spain did not become rich.
- Post Black Death wages rose when workers were scarce, with no rise in coin per head.
- Mansa Musa's gold cut the price of gold in Cairo for years and made nobody there richer.
- Farm work was 68 percent of English jobs in 1522, and Clark finds output and technology were static 1250 to 1600, moved by population. Output scarcity is the main cause.

Thesis part 3: about 80 percent could not compound a store of value, so wages made no dent.
FOR: Moatsos puts about 75 percent in extreme poverty in 1820 (Bourguignon and Morrisson 84 percent).
AGAINST: English real wages gained by more than GDP per head in the golden age after 1348. Allen says B&M overstate poverty for rich countries. Medieval rates of 20 to 33 percent in Mesopotamia do show that compounding was possible for the few, but the cost to a small farmer was high.

Thesis part 4: after 1971, credit is the route to self-sovereignty, with requirements.
FOR: US total credit rose from about 133 to 251 percent of GDP. Mortgages are about 70 percent of US household debt and about 60 percent of bank loans in 17 economies, so most credit sits against an asset.
AGAINST: Credit was not born in 1971. Credit is millennia old (about 2400 BCE), deposits passed notes in the US by 1860. Malaysia household debt rose to 84 percent of GDP (peak 93 percent in 2020), which shows the burden side. Foreign holders own about 31 percent of US public debt, so some of the claim goes to outsiders.
