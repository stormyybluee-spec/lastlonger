## L3.2 Money from the future: seven harder questions about credit (id rb-credit)

Kicker: The last chapter put printing on trial and found a toll, not a scam. Now the defence makes a bigger claim: that money made from nothing is the reason ordinary people are no longer peasants. Seven hard questions test that claim, and the epilogue grades it line by line.

[0 tldr]
- Credit is a promise that lets you build today and pay from tomorrow's income. It is thousands of years old. It was not born in 1971. What 1971 did was remove the last gold limit on it.
- On most evidence, the peasants of old kingdoms were poor mostly because output was scarce, not because coins were scarce. Spain shipped home mountains of silver in the 1500s and got higher prices, not lasting riches.
- The law leans toward money made from nothing for three reasons: the state gains, the banks gain, and when Swiss voters were asked in 2018, they kept it. Insiders helped write the rules, and elected lawmakers passed them.
- Credit that builds something makes plenty. Credit that only bids up old houses, or pays for spending, does not. And credit has a gate: the less you have, the harder it is to pass, and loans with less security cost more.
- So credit is a lever, not a gift. It can make a person free or owned. The difference is what the loan buys, what it costs, and whether the borrower can live through a bad year.

[1 bridge] Built on earlier chapters: recall these before you start
- rb-print: Printing is not a scam in the plain sense. It is a toll, paid most by cash holders, renters and late savers. Hard money has its own toll, on borrowers and the young.
- rb-moneymake: Most money is born when a bank makes a loan. The loan creates a new deposit that did not exist before.
- rb-07: Interest can be paid if money keeps moving. Trouble starts when debt grows faster than income.
- rb-02: Rulers watered down coins long before paper money. Hard money protects savers and squeezes borrowers.

[2 callout] (How to read this chapter) Seven questions and an epilogue, about 40 minutes in all. Each question ends with a short line on what it does to the claim. Read the main path first and skip the boxes marked Go deeper.


### The defence makes a bigger claim

[4 p] In the last chapter, money stood in the dock and the jury found a toll, not a scam. The defence now goes further. It says money made from nothing is the reason most people alive today are not peasants.

[5 p] The claim came from a reader of this site, and it has four parts. Here it is in plain words.

[6 steps]
- Part 1. Without credit there is no abundance. Credit is a token we borrow from our own future.
- Part 2. If money is kept scarce, it can never be plentiful for ordinary people. That is why kingdoms and empires had so many poor peasants for so many centuries.
- Part 3. About 80% of people in those centuries had no way to save and grow their savings. Money was so rare that wages could not fund any real growth.
- Part 4. So the system after 1971, when President Nixon cut the dollar's last link to gold, pushes everyone toward standing on their own feet through credit. And credit has requirements.

[7 p] It is a serious claim, and parts of it are right. A fair court does not accept a claim because it sounds good. It cross-examines. The first three questions were asked in the last chapter. Here are questions 4 to 10.


### Question 4. Were the peasants poor because money was scarce, or because output was scarce?

[9 p] Start with what money is. Money is a claim ticket on the things people make. If a village bakes 100 loaves, printing more tickets does not bake loaf 101. It only changes how many tickets each loaf costs. So the question is which was missing in the old kingdoms: tickets, or loaves?

[10 table]
| Evidence | What happened | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Spain's silver | Hamilton counted about 210 million pesos of silver landed at Seville from the Americas in the 1500s. By one estimate, prices in Western Europe rose 3 to 4 times in that century. The Spanish king still failed to pay his debts four times, in 1557, 1560, 1575 and 1596 | A silver flood, higher prices, and no lasting riches. Why is still argued |
| The king's gold in Cairo | In 1324 Mansa Musa, king of Mali, spent so much gold in Cairo that the price of gold there fell for about twelve years, says the writer al-Umari | A flood of gold made gold itself cheaper there |
| After the plague | The Black Death of 1348 killed many of England's workers. By most estimates, the real day wages of those who lived rose, because workers were scarce | Pay rose with output per worker. But coins per person may have risen too, so this case cannot settle it alone |
| Who worked where | In 1522 about 68% of English working men farmed, by one count (another says 76%) | Most men worked the land, and output per person grew only about 0.2% a year from 1270 to 1700 |
| The coin famines | From the 1370s to the 1470s Europe ran short of silver. Mints in Ghent and Bruges stopped making coins. Small coins for daily trade were short in England, France and Florence for centuries | Scarce coins did hurt trade, for real |

[11 p] So both things were true, but not equally. On the evidence here, the bigger cause of old poverty was scarce output. A bad harvest meant hunger no matter how many coins the king had. Scarce coins made trade harder at times. People also got round the shortage with credit: in early modern England, the historian Craig Muldrew found, most everyday buying and selling ran on credit between households. Our World in Data puts extreme poverty at about three quarters of the world in 1820 and about 8% today, on different poverty lines. As output grew, money had to stretch to keep up, which is the next question.

[12 insider] (What question 4 does to the claim) Part 2 of the claim holds only in part. Scarce money did choke trade at times. But the peasants were poor mainly because there was little to buy, not because there was little to buy it with. Spain suggests, though historians still argue, that more money alone does not make plenty.


### Question 5. Was credit born with Nixon in 1971?

[14 p] No. Credit is far older than paper money, and older than coins.

[15 table]
| When | What existed |
|---|---|
| About 4,400 years ago | One of the oldest written records of a loan, in Mesopotamia. Later, Old Babylonian law set interest at one third on barley loans and usually 20% on silver loans |
| 1694 | The Bank of England was founded to lend 1.2 million pounds to the Crown at about 8% interest |
| 1815 | Britain's government debt was over 200% of its yearly income after the wars with Napoleon. It fell to about 30% by the early 1900s, while Britain led the industrial age |
| By 1860 | In America, bank deposits were already a bigger part of the money supply than bank notes. This was more than a century before 1971 |
| 1971 | President Nixon and fifteen advisers met at Camp David and ended the official trading of dollars for gold. It was announced on television on 15 August. It was the president's decision |

[16 p] So what did 1971 change? It removed the last outside limit. Before it, gold put a ceiling somewhere above the system. After it, no metal limited credit, only laws, central banks and the judgment of lenders and borrowers.

[17 p] Did plenty arrive with it? Look at growth in income per person in the rich industrial countries.

[18 table]
| Period | The money rule | Growth per person, a year |
|---|---|---|
| 1870 to 1913 | Gold standard | 1.3% |
| 1951 to 1973 | Dollar tied to gold for central banks | 3.5% |
| 1974 to 1989 | Paper money, no gold link | 2.1% |

[19 p] The fastest growth came before 1971. There is no clean figure here for bank lending then. In the rich countries it was about 30% of yearly output (GDP) in 1945 and about 120% just before 2008. In America, total credit to households, firms and government rose from about 133% of GDP in 1971 to about 251% in 2024.

[20 insider] (What question 5 does to the claim) Part 4 of the claim needs a new date. Credit did not start in 1971. Societies have borrowed from the future for four thousand years. What 1971 did was take off the last metal brake. Since then American credit has nearly doubled against GDP, and rich-world growth in 1974 to 1989 was slower than before, not faster. This table cannot prove that credit slowed growth. It shows only that the end of gold brought no speed-up in rich countries.


### Question 6. Can a society really borrow from the future?

[22 p] Here is the rebuttal most people give: borrowing does not create anything. It just steals from our children, who will have to pay it back.

[23 p] Think about a bridge built this year. It is made with this year's cement and this year's workers. Nobody can ship cement back from the year 2050. So in real things, the world as a whole cannot borrow from the future. One country can borrow from another and pay later. What a loan moves is a promise: who will be paid, later, out of what is made later.

[24 p] Economists have argued over this for a long time. One side says a national debt owed inside the country is not a burden on the next generation as a whole, because the children inherit the bonds as well as the tax bills (Abba Lerner). Debt owed to foreigners is different. The other side says future taxpayers carry a real burden (James Buchanan, 1958). A 2003 review for the US Congress found the burden is heaviest when borrowing pays for spending that is used up, and that much of it can pass to the future.

[25 table]
| The test | A case that passed | A case that failed |
|---|---|---|
| Did the money build something that makes the future richer? | America's interstate highways, built from the 1950s, gave a large one-time lift to the output of the industries that used them | Latin America's debt grew from 29 billion dollars in 1970 to 327 billion in 1982, much of it foreign loans. Then interest rates rose. Income per person fell by roughly a tenth in the 1980s, and about a third of the debt was later forgiven. Chile and Colombia were exceptions |
| Is the debt owed to ourselves or to outsiders? | Debt held at home moves money between citizens of the same country | About 31% of US government debt held by the public was owned by foreigners in December 2025. That part is a real claim by outsiders on future American output |

[26 p] US government debt held by the public rose from 27% of GDP in 1971 to about 100% in 2024. Public investment outside defence, at all levels of government, fell from above 4% of GDP in the 1960s to about 2.7% in 2019. These figures do not show that one caused the other.

[27 insider] (What question 6 does to the claim) Part 1 of the claim has the right picture with one correction. You do not borrow goods from the future. You borrow a promise. The promise is kept easily if the loan built something that makes tomorrow richer. If it did not, the children really do pay.

[28 check] Q: A friend says all government debt is theft from our grandchildren. What two questions would you ask?
A: First, what did the borrowing buy: something that lasts and earns, or spending that is used up? Second, who holds the debt? If citizens hold it, the grandchildren inherit the bonds as well as the taxes. If foreigners hold it, part of future output really is promised away.


### Question 7. Why does the law lean toward money made from nothing?

[30 p] This is the hardest question, and the last chapter did not push it far enough. Money made from nothing is not an accident. It stands on laws. Each law has a date, and each has someone who gains from it.

[31 table]
| The legal pillar | What it does | Who gains |
|---|---|---|
| Legal tender. In Malaysia, only notes and coins issued by Bank Negara are legal tender | Makes the state's money the money a creditor must accept to settle a debt | The state. Its money can always settle a debt |
| The bank licence | Only a licensed bank may take deposits. When it lends, it creates new deposits | Banks, which earn the gap between loan rates and deposit rates. Borrowers, who can get a loan |
| Deposit insurance. America: law in 1933, cover from 1934. Malaysia: from 2005, up to RM250,000 for each depositor at each bank since 2010, with separate Islamic cover | Promises savers their money back if a bank fails | Savers, who are safe. Banks, which can borrow from savers more cheaply because of the promise |
| The central bank as rescuer. America's Federal Reserve Act was signed in December 1913 | Lets the central bank lend to banks in a panic | Banks, and everyone who would lose a job or a deposit in a panic |
| The end of the gold link, 1971 | Removed the last metal limit on how much money can be made | Governments and borrowers, who gained room. Holders of cash lost a protection |

[32 p] Who wrote these rules? The plan behind America's central bank law was drawn up in secret in 1910 by a senator, a Treasury official and bankers, on a trip they called a duck hunt. That plan died, and Congress passed a changed version in 1913. The 1971 decision was made by a president and his advisers over one weekend. So insiders had a large hand in the rules, and elected lawmakers passed them where there was a vote. Voters were seldom asked directly. Switzerland, below, is a rare case.

[33 p] The safety net is worth real money to the biggest banks. Lenders believe a giant bank will be rescued, so they lend to it more cheaply. The International Monetary Fund, in 2014, put the value of that belief in 2012 at up to 70 billion dollars for the biggest US banks and up to 300 billion dollars for those in the euro area, depending on the method. The value fell after later reforms.

[34 p] Now the defence. The net was built because life without it was worse.

[35 table]
| Years in America | Banks that failed or shut | The rule at the time |
|---|---|---|
| 1921 to 1929 | More than 600 a year, on average | No deposit insurance |
| 1930 | 1,350 | No deposit insurance |
| 1931 | About 2,300 | No deposit insurance |
| 1933 | About 4,000 never reopened after the bank holiday | No deposit insurance |
| 1934 | 61: 9 insured and 52 uninsured | Deposit insurance began in January |

[36 p] The agency that runs the insurance says other things helped in 1934 too: rescue money, a better economy, and the weakest banks already gone. Insurance is not the only way to stop panics. Canada had none until 1967, yet none of its banks failed in the Great Depression (Cato Institute, NBER). Insurance has risks too (see the box at the end). Still, when Swiss voters were asked in 2018 whether to stop banks creating money, 75.7% said no.

[37 insider] (What question 7 does to the claim) So why does the law lean? Three reasons at once. The state gains room to borrow and to act in a crisis. The banks gain a public safety net under a private profit. And voters, when asked in Switzerland, kept money that stretches. So the lean is real, it has winners, and insiders had a large hand in drafting it, though elected lawmakers passed it. It was also chosen because the old way broke thousands of banks.


### Question 8. If credit makes plenty, why are the biggest printers among the poorest?

[39 p] Here is the next rebuttal. If money from nothing made countries rich, Zimbabwe would be the richest country on earth. In November 2008 its prices were doubling about once a day, by one well-known estimate. In 2018 Venezuela had the highest inflation in the world. But Zimbabwe's case was a state printing to pay its bills, not banks lending to builders. The IMF reports that its output had already fallen by about 40% from 2000 to 2007.

[40 p] The answer is that credit is only a promise to deliver later. Everything depends on what the new money is used for.

[41 table]
| What the new money buys | What it leaves behind | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A new factory, road, machine or skill | New output, which repays the loan | Plenty. This is the credit the claim is talking about |
| A house or share that already exists | The same house at a higher price | Higher asset prices. Owners gain and newcomers must borrow more |
| Spending that is used up, or a central bank paying a government's bills while output falls | More money chasing the same goods | Inflation, and at the extreme, Zimbabwe |

[42 p] Which kind do banks make most? In 17 rich countries, loans against property were about 30% of all bank lending in 1900 and about 60% by 2014. Does more credit bring more growth? Studies at the International Monetary Fund and the Bank for International Settlements found that it helps only up to a point. Past about 100% of GDP, more private credit goes with slower growth. Other economists dispute this, and older work found that deeper banking went with faster growth (see the box at the end). Malaysia's household debt was 84.8% of GDP in the first half of 2025. That leaves out loans to firms.

[43 p] Small loans to the poor were once hoped to end poverty. Six careful field trials in six countries found modest gains and more freedom of choice, but no fall in poverty.

[44 insider] (What question 8 does to the claim) Part 1 of the claim needs two more words. Not credit, but credit that builds. Credit used to make new things brings plenty. Credit used to bid for old things brings higher prices. Money printed to pay a state's bills brings inflation. That is state printing, not bank credit.


### Question 9. Who gets through the credit gate?

[46 p] The claim ends with the words: credit has requirements. That is true. A lender asks three things. Do you have income? Do you have something to pledge, such as a house? Do you have a record of paying on time? Notice what the three have in common. Each is easier to show if you already have money.

[47 table]
| Who is outside the gate | Number |
|---|---|
| Adults in the world with no financial account at all | 1.3 billion in 2024. About 79% of adults do have one, up from about half in 2011 |
| Adults in poorer and middle-income countries who borrowed from a bank or other formal lender in 2024 | 24%, up from 15% in 2014. Another 35% relied on informal sources, such as family |
| American adults with no credit record at all | About 26 million, or 1 in 10, on 2010 data. Another 19 million had too thin a record to score |

[48 p] And the gate is not simply open or shut. It has a price list. Loans with less security tend to cost more, though the numbers below are of different kinds.

[49 table]
| Kind of loan | Yearly interest | What kind of number it is |
|---|---|---|
| US home loan, 30 years | About 6% at the end of 2025 | An average, for a loan backed by a house |
| US credit card | About 21% to 23% in 2025 | An average, with no security |
| US payday loan, two weeks | Almost 400% when stated as a yearly rate | An example from the US consumer finance regulator: a fee of 15 dollars for each 100 dollars borrowed |
| Malaysia, licensed moneylender | Capped by law at 12% with a pledge and 18% without | A legal ceiling, not an average. One 2023 complaint: RM3,000 to be repaid at about RM11,000 (Malay Mail) |

[50 p] Bank Negara reports that newly approved loans in Malaysia had a median debt service ratio of 41%: payments take that share of income. So the gate is real even for people who pass it.

[51 p] In Malaysia, Islamic financing was 46.6% of all financing in the financial system in 2024, Bernama reported from Bank Negara data. It follows rules against riba, usually read as interest. Whether its contracts share risk in a different way from an ordinary loan is argued, and this chapter has not tested it.

[52 insider] (What question 9 does to the claim) Part 4 of the claim holds, and more strongly than its author may like. Credit does have requirements, and the requirements are the things the comfortable already have. In 2024 about a quarter of adults in poorer countries borrowed from a formal lender. Not borrowing is not always being shut out: some do not want a loan. The gate is wider than it was. It is still a gate.


### Question 10. Does credit make a person free, or owned?

[54 p] The claim says the modern system pushes people toward standing on their own feet through credit. An old warning in the Bible's book of Proverbs says the opposite: the borrower is servant to the lender. Which is it? The evidence says credit is a lever. A lever multiplies force in whichever direction you push.

[55 table]
| The lever working for the borrower | The lever working against the borrower |
|---|---|
| In 2022 the typical American home owner had a net worth of $396,200. The typical renter had $10,400. Owners are older and richer to begin with, so the home loan is not the whole reason, but about half of the owner's wealth was the home | American card holders paid over 105 billion dollars in card interest in 2022. Nearly one in ten card users were charged more in interest and fees each year than they paid off the debt |
| A loan for a home or a skill can buy something that grows or earns | The poorest fifth of American home owners owed about 80% of the value of what they owned in 2007. Their net worth fell from about $30,000 to almost nothing by 2010 |
| The law is kinder than it was. England stopped jailing most debtors in 1869. In Malaysia a person can be made bankrupt only for debts of RM100,000 or more, and can be released after three years if the conditions are met | Malaysia recorded 5,977 bankruptcies in 2024, and 46% of them came from personal loans. Malaysia's student loan fund, PTPTN, reported about 1.2 million borrowers behind on their loans and RM11.18 billion in missed payments in 2025 |

[56 p] So the same tool that goes with the owners' wealth also wiped out the wealth of the poorest owners. Three plain tests separate the two cases. This is how the idea works. It is not advice on any loan.

[57 table]
| Test | When the lever lifts the borrower | When it crushes the borrower |
|---|---|---|
| What does the loan buy? | Something that grows, earns or lasts | Something used up this month |
| What does it cost against what it earns? | The thing bought grows faster than the interest rate | The interest rate is far above anything the thing can earn |
| Can the borrower carry a bad year? | Payments are small enough to survive a lost job or a fall in prices | One bad year and the lender takes the asset |

[58 insider] (What question 10 does to the claim) Part 4 of the claim is right that the system rewards those who can use credit, and wrong if it suggests credit by itself sets anyone free. Credit multiplies what you already are: your income, your judgment and your luck.

[59 check] Q: Two people each borrow RM50,000. One ends up better off and one ends up bankrupt. Name three things that could explain the difference.
A: What the loan bought: a skill or tool that earns, against spending that is used up. What it cost: a bank rate, against a moneylender or card rate. And the cushion: whether the borrower could keep paying through a bad year.


### Epilogue. The claim, graded

[61 p] The jury has heard seven questions. Here is the reader's claim again, line by line, with the finding.

[62 table]
| The claim | Finding | The number that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Without credit there is no abundance | Holds in part. Credit that builds is one engine of plenty. Credit by itself is not | Rich-world growth was slower in 1974 to 1989 than in 1951 to 1973. Six trials of small loans found no fall in poverty |
| 2. Scarce money explains the centuries of poor peasants | Holds in part. Scarce coins hurt trade at times, but scarce output was the bigger cause | Coin famines closed mints, but about 68% of English working men still farmed in 1522 |
| 3. About 80% could not save and compound | Holds in part. Most were very poor, but poverty does not prove they could not save. Low output was the bigger cause, not rare money | About three quarters of the world lived in extreme poverty in 1820, by one estimate. The 80% is the claim's own figure |
| 4. After 1971 the system pushes everyone to stand on their own through credit, which has requirements | Holds in part. Credit is ancient. 1971 removed the last metal limit. The requirements are real and favour those who already have | Loan records are about 4,400 years old. Only 24% of adults in poorer countries borrowed formally in 2024 |

[63 p] So here is the claim again, rebuilt so that the evidence can carry it.

[64 steps]
- Plenty comes from output, from what work and tools make. Money alone does not create it. Spain's silver is the classic warning.
- Credit lets people build before they have saved, and it is four thousand years old. Whether money that cannot stretch would hold back building today is untested. In the rich world, the fastest growth came before 1971.
- The law leans toward stretchy money because the state gains, the banks gain, and voters, when asked, have kept it. The lean has a price: the toll from the last chapter, and a safety net worth billions to the biggest banks.
- Since 1971 no metal limits credit, only laws and human judgment. Rich-world growth did not speed up after it, at least to 1989. Credit is one ladder for ordinary people, but what it buys can also fall, as America's poorest home owners found after 2007.
- The ladder has a gate, and the gate favours people who already have income, property and a record. So the fight is not only about whether money should stretch. It is also about what credit is used for, and who is let through the gate.

[65 p] The reader who made this claim saw something true and important. The modern system is not only a toll. It lets more people borrow formally than before, but credit between neighbours is far older. The claim reached too far in one place, by blaming scarce money for a poverty that was mostly scarce output. And it stopped short in another, because a ladder with a gate does not free everyone who needs it.

[66 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that countries with the most credit grow fastest even past 100% of income. Second, that access to credit, on its own, lifts the poor in careful trials. Third, that a fixed money could fund the same building through falling prices and saving alone. Today the evidence leans against the first, though it is disputed, and is against the second. The third has not been tested in a modern economy.

[67 steps]
- When someone says credit is slavery, ask: what did the loan buy, and at what rate?
- When someone says credit is freedom, ask: who is let through the gate, and what does it cost those who are not?
- When someone says printing made us rich, ask: was it the money, or the things the money was used to build?
- When someone says gold kept us poor, ask: was the problem too little money, or too little made?

[69 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The strongest objections to this chapter
[69.0 p] From the hard-money side: the fastest growth on record, 1951 to 1973, came while the dollar was still tied to gold. That is true, and the table in question 5 shows it. The reply is that this chapter has no clean credit figure for those years, and that the table cannot separate the money rule from everything else that changed after the war. The point still stands as a warning to anyone who says the end of gold brought plenty.

[69.1 p] From the credit side: the slower growth after 1973 may have had other causes. That may well be true. The credit side also cites a study of about 80 countries (King and Levine, 1993): those with deeper banking in 1960 grew faster over the next thirty years, though that does not prove the banks caused it. And William Cline of the Peterson Institute argued in 2015 that the 100% threshold is partly a quirk of the method. The threshold's authors reply that it holds. This chapter does not claim that ending the gold link slowed growth. It claims only that the speed-up the claim needs is not in the numbers.

[69.2 p] From the reformers: some want banks stopped from creating money at all, so that only the state could. Swiss voters rejected that in 2018. Nobody has run a modern economy that way, so its costs and gains are unknown. The safety net has critics too. A study of 61 countries found that where rules were weak, deposit insurance made banking crises more likely (Demirguc-Kunt and Detragiache, 2002).

[69 end of deeper box]

[70 callout] (Not advice) This chapter weighs an argument about how money works. It does not tell you to borrow or not to borrow. Every number has a date and will change.
