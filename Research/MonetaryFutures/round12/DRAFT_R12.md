## L3.2 Money from the future: seven harder questions about credit (id rb-credit)

Kicker: The last chapter put printing on trial and found a toll, not a scam. Now the defence makes a bigger claim: that money made from nothing is the reason ordinary people are no longer peasants. Seven hard questions test that claim, and the epilogue grades it line by line.

[0 tldr]
- Credit is a promise that lets you build today and pay from tomorrow's income. It is thousands of years old. It was not born in 1971. What 1971 did was remove the last gold limit on it.
- The peasants of old kingdoms were poor mostly because output was scarce, not because coins were scarce. Spain shipped home mountains of silver in the 1500s and got higher prices, not riches.
- The law leans toward money made from nothing for three reasons: the state gains, the banks gain, and voters fear bank panics more than they fear a slow toll. Nobody asked the people who pay the toll.
- Credit that builds something makes plenty. Credit that only bids up old houses, or pays for spending, does not. And credit has a gate: the poorer you are, the harder it is to pass and the more you pay.
- So credit is a lever, not a gift. It can make a person free or owned. The difference is what the loan buys, what it costs, and whether you can live through a bad year.

[1 bridge] Built on earlier chapters: recall these before you start
- rb-print: Printing is not a scam in the plain sense. It is a toll, paid most by cash holders, renters and late savers. Hard money has its own toll, on borrowers and the young.
- rb-moneymake: Most money is born when a bank makes a loan. The loan creates a new deposit that did not exist before.
- rb-07: Interest can be paid if money keeps moving. Trouble starts when debt grows faster than income.
- rb-02: Rulers watered down coins long before paper money. Hard money protects savers and squeezes borrowers.

[2 callout] (How to read this chapter) Seven questions and an epilogue, about 40 minutes in all. Each question ends with a short line on what it does to the claim. Read the main path first and skip the boxes marked Go deeper.


### The defence makes a bigger claim

[4 p] In the last chapter, money stood in the dock and the jury found a toll, not a scam. The defence is not satisfied with that. It stands up again and says: you have treated printing as a cost to be excused. It is more than that. It is the reason most people alive today are not peasants.

[5 p] The claim came from a reader of this site, and it has four parts. Here it is in plain words.

[6 steps]
- Part 1. Without credit there is no abundance. Credit is a token we borrow from our own future.
- Part 2. If money is kept scarce, it can never be plentiful for ordinary people. That is why kingdoms and empires had so many poor peasants for so many centuries.
- Part 3. About 80% of people in those centuries had no way to save and grow their savings. Money was so rare that wages could not fund any real growth.
- Part 4. So the system after 1971, when President Nixon cut the dollar's last link to gold, pushes everyone toward standing on their own feet through credit. And credit has requirements.

[7 p] It is a serious claim, and parts of it are right. A fair court does not accept a claim because it sounds good. It cross-examines. The first three questions were asked in the last chapter. Here are questions 4 to 10.


### Question 4. Were the peasants poor because money was scarce, or because output was scarce?

[9 p] Start with what money is. Money is a claim ticket on the things people make. If a village bakes 100 loaves, printing more tickets does not bake loaf 101. It only changes how many tickets each loaf costs. So the question is which was missing in the old kingdoms: tickets, or loaves?

[10 table]
| Evidence | What happened | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Spain's silver | Spain shipped home about 210 million pesos of silver from the Americas in the 1500s. Prices in Western Europe rose 3 to 4 times in that century. The Spanish king still failed to pay his debts four times, in 1557, 1560, 1575 and 1596 | More money, same output: higher prices, not riches |
| The king's gold in Cairo | In 1324 Mansa Musa, king of Mali, spent so much gold in Cairo that the price of gold there fell for years | A flood of money made gold cheap. It made nobody rich |
| After the plague | The Black Death of 1348 killed a large share of England's workers. No new coins arrived. Yet the pay of those who lived rose in real terms, because workers were scarce | Pay followed output per worker, not the number of coins |
| Who worked where | In 1522 about 68% of English workers were farming | Most people grew food by hand. There was little left over to save |
| The coin famines | From the 1370s to the 1470s Europe ran short of silver. Mints in Ghent and Bruges stopped making coins. Small coins for daily trade were short in England, France and Florence for centuries | Scarce coins did hurt trade, for real |

[11 p] So both things were true, but not equally. The main cause of old poverty was scarce output. Most people farmed by muscle, and a bad harvest meant hunger no matter how many coins the king had. Scarce coins made it worse at times, because trade is hard when there is nothing to pay with. In 1820 about three quarters of the world still lived in extreme poverty. Today it is about 8%. What changed first was machines, energy and knowledge. Money had to stretch to keep up, which is the next question.

[12 insider] (What question 4 does to the claim) Part 2 of the claim holds only in part. Scarce money did choke trade at times. But the peasants were poor mainly because there was little to buy, not because there was little to buy it with. Spain proves that more money alone does not make plenty.


### Question 5. Was credit born with Nixon in 1971?

[14 p] No. Credit is far older than paper money, and older than coins.

[15 table]
| When | What existed |
|---|---|
| About 4,400 years ago | The earliest known record of a loan, in Mesopotamia. Later Babylonian law set interest at one third on barley loans and usually 20% on silver loans |
| 1694 | The Bank of England was founded to lend 1.2 million pounds to the Crown at about 8% interest |
| 1815 | Britain's government debt was over 200% of its yearly income after the wars with Napoleon. It fell to about 30% by 1914, while Britain led the industrial age |
| By 1860 | In America, bank deposits were already a bigger part of the money supply than bank notes. This was under gold and silver |
| 1971 | President Nixon and fifteen advisers met at Camp David and ended the right of foreign central banks to trade in dollars for gold. It was announced on television on 15 August. Congress did not vote on it |

[16 p] So what did 1971 change? It removed the last outside limit. Before it, gold put a ceiling somewhere above the system. After it, the only limits on credit were the judgment of banks, central banks and borrowers.

[17 p] Did plenty arrive with it? Look at growth in income per person in the rich industrial countries.

[18 table]
| Period | The money rule | Growth per person, a year |
|---|---|---|
| 1870 to 1913 | Gold standard | 1.3% |
| 1951 to 1973 | Dollar tied to gold for central banks | 3.5% |
| 1974 to 1989 | Paper money, no gold link | 2.1% |

[19 p] The fastest growth came before 1971, when bank lending was still small: about 30% of yearly income in the rich countries in 1945. It reached about 120% by 2008, and most of that rise came after the mid 1980s. In America, all debt outside the finance industry went from about 133% of yearly income in 1971 to about 251% in 2024.

[20 insider] (What question 5 does to the claim) Part 4 of the claim needs a new date. Credit did not start in 1971. Societies have borrowed from the future for four thousand years, and the industrial age was built on bank credit under gold. What 1971 did was take off the last brake. Since then credit has roughly doubled against income, and growth has been slower than in the years just before, not faster.


### Question 6. Can a society really borrow from the future?

[22 p] Here is the rebuttal most people give: borrowing does not create anything. It just steals from our children, who will have to pay it back.

[23 p] Think about a bridge built this year. It is made with this year's cement and this year's workers. Nobody can ship cement back from the year 2050. So in real things, a society cannot borrow from the future at all. What a loan moves is a promise: who will be paid, later, out of what is made later.

[24 p] Economists have argued over this for a long time. One side says a national debt owed inside the country is not a burden on the next generation as a whole, because the children inherit the bonds as well as the tax bills (Abba Lerner). The other side says the taxpayers who must service the debt do carry a real burden (James Buchanan, 1958). A later review for the US Congress put the two together: the burden is real when the borrowing paid for spending that is used up, and small when it paid for something that lasts.

[25 table]
| The test | A case that passed | A case that failed |
|---|---|---|
| Did the borrowing build something that makes the future richer? | America's interstate highways, built from the 1950s, gave a large one-time lift to the output of the industries that used them | Latin America's debt grew from 29 billion dollars in 1970 to 327 billion in 1982. Then income per person fell about 9% to 10% in the 1980s, and about a third of the debt was later forgiven |
| Is the debt owed to ourselves or to outsiders? | Debt held at home moves money between citizens of the same country | About 31% of US government debt held by the public was owned by foreigners in December 2025. That part is a real claim by outsiders on future American output |

[26 p] One more number matters. US government debt held by the public rose from 27% of yearly income in 1971 to about 100% in 2024. Over the same years, public investment outside defence fell from above 4% of income in the 1960s to about 2.7% in 2019. More was borrowed, and less of it was built into things that last.

[27 insider] (What question 6 does to the claim) Part 1 of the claim has the right picture with one correction. You do not borrow goods from the future. You borrow a promise. The promise is kept easily if the loan built something that makes tomorrow richer. If it did not, the children really do pay.

[28 check] Q: A friend says all government debt is theft from our grandchildren. What two questions would you ask?
A: First, what did the borrowing buy: something that lasts and earns, like a road or a power grid, or spending that is used up? Second, who holds the debt? If citizens hold it, the grandchildren inherit the bonds as well as the taxes. If foreigners hold it, part of future output really is promised away.


### Question 7. Why does the law lean toward money made from nothing?

[30 p] This is the hardest question, and the last chapter did not push it far enough. Money made from nothing is not an accident. It stands on laws. Each law has a date, and each has someone who gains from it.

[31 table]
| The legal pillar | What it does | Who gains |
|---|---|---|
| Legal tender. In Malaysia, only notes and coins issued by Bank Negara are legal tender (Currency Act 2020) | Makes the state's money the one money that settles a debt | The state. Everyone must hold its money |
| The bank licence | Lets a licensed bank take deposits and create new ones when it lends | Banks, which earn the gap between loan rates and deposit rates. Borrowers, who can get a loan |
| Deposit insurance. America in 1933. Malaysia in 2005, covering up to RM250,000 for each depositor at each bank since 2010 | Promises savers their money back if a bank fails | Savers, who are safe. Banks, which can borrow from savers more cheaply because of the promise |
| The central bank as rescuer. America's Federal Reserve Act was signed in December 1913 | Lets the central bank lend to banks in a panic | Banks, and everyone who would lose a job or a deposit in a panic |
| The end of the gold link, 1971 | Removed the last outside limit on how much money can be made | Governments and borrowers, who gained room. Holders of cash lost a protection |

[32 p] Who wrote these rules? The first draft of America's central bank law came from a secret meeting of a senator and leading bankers in 1910, disguised as a duck hunt. A changed version was then passed by an elected Congress. The 1971 decision was made by a president and his advisers over one weekend. So the rules were written partly by bankers and officials, and passed, where there was a vote, by lawmakers. The people who pay the toll were rarely asked.

[33 p] The safety net is worth real money to the biggest banks. Lenders believe a giant bank will be rescued, so they lend to it more cheaply. The International Monetary Fund put the value of that belief in 2012 at up to 70 billion dollars for the biggest US banks and up to 300 billion dollars for those in the euro area.

[34 p] Now the defence. The net was built because life without it was worse.

[35 table]
| Years in America | Banks that failed or shut | The rule at the time |
|---|---|---|
| 1921 to 1929 | More than 600 a year, on average | No deposit insurance |
| 1930 | 1,350 | No deposit insurance |
| 1931 | About 2,300 | No deposit insurance |
| 1933 | About 4,000 never reopened after the bank holiday | No deposit insurance |
| 1934 | 9 insured banks | Deposit insurance began in January |

[36 p] The agency that runs the insurance says other things helped in 1934 too: rescue money, a better economy, and the weakest banks already gone. Still, the fear of panics is why voters keep the system. In 2018 Swiss voters were asked whether to stop banks creating money. 75.7% said no.

[37 insider] (What question 7 does to the claim) So why does the law lean? Three reasons at once. The state gains room to borrow and to act in a crisis. The banks gain a public safety net under a private profit. And most voters, given the choice, prefer money that stretches to money that snaps in a panic. That is the fair answer to the reader's complaint: the lean is real, it has winners, and it was chosen more by insiders than by the people who pay. But it was also chosen because the old way broke thousands of banks.


### Question 8. If credit makes plenty, why are the biggest printers among the poorest?

[39 p] Here is the next rebuttal. If money from nothing made countries rich, Zimbabwe would be the richest country on earth. In November 2008 its prices were doubling about once a day, by one well-known estimate. In 2018 Venezuela had the highest inflation in the world. Both printed without limit. Both grew poorer.

[40 p] The answer is that credit is only a promise to deliver later. Everything depends on what the new money is used for.

[41 table]
| What the new money buys | What it leaves behind | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A new factory, road, machine or skill | New output, which repays the loan | Plenty. This is the credit the claim is talking about |
| A house or share that already exists | The same house at a higher price | Higher asset prices. Owners gain and newcomers must borrow more |
| Spending that is used up, or a government's bills with nothing new made | More money chasing the same goods | Inflation, and at the extreme, Zimbabwe |

[42 p] Which kind do banks make most? In 17 rich countries, loans against property were about 30% of all bank lending in 1900 and about 60% by 2007. Much of that pays for houses that already stand. And studies at the International Monetary Fund and the Bank for International Settlements both found that more credit helps growth only up to a point. Once private credit passes about 100% of a country's yearly income, more of it goes with slower growth. Malaysia's household debt alone was 84.8% of yearly income in the first half of 2025.

[43 p] Small loans to the poor were once hoped to end poverty. Six careful field trials in six countries found modest gains and more freedom of choice, but no fall in poverty.

[44 insider] (What question 8 does to the claim) Part 1 of the claim needs two more words. Not credit, but credit that builds. Credit used to make new things brings plenty. Credit used to bid for old things brings higher prices. Credit used to cover bills brings inflation.


### Question 9. Who gets through the credit gate?

[46 p] The claim ends with the words: credit has requirements. That is true, and it matters more than it sounds. A lender asks three things. Do you have income? Do you have something to pledge, such as a house? Do you have a record of paying on time? Notice what the three have in common. Each is easier to show if you already have money.

[47 table]
| Who is outside the gate | Number |
|---|---|
| Adults in the world with no financial account at all | 1.3 billion in 2024. About 79% of adults do have one, up from 50% in 2011 |
| Adults in poorer and middle-income countries who borrowed from a bank or other formal lender in 2024 | 24%. Another 35% borrowed only from family, friends or other informal sources |
| American adults with no credit record at all | About 26 million, or 1 in 10, on 2010 data. Another 19 million had too thin a record to score |

[48 p] And the gate is not simply open or shut. It has a price list, and the price rises as the borrower gets poorer.

[49 table]
| Kind of loan | Typical yearly interest | Who usually gets it |
|---|---|---|
| US home loan, 30 years | About 6% at the end of 2025 | People with steady income, savings and a good record |
| US credit card | About 21% to 23% in 2025 | Most people with a record |
| US payday loan, two weeks | Almost 400% when stated as a yearly rate | People who cannot get the other two |
| Malaysia, licensed moneylender | Capped by law at 12% with a pledge and 18% without | People a bank has turned away |

[50 p] In Malaysia the typical new borrower already uses 41% of income to service debt, according to Bank Negara. So the gate is real even for people who pass it.

[51 insider] (What question 9 does to the claim) Part 4 of the claim holds, and more strongly than its author may like. Credit does have requirements, and the requirements are the things the comfortable already have. In 1820 about three quarters of the world was too poor to save. In 2024 about three quarters of adults in poorer countries did not borrow from a formal lender. The gate is wider than it was. It is still a gate.


### Question 10. Does credit make a person free, or owned?

[53 p] The claim says the modern system pushes people toward standing on their own feet through credit. The oldest warning about debt says the opposite: the borrower is servant to the lender. Which is it? The evidence says credit is a lever. A lever multiplies force in whichever direction you push.

[54 table]
| The lever working for the borrower | The lever working against the borrower |
|---|---|
| In 2022 the typical American home owner had a net worth of $396,200. The typical renter had $10,400. Owners are older and richer to begin with, so the home loan is not the whole reason, but about half of the owner's wealth was the home | American card holders paid over 105 billion dollars in card interest in 2022. Nearly one in ten paid more in interest and fees each year than they paid off the debt |
| A loan for a home or a skill can buy something that grows or earns | The poorest fifth of American home owners owed about 80% of the value of what they owned in 2007. Their typical net worth fell from about $30,000 to almost nothing by 2010 |
| The law is kinder than it was. England stopped jailing most debtors in 1869. In Malaysia a person can be made bankrupt only for debts of RM100,000 or more, and can be released after three years if the conditions are met | Malaysia recorded 5,977 bankruptcies in 2024, and 46% of them came from personal loans. About 1.2 million people are behind on government student loans, owing RM11.18 billion in missed payments |

[55 p] So the same tool that built the owner's wealth wiped out the poorest owner's wealth. Three plain tests separate the two cases. This is how the idea works. It is not advice on any loan.

[56 table]
| Test | The lever lifts you | The lever crushes you |
|---|---|---|
| What does the loan buy? | Something that grows, earns or lasts | Something used up this month |
| What does it cost against what it earns? | The thing bought grows faster than the interest rate | The interest rate is far above anything the thing can earn |
| Can you carry a bad year? | Payments are small enough to survive a lost job or a fall in prices | One bad year and the lender takes the asset |

[57 insider] (What question 10 does to the claim) Part 4 of the claim is right that the system rewards those who can use credit, and wrong if it suggests credit by itself sets anyone free. Credit multiplies what you already are: your income, your judgment and your luck.

[58 check] Q: Two people each borrow RM50,000. One ends up better off and one ends up bankrupt. Name three things that could explain the difference.
A: What the loan bought: a skill or tool that earns, against spending that is used up. What it cost: a bank rate, against a moneylender or card rate. And the cushion: whether the borrower could keep paying through a bad year. The loan is the same size. The lever points in opposite directions.


### Epilogue. The claim, graded

[60 p] The jury has heard seven questions. Here is the reader's claim again, line by line, with the finding.

[61 table]
| The claim | Finding | The number that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Without credit there is no abundance | Holds in part. Credit that builds is one engine of plenty. Credit by itself is not | Growth was fastest, 3.5% a year, when bank lending was small. Past about 100% of income, more credit goes with slower growth |
| 2. Scarce money explains the centuries of poor peasants | Holds only a little. Scarce output was the main cause | Spain's silver raised prices 3 to 4 times and left Spain no richer. 68% of English workers farmed in 1522 |
| 3. About 80% could not save and compound | Holds as a fact. The cause was low output more than rare money | About three quarters of the world lived in extreme poverty in 1820 |
| 4. After 1971 the system pushes everyone to stand on their own through credit, which has requirements | Holds in part. Credit is ancient. 1971 removed the last limit. The requirements are real and favour those who already have | US debt outside finance went from 133% to 251% of income. Only 24% of adults in poorer countries borrowed formally in 2024 |

[62 p] So here is the claim again, rebuilt so that the evidence can carry it.

[63 steps]
- Plenty comes from output: from machines, energy, knowledge and work. Money does not create it. Spain's silver proved that.
- But output grows fastest when people can build before they have saved. Credit is the tool for that, and it is four thousand years old. A money that cannot stretch makes building wait for savings that most people never had.
- The law leans toward stretchy money because the state gains, the banks gain, and most voters fear panics more than a slow toll. The lean has a price: the toll from the last chapter, and a safety net that is worth most to the biggest banks.
- Since 1971 nothing outside human judgment limits credit. That did not bring faster growth. It did make access to credit the main ladder for ordinary people, because cash melts and the things credit buys tend to rise.
- The ladder has a gate, and the gate favours people who already have income, property and a record. So the fair fight is no longer about whether money should stretch. It is about what credit is used for, and who is let through the gate.

[64 p] The reader who made this claim saw something true and important. The modern system is not only a toll. It is also the first money system that offers ordinary people a way to build before they have saved. The claim reached too far in one place, by blaming scarce money for a poverty that was mostly scarce output. And it stopped short in another, because a ladder with a gate does not free everyone who needs it.

[65 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that countries with the most credit grow fastest even past 100% of income. Second, that access to credit, on its own, lifts the poor in careful trials. Third, that a fixed money could fund the same building through falling prices and saving alone. Today the evidence is against the first two, and the third has not been tested in a modern economy.

[66 steps]
- When someone says credit is slavery, ask: what did the loan buy, and at what rate?
- When someone says credit is freedom, ask: who is let through the gate, and what does it cost those who are not?
- When someone says printing made us rich, ask: was it the money, or the things the money was used to build?
- When someone says gold kept us poor, ask them about Spain.

[68 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): The strongest objections to this chapter
[68.0 p] From the hard-money side: the fastest growth on record, 1951 to 1973, came while the dollar was still tied to gold. That is true, and the table in question 5 shows it. The reply is that bank credit grew strongly inside that system too, and that those years also had post-war catching up. The point still stands as a warning to anyone who says the end of gold brought plenty.

[68.1 p] From the credit side: the slower growth after 1973 had other causes, such as oil shocks and the end of catching up. That is also true. This chapter does not claim that ending the gold link slowed growth. It claims only that the speed-up the claim needs is not in the numbers.

[68.2 p] From the reformers: some want banks stopped from creating money at all, so that only the state could. Swiss voters rejected that in 2018. Nobody has run a modern economy that way, so its costs and gains are unknown.

[68 end of deeper box]
