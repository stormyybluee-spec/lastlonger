# The pressure: the debt treadmill

Landscape chapter 02. Stack: Intelligence (graded, dated findings). Id: ls-02. Facts as of 2026-10-23.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#ls-02 . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*The US debt grows on its own, mainly because the government spends more than it taxes even before paying interest.*

Everything in this story starts with one pressure. The US government owes $40.44 trillion in total, and $32.566 trillion of that is held by the public, about 100% of the economy's yearly output (GDP). In the fiscal year that ended on 30 September 2026 it collected $5,403.3 billion, spent $7,395.8 billion, and borrowed the difference: $1,992.5 billion, about 6.2% of GDP.

Two numbers inside that deficit matter most. Interest on the debt cost $1,051.8 billion. And the rest, the gap between what the government spends on everything else and what it collects, was $940.7 billion, or 2.92% of GDP. Economists call that second number the primary deficit. It is the engine of the whole problem.

*[Picture on the web page: The debt treadmill. The belt is the interest cost, the runner's legs are real growth, and the steady push is the primary deficit. Ways off: spend less, grow faster, or let inflation shrink the debt.]*

> **Picture this.** Imagine running on a treadmill while someone keeps adding weight to your backpack. The belt speed is the interest rate on your debt. Your legs are economic growth. The backpack is the primary deficit, new borrowing every year just to cover normal spending. Mallers framed the problem as belt versus legs. The project found the bigger issue is the backpack.

### The surprising finding: it is the backpack, not the belt

The popular story says high interest rates are what make the debt explode. The project tested that with Treasury's own numbers. The real interest rate on 10-year debt (2.76%, the yield on inflation-protected bonds) is only slightly above the economy's trend real growth (2.50% a year over ten years). The gap is just +0.26 percentage points. At market rates, the primary deficit explains 92% to 102% of the yearly rise in the debt ratio. Ruling Dc-02: Confirmed, 80.

### Repricing: why the bill keeps rising even if nothing changes

The debt is not one loan. It is a pile of loans with expiry dates. Each time one expires, the Treasury borrows again at today's rate. The average rate on the whole pile is 3.518%, but new borrowing costs about 4.88%. Because 70.4% of the marketable debt reprices within five years, the interest bill climbs on its own. If yields hold, the project's model has interest heading to about $1.6 trillion in three years and $1.9 trillion in five.

> The Treasury has been funding itself more with short-term bills, which now make up 22.36% of marketable debt. That keeps costs down when short rates are low, but it means every Fed hike passes through to the federal budget within months. Shortening the debt is a bet that short rates will fall. It makes the budget more sensitive, not less.

### Was the Fed's hike "gasoline on the fire"?

Mallers argued that when the Fed raises rates, it pays more interest into the economy, so hiking is stimulus. The project checked the size. A quarter-point hike costs about $20 billion to $26 billion a year, about 2% of net interest. The real policy rate is mild: about +0.2% on 12-month inflation of 3.7%. And his inputs were overstated: the debt reprices from 3.52% to about 4.9%, not from 2% to 5%. Ruling Dc-07: the "gasoline" claim is Refuted, 70. The deeper point cuts the other way: a Fed that hikes against the President is evidence against fiscal dominance, not for it.

### The quiet helper: inflation

*[Picture on the web page: A shrinking yardstick. Debt is written in dollars, and when prices rise faster than the interest rate on the debt, the real burden shrinks without anyone voting for it.]*

Here is the part almost nobody says out loud. Nominal growth (real growth plus inflation) is running about 5.7% to 6%, while the average cost of the debt is about 3.2% to 3.5%. That gap quietly holds the debt ratio's drift to about 0.4 to 0.6 points a year. The project calls this passive inflation doing the stabilizing work (Dc-09, Plausible, 60). It fades as the debt reprices. And if inflation fell back to 2% while real rates stayed near 2.7%, the ratio would climb faster, which is a strange truth: success against inflation makes the debt math harder.

### Can AI growth fix it?

Elon Musk has said only AI and robots can solve the US debt. The word "only" is Refuted (70): a primary adjustment of about 2.9% of GDP would stabilize the ratio, and history shows growth, inflation, repression and discipline doing the work, never technology alone. But AI can help under three conditions (Dc-03, Confirmed as arithmetic, 75):

- Spending must not rise with the growth. In the project's model, +2.26 points of AI growth with spending held takes the debt to 87% of GDP. If spending scales up with it, 107%.
- Real interest rates must not rise with growth. If they rise one for one, the debt reaches about 129%, the same as with no AI at all. In 2026 the real 10-year yield rose 82 basis points, so this condition is already under strain. Round 5 update: measured from end 2025 to 23 October the rise is 73bp to 2.66%, mostly a tighter expected Fed path with Japan adding about 18bp, not AI demand directly.
- The gains must land in taxable income. In FY2026 corporate receipts fell 15.6%, from $452.1B to $381.4B, as firms deducted AI investment faster. This condition is failing now. Round 5 update: the drag tracks capex growth and should shrink once capex goes flat, and September 2026 receipts were already up 38% on a year.

- **Myth:** Money printing has a hard ceiling, and the US is nearly maxed out.
- **Reality:** There is no fixed ceiling. The real limits are the primary deficit, the real interest rate and how much inflation the public will tolerate. The debt can grow for a long time. What changes is who pays, and how visibly.

**Check yourself.** Q: If the Fed cut rates to zero tomorrow, would the debt problem be solved?
A: No. Interest costs would fall over time, but the primary deficit of about 2.9% of GDP would keep adding debt every year. The backpack stays even if the belt slows.
