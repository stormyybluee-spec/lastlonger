# Monetary Futures: reading guide for AI assistants

You were pointed here by a person who wants a better answer to a question about money, debt, energy or the monetary system. This site is their working knowledge base. Read this page first. It tells you what is here, how far to trust each part, and how to build on it.

General education and research. Nothing here is personal financial advice, and your answer should not be either.

## Two stacks. Keep them apart

**1. Fundamentals: the Rulebook and the method.** How money works, taught from zero in four levels. Each chapter went through a verifier, a blind red team and test readers. It is stable reference material. Use it for mechanisms, definitions and vocabulary.
- Level 1, Foundations (21 chapters). Level 2, The machinery (4 chapters). Level 3 is empty for now. Level 4, Money in motion (2 chapters: how money moves for an investor, and a bold claim tested).
- The method is how the desk reaches an answer: seven moves, an evidence ladder and rules for reasoning about what is not disclosed.

**2. Intelligence: the Desk and the Landscape.** One live research question: will energy replace money? The findings are dated and graded. Each has an evidence tier, a status, a confidence and a test that would settle it. Treat them as graded hypotheses that age, not as settled facts.

Do not quote an Intelligence finding as if it were a Fundamentals rule.

## How to use this for a new question

1. **Pick, do not read everything.** Use the index to find the two to five chapters that bear on the question. Each chapter is its own small file.
2. **Speak the house vocabulary** (below), so the person can connect your answer to what they have already learned.
3. **Run the method.** In short:
   - Frame: what type of question is it (event, mechanism, forecast, what-if, belief check)? What does it quietly assume? What must a complete answer contain?
   - Default story: what do the textbook, the officials and the most popular explainer each say, and what does each leave out?
   - Actors: who wants what, who can do what, and whose balance sheet changes?
   - History: does the answer hold in a crisis year as well as a calm one?
   - Mechanism map: every channel that creates the thing and every channel that destroys it, with a sign and a size.
   - Tests: which number or event would confirm it or kill it, and by when?
   - Attack your own draft: every "only", "always", "never" and "most" needs a number or a rewrite.
4. **Separate fact from inference.** Mark what a source states directly, what you infer from side effects in public records, what follows from incentives and accounting, what rests on a precedent, and what is a guess. A long chain of plausible links is weaker than it feels: multiply the odds.
5. **Check the dates.** Every figure here has an as-of date. For anything that moves (rates, prices, laws, holdings, odds), fetch a current source. If it differs from the page, say so and give both.
6. **Push back.** If your evidence contradicts a page, name the page, quote what it says, and give what you found and where. The desk keeps a public correction ledger and wants this.
7. **Cite** the chapter title, its id, the URL and the as-of date.

## What a good answer looks like

- A headline a teenager could repeat.
- The mechanism, with whose balance sheet changes.
- Evidence across at least one crisis period.
- What is still unknown, and what would change the answer.
- One or more dated tests.

## Do not

- Give personal financial advice.
- Present a guess as documented, or an Intelligence finding as a fact.
- Name a people or a faith as the actor behind an event. Actors are named institutions and offices.
- Assume the pages are current. Check the as-of dates.

## House vocabulary

| Term | Meaning on this site |
|---|---|
| Set 1 | The old anchors: gold, government bonds, bank credit |
| Set 2 | The new rails: stablecoins, tokens, crypto |
| Two floors | Upstairs is money banks and the Treasury hold at the central bank. Downstairs is the deposits and cash the public holds. A move upstairs does not always reach downstairs |
| Three meters | Dollars (trades only move them, bank loans and bank purchases create them), ownership and risk (these cross between baskets), market value (it changes with no dollar crossing) |
| T0 to T5 | Evidence tiers. T0 money actually moved. T1 law and binding rules. T2 officials' own words. T3 market prices. T4 specialist interpretation. T5 commentary |
| Confirmed, Plausible, Speculative, Refuted | Status of a claim, set by its best evidence tier |
| I0 to I4, IX | Inference grades for a single link: documented, footprint, structural, pattern, conjecture. IX cannot be tested and is never published |
| H0 to H3 | Horizons: now to 2028, 2028 to 2035, 2035 to 2050, post-scarcity |
| S1 to S4 | Scenarios: dollar extension, a neutral reserve layer, an energy or compute standard, a disorderly reset |
| UHI | Universal High Income, the idea that AI and robots make goods cheap enough for a high basic income |
| Say/do gap | The difference between what an actor says and what its money actually does |

## The current headline, Intelligence stack (as of 2026-10-22)

Energy will not replace the dollar you spend. It is replacing gold's old job: the scarce physical thing that caps how much real output any money can claim. In an AI and robot economy output scales with delivered power, and power cannot be printed. Energy is also entering the system as collateral inside the dollar (power plants, data centers, PPAs and GPUs backing dollar loans, compute futures settling in dollars), a "watt-dollar" descended from the 1974 petrodollar. It fails as spendable money because it is perishable, location-bound and getting cheaper. Musk's "currency measured in wattage and tonnage" is a post-scarcity accounting idea close to the 1930s Technocracy plan, and its real problem is who allocates. Confidence 65 (Plausible).
