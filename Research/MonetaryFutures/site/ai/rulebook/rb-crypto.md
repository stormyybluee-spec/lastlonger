# Crypto: Set 2 money, dissected

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 20. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-crypto.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-crypto . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Bitcoin, stablecoins and twenty million other tokens, sorted into three families, tested against the jobs of money, and taken apart with six questions you can use on any coin you ever hear about.*

**In short**
- Chapter 1 said money is a trusted record of who can claim what. Crypto is a new way to keep that record: a shared ledger that no single bank or government runs.
- There are three very different families. Bitcoin is a scarce asset that is nobody's debt. Stablecoins are digital dollars backed by Treasury bills. Everything else is closer to a share in a platform, a loyalty point or a lottery ticket.
- Lumping them together as 'crypto' is the most common mistake. Each family answers the money questions differently.
- So far Set 2 is not replacing Set 1. Stablecoins carry dollars onto new rails, and Bitcoin competes with gold as a neutral store. Set 1 is absorbing Set 2.
- To judge any token, ask six questions: who issues it, whose debt is it, what anchors it, which job of money does it do, who can freeze it, and where does its return come from.

**Five words for this chapter.** 
- **Ledger:** A record of who owns what. Every money in history has been one.
- **Blockchain:** A ledger copied across thousands of computers, updated in blocks and very hard to change afterward.
- **Token:** A unit recorded on a blockchain. It can stand for a coin, a dollar, a share or a vote.
- **Keys:** The secret password that moves your tokens. Lose it and nobody can restore it.
- **Stablecoin:** A token built to stay worth $1, usually backed by dollars and Treasury bills.

### Why crypto matters for what money is

Go back to chapter 1. Money is not the paper or the metal. It is a trusted record of who can claim what, and the fight has always been over who keeps the record: the temple, the king, the goldsmith, the central bank, the commercial bank. Crypto's real invention, published in 2008, is a record kept by a network instead of a keeper. The rules are written in code, anyone can check the ledger, and no single party can quietly edit it.

> **Picture this.** On 3 January 2009 the first Bitcoin block was created with a newspaper headline written into it: 'Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks', from The Times of London. Whoever built it was making a point in the middle of the financial crisis: here is a money whose ledger no bank can lose and no government can inflate. The creator, known only as Satoshi Nakamoto, has never been identified.

That is why crypto belongs in this book. It is the first serious attempt since gold to create money that is nobody's debt and nobody's ledger. Chapter 19 showed that almost all modern money is someone's IOU. Bitcoin is the deliberate exception. Whether or not it succeeds, it forces every government to answer an old question in a new form: who should keep the record?

### Set 1 and Set 2

This project splits the money world into two sets. Set 1 is the old anchors: gold, government bonds and bank credit. Set 2 is the new rails: blockchains, stablecoins and tokens. After three research rounds the desk's finding is that Set 1 is absorbing Set 2, an acquisition rather than a merger. Stablecoins must hold Treasury bills by law, Wall Street sells Bitcoin through exchange-traded funds approved in January 2024, and asset managers are moving Treasury funds onto blockchains.

### The three families

*[Picture on the web page: Three families that share a technology and little else. Sizes from mid-October 2026.]*

### Family 1: Bitcoin, digital gold

- Fixed supply: the code caps it at 21 million coins. About 20.08 million had been mined by August 2026, and an estimated 10% to 20% of those are lost forever with forgotten keys.
- Slowing issuance: new coins are created as a reward for running the network, and the reward halves about every four years. The last halving, in April 2024, cut it to 3.125 coins per block.
- Nobody's debt: there is no issuer to redeem it and no one who owes you anything. On chapter 19's mirror it sits beside gold.
- Size: about $80,900 a coin and about $1.63 trillion in total on 15 October 2026, roughly 60% of all crypto by value.
- Wild swings: it fell about 77% from its November 2021 peak to November 2022, and has had several falls of that size.
- Governments are circling: a March 2025 executive order created a US Strategic Bitcoin Reserve from coins already seized in court cases. The Treasury Secretary said in August 2025 the US would not buy more.

Test it against the three jobs of money. As a store of value it is a real candidate, scarce and owed by no one, but its swings are far too large for most people's savings. As a medium of exchange it is rarely used, because, as chapter 9 showed with the 10,000-bitcoin pizza, people spend the money that melts and keep the one they expect to grow. As a unit of account it barely exists: almost nothing is priced in Bitcoin. Its realistic job is a neutral store of value next to gold.

### Family 2: Stablecoins, dollars on new rails

A stablecoin is a token that tries to stay worth exactly $1. A company holds dollars and short-term Treasury bills, issues a token for each dollar deposited and destroys it when the dollar is taken back out. In October 2026 stablecoins totaled about $303.8 billion, led by Tether's USDT and Circle's USDC. Under the GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025 and taking full effect on 18 January 2027, compliant issuers must hold reserves in cash and Treasury bills of 93 days or less, and may not pay holders interest. The issuer keeps the interest on the bills.

> Stablecoins are Set 1 wearing Set 2 clothes. They carry the dollar to anyone with a phone, including savers in weak-currency countries like those in chapter 12, and every new compliant stablecoin dollar is a new buyer of US Treasury bills. Offshore coins are not bound the same way: Tether's USDT also reported about $8.4 billion of Bitcoin and $19.1 billion of gold in its reserves on 30 September 2026. Stablecoins already hold about 4.27% of all T-bills. That is why Washington legalized private stablecoins while a January 2025 executive order blocked work on a Federal Reserve digital dollar for the public. A stablecoin is a dollar IOU, so on chapter 19's mirror it sits on the debt side.

Stable does not mean safe. In March 2023 USDC briefly fell to about 87 cents when $3.3 billion of its reserves were stuck at the failing Silicon Valley Bank. In May 2022 TerraUSD, a so-called algorithmic stablecoin backed mainly by its own sister token instead of dollars, collapsed from about $40 billion to almost nothing within days.

### Family 3: everything else

More than 20 million tokens had been created by 2025, most through do-it-yourself launch sites, and CoinGecko counts more than half of the ones it tracks as already dead. The survivors follow a handful of business models.

| Type | What it is | Business goal | Closest old cousin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Platform tokens | Fuel to run apps on a blockchain, such as Ether (about $288B in October 2026) | Collect fees from everyone who uses the network | A share in a toll road |
| Exchange and company tokens | Issued by a crypto business | Discounts, loyalty, raising money | Loyalty points, or shares without shareholder rights |
| Governance tokens | Votes over how a protocol is run | Steer and fund the project | Voting shares |
| Tokenized real assets | Bonds, funds or property recorded on a blockchain, such as BlackRock's BUIDL | Faster, cheaper settlement | The same asset with a new ledger |
| Meme coins | Jokes and online communities | Attention and speculation | A lottery ticket |

Tokenized US Treasury funds grew about fiftyfold from early 2024 to about $16 billion by August 2026, which is the clearest sign of Set 1 moving onto Set 2 rails. Most of the rest are not money at all in the sense of chapter 1. They are claims on a business, a network or a hope.

> **Not crypto, but a cousin.** A central bank digital currency uses some of the same technology for the opposite purpose: the state keeps the ledger and can see every payment. China's e-CNY is the largest. Since January 2026 its wallet balances count as deposits at commercial banks and can earn interest. Bitcoin was built to take the ledger away from the state. A central bank digital currency brings it closer.

### How to dissect any token: six questions

*[Picture on the web page: Six questions that work on any coin, old or new. The last one catches most scams.]*

1. Who issues it, and can they make more? Find the supply rule and who is able to change it.
2. Whose debt is it? If someone must redeem it, it is an IOU and you are trusting them. If no one owes it, its value rests only on demand.
3. What anchors its value? Reserves, scarcity, fees from a working network, or nothing but attention.
4. Which job of money does it do: spend, store or measure? Or is it really a share, a coupon or a lottery ticket?
5. Who runs the ledger, and who can freeze or change it? An issuer that can freeze your coins is a bank in all but name.
6. Where does the return come from? If it pays a yield, someone must be paying it. If you cannot name who, the yield may be paid with newer investors' money.

*[Interactive tool on the web page: Try it. Pick a token and read its dissection. Compare Bitcoin with a stablecoin, then with a meme coin.]*

> Question six would have saved a lot of people in 2022. Terra's Anchor platform paid about 20% a year on its stablecoin when most dollar savings paid under 2%, and at its peak held about $14 billion. The return was topped up from reserves rather than earned, so when the reserves ran low the whole structure collapsed. The same question applied to the FTX exchange, which collapsed in November 2022 after customer deposits were moved to its sister trading firm.

### Where crypto sits on the layers of money

*[Picture on the web page: Bitcoin sits beside gold at the narrow, solid bottom because it is nobody's debt. Stablecoins sit on top of Treasuries. Most other tokens belong with the riskiest claims near the top.]*

- **Myth:** Crypto is backed by nothing, so it is all worthless.
- **Reality:** Some tokens are backed by nothing and are worth nothing. Stablecoins are backed by dollars and bills. Bitcoin is backed by nothing in the way gold is backed by nothing: its value rests on scarcity and demand. Judge each one with the six questions, not by the label.

- **Myth:** Bitcoin will replace the dollar.
- **Reality:** Not as things stand. Almost nothing is priced in Bitcoin, few people spend it, and its swings are too large for wages and rent. Its realistic job is a neutral store of value next to gold. The challenger on the spending side is the stablecoin, and that is the dollar itself.

### Why it matters for the future this desk studies

- It tests who keeps the record. Every government now has to choose between private stablecoins, a state digital currency, or leaving the old bank ledger alone.
- It extends the dollar. Stablecoins are becoming one of the largest new sources of demand for US Treasury bills, the captive demand Level 2 chapter 1 described.
- It offers a neutral store. If trust in all government debts weakens, Bitcoin and gold are the two assets that are nobody's IOU. The desk's scenario of a neutral reserve layer stands at about 15% to 2028, 23% from 2028 to 2035 and 28% from 2035 to 2050.
- It moves the ledger itself. Tokenized Treasuries and funds could make settlement instant, which changes who earns the float, the fees and the data.

### The owner's question: what if the dollar ruler disappears?

> **The owner's question.** Today everything, a share, a company bond, a token, has an easy measuring stick, because the dollar is there. Remove that layer, and we would probably learn to price things in Bitcoin. But right now crypto keeps getting absorbed into Set 1, because most of the world still settles in currencies. If tokens get good enough that nobody needs to convert Solana into stablecoins to buy things, and so many tokens can exist, where does their value come from? It still seems to come from base-layer credit and bond money. And if anyone lucky enough, a lottery winner or someone with a big enough yield, can launch their own token, it looks like a utopia. The opportunity seems enormous, and this is where it stops making sense.

This is chapter 1's question in its newest form: what makes something money, and who decides how everything is measured? It untangles in five pieces, followed by the questions worth carrying with you.

### 1. The ruler is the hardest job of money

Money has three jobs: spend, store and measure. Crypto has made real progress on the first two. The third, being the ruler everything is priced in, is the hardest, and no token has won it. When you pay with a token through a crypto debit card, the shop's price was set in dollars and your token was sold for dollars at the till. The conversion is invisible, not absent. The European Central Bank finds crypto and stablecoins below 1% of in-store payments in the euro area, in both 2024 and 2026.

*[Picture on the web page: Why the dollar keeps the ruler: every link in an ordinary life is a dollar contract.]*

The ruler belongs to whatever debts and taxes are written in. Your salary, your rent, your landlord's mortgage, the bank's loans, the government's bonds and your taxes are all dollar contracts. A token can become the ruler only when those debts start being written in it. Chapter 18 showed that taxes give a money its value. El Salvador tested the opposite. It made Bitcoin legal tender in 2021, but its debts, wages and taxes stayed in dollars, use stayed low, and in January 2025, as part of a $1.4 billion IMF deal, it made accepting Bitcoin optional and dropped the state's promise to convert it to dollars.

### 2. Many monies, one ruler: why markets always rebuild a hub

*[Picture on the web page: Six monies with no common ruler need 15 exchange rates. With one ruler in the middle, each needs only one price.]*

If every token traded directly against every other, each pair would need its own price. With 10 monies that is 45 exchange rates. With 1,000 it is 499,500. With the more than 20 million tokens created by 2025 it would be about 200 trillion. Nobody can think in that many prices, so markets always choose one hub that everything else is priced through. That is how barter turned into money in chapter 1, and it is happening again inside crypto, where most trading runs through a dollar stablecoin, Ether or Bitcoin acting as the hub.

*[Interactive tool on the web page: Try it. Slide from 2 monies to 20 million and watch how many exchange rates you would need without a common ruler.]*

> **This happened before.** From 1837 to 1863 America had no national paper currency. Hundreds of banks printed their own notes, and each note traded at its own discount depending on the bank's reputation and how far away it was. Shopkeepers kept bank note reporters, booklets listing what each note was worth and which ones were forgeries. It was a world of many tokens. It ended when Congress taxed state bank notes at 10% in 1865, and their circulation fell from $143 million to $4 million within two years. The ruler went back to one national dollar.

The economist Friedrich Hayek argued in 1976, in The Denationalisation of Money, that private monies should compete and the public would choose the most stable one. Crypto is the first large-scale test of his idea. So far the public keeps choosing the most stable token on offer, and that token is a dollar.

### 3. Where a token's value really comes from

Your instinct is half right. Today almost every token's price is a dollar price, and most of the money that buys tokens was born as dollar credit, so crypto prices tend to rise when dollar credit is easy and fall when it tightens. But the deeper source of any money's value is the base layer from chapter 19: the real goods, work and assets people will hand over for it. A token is worth what someone will give up to hold it. If everyone held tokens and nobody produced anything, every token would buy nothing.

> Bitcoin is scarce, but crypto is not. One coin can be capped at 21 million while anyone can launch a new one in minutes, and more than 20 million have been launched. Each new token is a new claim competing for the same real goods, the same attention and the same buyers' dollars. That is chapter 19's picture again: the claims multiply, and the base layer does not.

### 4. The lottery winner who launches a token

*[Picture on the web page: The life of a typical launch. Bars show the price. The dashed arrow is where the dollars go.]*

Launching a token is printing money, and the profit has an old name: seigniorage, from chapter 16. A token costs almost nothing to create. When its founders sell part of it for dollars, they turn newly printed units into real buying power, exactly as a government does when it issues money. That buying power comes from the buyers. Unless the token's network produces something people pay for, the founders' gain is the later buyers' loss. That is why the utopia feels so big: the power to issue money has been opened to everyone, but the pool of real goods all those monies can buy has not grown with it.

| What a project does | Creates value or captures it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Runs a network people pay to use | Creates | Users pay fees for a real service, such as settlement or storage |
| Moves a real asset onto a faster ledger | Creates | Cheaper, quicker settlement saves real costs |
| Launches a token and sells it to later buyers | Captures | Seigniorage moves dollars from late buyers to early ones |
| Pays a high yield from new deposits | Captures, then collapses | Returns come from newer money, as at Terra in 2022 |

### 5. So where is the real opportunity?

- New rails: cheaper, faster payments across borders, especially for people in weak-currency countries, mostly using dollar stablecoins.
- Settlement: tokenized Treasuries and funds that settle in seconds, cutting into the float and fees banks earn today.
- Money for machines: AI agents paying each other per request, which this project found already happens mostly in dollar tokens.
- Early title to new networks: owning part of a network people truly pay to use, before it is titled. Chapter 19's rule applies: look for the next base layer before it is titled.
- The ruler itself: if trust in all government debt ever broke, the next ruler would be whatever is hardest and most widely accepted. Gold did that job for thousands of years, Bitcoin is a candidate, and this project's founding question asks whether energy could be one. The desk puts an energy or compute standard at about 1% to 2028, 3% from 2028 to 2035 and 10% from 2035 to 2050.

The opportunity is real but narrower than it looks. Most of the lasting value is in building rails and owning networks that produce something. Much of the rest is seigniorage passing from late buyers to early ones.

### Questions worth carrying with you

**Check yourself.** Q: If your salary were paid in Solana, what would you pay your rent in, and what is your landlord's mortgage written in?
A: Dollars, and dollars. Every link in the chain is a dollar contract, so the token is converted somewhere even when you never see it. The ruler changes only when the debts change.

**Check yourself.** Q: If Bitcoin became the ruler, what would happen to everyone with a mortgage?
A: If Bitcoin kept rising against goods, prices in Bitcoin would fall and debts written in Bitcoin would get heavier every year. Chapter 9's deflation debate applies: borrowers lose, savers win, and with no central bank there is no lender of last resort in a crisis.

**Check yourself.** Q: Who would set interest rates in a world of a thousand tokens?
A: No one centrally. Each token's lenders and borrowers would set their own, and the most trusted token's rate would become the benchmark, the way the Treasury yield is today. The hub returns.

**Check yourself.** Q: When AI agents pay each other, which ruler will they use?
A: So far the dollar, through stablecoins. Machines need stable prices even more than people do, because they price millions of tiny transactions. A machine economy is more likely to strengthen the dollar ruler than replace it, unless energy or compute itself becomes the unit.

**Check yourself.** Q: If anyone can launch a token, what stops the world from drowning in money?
A: Nothing stops the launching. What stops the drowning is that buyers ignore most tokens, which is why more than half are already dead. Free issuance makes money cheap to create and trust expensive to earn.

> General education, not advice. Crypto adds risks that bank money does not have: lost keys with no reset button, hacks, scams that target newcomers, exchanges that fail with customers' money inside, and rules that can change by executive order. Anyone who holds it should know which of the three families they own, and the answer to all six questions.

**Check yourself.** Q: Is a dollar stablecoin closer to Bitcoin or to a bank deposit?
A: To a bank deposit. It is an IOU from an issuer, backed by dollars and Treasury bills, worth a dollar only as long as the issuer can redeem it. Bitcoin is nobody's IOU.

**Check yourself.** Q: A new token promises 30% a year, paid in the token itself. Which of the six questions matters most?
A: Question six: where does the return come from? If the yield is paid in newly created tokens or from newer investors' money, the high number is a warning, not a feature.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why do people spend dollars and stablecoins but hold Bitcoin?
A: Gresham's law from chapter 9: people spend the money they expect to lose value and keep the one they expect to gain.
