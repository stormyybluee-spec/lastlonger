# The view from a smaller currency: Malaysia, savings and where money lands first

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 12. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-em.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-em . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Why a calm local price tag can hide a shrinking passport, how compulsory savings fund the home government, why talent and capital crowd into a few hubs, and whether Singapore was luck, strategy or someone else's plan.*

Most of this Rulebook looks at money from the top, from Washington and the dollar. This chapter looks at it from the ground in a smaller economy. Malaysia is the worked example because its story touches every idea in this book, but the same logic applies to Thailand, Indonesia, the Philippines, Turkey, South Africa and most of the roughly 180 currencies on earth.

### The comfortable picture

A Malaysian worker sees a reassuring picture. Consumer inflation was 1.8% in September 2026 by the statistics department's count. The Employees Provident Fund (EPF), where most private-sector workers' retirement money must go by law, paid a 6.15% dividend for 2025 on investment assets of about RM1.41 trillion. Savings grow faster than prices. Why worry?

The picture is true, but it is measured with only one ruler: the ringgit, inside Malaysia. There is a second ruler most people never check.

### Two rulers: the home price tag and the passport price tag

*[Picture on the web page: What one ringgit buys abroad, then and now. Rates are approximate. The 1997 figures are before the Asian crisis.]*

Before the 1997 Asian crisis a US dollar cost about 2.50 ringgit. In October 2026 it costs about 4.08. So a ringgit buys roughly 39% fewer dollars than it did 29 years ago. Against the Singapore dollar the slide is steeper, from about 1.75 ringgit per Singapore dollar in early 1997 to about 3.2 in 2026. Inside Malaysia the loss is hidden, because local prices move gently. Outside, it shows up the moment you pay an overseas tuition bill, a foreign hospital, a holiday, an imported phone or anything priced in dollars.

- Imported goods: fuel, food ingredients, machines, medicines and electronics are often priced in dollars. A weaker ringgit raises their cost in ringgit.
- Education and health abroad: a family saving for a child's degree in the US or Britain is really saving in dollars or pounds, whatever its account says.
- Travel and work options: the same salary buys fewer days abroad, and moving to a richer country costs more.
- Assets priced globally: shares of global companies, gold and Bitcoin all cost more ringgit when the ringgit falls.

> **Be fair to both sides.** Currencies swing both ways. The ringgit weakened to about 4.80 per dollar in early 2024, its weakest since the 1998 crisis, then strengthened to about 4.08 by October 2026. Short moves reverse. For a lifetime of savings the long trend is what counts, and since 1997 the passport ruler has shrunk.

### Why the home price tag looks so calm

- Subsidies and price controls. The government absorbs part of the cost of fuel, electricity and some foods. In September 2026, for example, protection on household electricity bills was widened from 600 to 800 kWh a month, which helped pull the utilities index down. The cost does not disappear. It moves onto the government's budget, and later onto taxes, debt or a subsidy cut.
- The basket. Official inflation tracks an average household's local shopping. It does not include overseas school fees or the cost of moving abroad.
- Local wages and rents set many prices, and they move slowly.

### How Washington reaches a Malaysian wallet

1. The Fed raises rates or the Treasury sells more bonds, so dollar yields rise.
2. Global money leaves smaller markets to earn more in dollars, more safely. Foreign holders sell local bonds and shares.
3. The local currency falls. Imports get dearer, so inflation is imported even though nothing changed at home.
4. The local central bank must choose: raise its own rates to defend the currency, which hurts local borrowers and businesses, or let the currency fall and accept dearer imports.
5. Either way, households pay for a decision made in Washington. When the Fed eases or the dollar weakens, the flow reverses, which is part of why the ringgit strengthened in 2025 and 2026.

### The rule no small economy can escape

*[Picture on the web page: The impossible trinity. A country can hold only two corners at once. Each side of the triangle is a choice with a cost.]*

Economists call it the impossible trinity. A country cannot at the same time fix its exchange rate, let money flow freely in and out, and set its own interest rates. It must give one up. The US keeps free flows and its own rates, and lets the dollar float. Malaysia in September 1998 chose differently: it imposed capital controls and pegged the ringgit at 3.80 to the dollar, keeping control of its rates. The peg lasted until July 2005. Singapore chose a third path: it keeps money flowing freely and steers its exchange rate, and accepts that its interest rates largely follow world rates.

### Captive savings: the EPF and the home government

The EPF is one of the largest pension funds in Asia, and also the biggest single holder of Malaysian government bonds, with about 28% of them according to the fund's statement for December 2023. Its policy is to place about 70% to 80% of new money inside Malaysia. About 39% of its assets were overseas in late 2025, and in 2024 the overseas part, about 37% of assets, earned about half of all investment income.

> This is the same captive-demand playbook Level 2 chapter 1 showed in Washington, where stablecoins are required by law to hold Treasury bills. A government with a large pool of compulsory savings has a reliable buyer for its debt at home. That keeps its borrowing cheap and its markets steady. The cost is that workers' retirement money is concentrated in the same economy that pays their wages, in the same currency.

To be fair, the EPF has done well in ringgit, and the leak is slower than many fear. The ringgit's fall against the dollar since 1997 works out to roughly 1.7% a year, so a 6% ringgit dividend is still very roughly 4% a year in dollar terms. The leak is real but quiet. It matters most for people whose future spending will happen abroad.

### When the local economy hits bottom

The real test comes in a crisis. In 1997 and 1998 the ringgit fell from about 2.50 to about 4.88 per dollar at its worst in January 1998, roughly halving what Malaysian savings were worth in world terms within months. Stocks crashed, companies with dollar debts were crushed, and the government reached for capital controls. Malaysia recovered, but the lesson stayed: in a crisis, people with only local assets and a local passport have the fewest exits. People with income or savings in a strong currency, or skills that are paid in one, have options.

Malaysia remains deeply tied to the outside world. Its trade is larger than its whole economy, it is a key link in the world's chip supply chain, and its exporters earn dollars. That openness brought decades of growth. It also means a US recession, a tariff war or a Fed shock reaches Malaysian jobs within months. The same holds for most economies outside the few big hubs.

### Why money and talent crowd into a few places

*[Picture on the web page: New dollars ripple outward. The closer to the center, the earlier they arrive and the more they buy when they do.]*

Chapter 16 describes the Cantillon effect: whoever receives new money first spends it before prices rise. At world scale, new dollars are created in New York and Washington and land first in US banks, US markets and a handful of hubs such as London, Singapore, Hong Kong, Dubai and Zurich. Commodity exporters and factory economies receive them later, through trade. Households in smaller economies receive them last, often after their currency has already moved.

- Capital goes where returns, rule of law and exits are best.
- Talent follows capital, because that is where the jobs, the strong-currency salaries and the investors are.
- Capital follows talent, because investors want to be near founders, engineers and deal makers.
- The loop feeds itself. Each hub's lead makes the next arrival more likely. That is why successful businesses and entrepreneurs end up in the same few cities out of more than 190 countries.

Malaysia feels this loop directly. About 1.8 million Malaysians live abroad, and roughly 60% of them are in Singapore, according to a 2024 New Straits Times report citing World Bank work. The World Bank's chief economist Indermit Gill pointed to the currency gap as one reason educated Malaysians move there.

### Singapore: anomaly, strategy, or someone else's plan?

Singapore is an island with no farmland, little water and no natural resources, separated from Malaysia in August 1965. Within two generations it became one of the richest places on earth. Some people decode this as a plan by American money managers to plant a financial outpost in Asia. Here is the record, run through this project's decoding method.

| What happened | Who drove it | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| 1960 to 1961: Dutch economist Albert Winsemius leads a UN mission, reports in 1961 and becomes Singapore's long-time economic adviser | Singapore's government, with UN help | A home-grown plan with outside advice |
| 1968: Singapore abolishes the tax on interest paid to foreign depositors. Bank of America opens the first Asian Currency Unit, starting the Asian Dollar Market | Singapore's government and a US bank, together | Singapore made itself the dollar's hub in the Asian time zone, and US banks were glad to use it |
| 1971: the Monetary Authority of Singapore is set up. From 1981 monetary policy steers the exchange rate instead of interest rates | Singapore | A deliberate choice inside the impossible trinity |
| 1974 and 1981: Temasek and GIC are founded to invest national savings around the world | Singapore | National savings sent abroad by design, unlike most countries |
| 1990: an agreement gives US forces access to Singapore facilities. Changi Naval Base later hosts US aircraft carriers | Singapore and the US | A security umbrella traded for strategic usefulness |
| Location on the Strait of Malacca, one of the busiest sea lanes on earth | Geography | An advantage no plan could create |

> **The decode verdict.** Planted by American money managers: D3, folklore, about 10% likely as stated. No evidence shows Washington designed Singapore. Strategically welcomed by them: D1, decoded-strong, about 80% likely. Singapore's leaders deliberately made their island useful to the dollar system and to US security in Asia, and the US, its banks and its navy had every reason to support that. It was Singapore's plan, built to fit the American system. Small size let the plan be run tightly, and geography made it pay.

That is why Singapore is hard to copy. It needed a strategic location, leaders able to make long-term choices, rule of law trusted by foreign money, English as a working language, and the willingness to plug into the world's dominant system rather than fight it. Most countries have one or two of these. Very few have all of them at once.

### Tying it together

1. A decision in Washington moves dollar yields.
2. Global money flows toward or away from smaller markets, and their currencies move.
3. Home inflation stays calm, partly because subsidies absorb the shock, while the passport price tag changes.
4. Compulsory savings such as the EPF recycle most local money into the home government and home market, in the home currency.
5. Money reaches the hubs first. Talent follows money, and money follows talent.
6. In a crisis, people with only local assets and local options have the fewest exits.
7. A few places, like Singapore, escaped by choosing to become hubs themselves. That took geography, discipline and decades.

- **Myth:** My country's inflation is only 2%, so my savings are safe.
- **Reality:** Safe on the home ruler. Measure them also on the passport ruler: what they buy in dollars, in a foreign degree or in the cost of moving. Both rulers matter, and the second one moves with decisions made abroad.

> This is general education, not advice. The lesson people usually draw is to know which ruler their future spending will use. If your life will be lived at home, the home ruler matters most. If your children may study or work abroad, part of your savings is really owed in another currency. Skills that are paid in global terms are the most portable asset a person can own.

**Check yourself.** Q: The Fed raises rates and the ringgit falls 10% against the dollar. Malaysian inflation stays at 2%. Has anything changed for a family saving for a child's degree in the United States?
A: Yes. The degree is priced in dollars, so it now costs about 11% more ringgit. The home price tag stayed calm, but the passport price tag rose.
