# Who really creates money: banks, the Fed and the Treasury

Rulebook Level 2 (The machinery), chapter 02. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-moneymake.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-moneymake . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Bank loans are one door, the busiest one in normal years. Level 2 adds the others: the Fed, the Treasury, the sponges that soak money up, the shredders that destroy it, the brakes on each printer, and how other countries do it.*

**In short**
- Money lives on two floors. Upstairs, banks and the Treasury hold accounts at the Federal Reserve. Downstairs, you and every business hold bank deposits. You can only spend the downstairs kind.
- In normal years banks create most new downstairs money when they lend (1971 to 2007, 87% to 96% of the two printers combined). They also create it when they buy a bond or anything else from someone who is not a bank. In 2008 to 2014 and 2020 to 2021 the Fed did most of it.
- The Fed creates upstairs money, called reserves, every time it buys a bond. Whether new downstairs money appears too depends on who sold the bond: a bank (no) or a pension fund or other nonbank (yes).
- The Treasury moves money between the floors. Selling bonds and collecting taxes pulls deposits up into its Fed account. Spending pushes them back down.
- So money is not created only by bank loans. In 2020 and 2021 the Treasury and the Fed together created most of the $4.7 trillion rise in bank deposits.
- The Fed is not allowed to buy new bonds straight from the Treasury. It must buy in the open market, through 25 primary dealers.
- In short: banks are the everyday printer. The government and the Fed together are the emergency printer, and when both switch it on, as in 2020, it is bigger than anything the banks do.
- From 1971 to 2026, bank loans grew about $13.79T and the Fed's bonds about $6.43T: two thirds downstairs, one third upstairs, with the upstairs third arriving in two bursts, 2008 to 2014 and 2020 to 2021.

**Five key words.** 
- **Reserves:** Banks' own accounts at the Fed. Upstairs money. About $2.97 trillion on 21 October 2026. Only banks and a few institutions can hold them.
- **Deposits:** The money in your bank account. Downstairs money, a promise from your bank. About $19.6 trillion at US commercial banks.
- **Treasury General Account:** The government's checking account at the Fed. About $945 billion on 21 October 2026. Money in it is out of public hands.
- **Primary dealer:** One of 25 big dealers that must bid at every Treasury auction and that trade directly with the Fed.
- **Open market:** The market where bonds already issued change hands. The only place the Fed may buy Treasuries.
- **Risk-weighted assets:** A bank's assets, each scaled by how risky the rules say it is. Capital rules ask for owners' money of at least a set share of this total.

Level 1's "How money is born today" showed that a bank loan creates a deposit. In normal years that is the most common way money is born, but not the only one. This chapter finishes the picture. It adds the Fed, the Treasury and the dealers in between, and shows exactly which moves create money you can spend and which only shuffle money that banks use among themselves.

### Part 1 of 3. Two floors and three printers

### The whole idea in three rows

Here is the whole idea before any numbers. Steps 1 to 3 explain each row, and after Step 3 the full table puts sizes on every channel in four kinds of year.

| Who | Creates money you can spend when | Destroys it when |
|---|---|---|
| Banks | they lend, or buy anything from someone who is not a bank | loans are repaid, or they sell to someone who is not a bank |
| The Fed | it buys bonds. Reserves rise every time, your deposits only if the seller is not a bank | it shrinks its bonds (QT) |
| The Treasury | it spends | it collects taxes or sells bonds to savers, until it spends the money again |

### Two floors of money

Picture the dollar system as a building with two floors. Upstairs are the Fed's books. Banks hold reserves there, the Treasury holds its account there, and money funds can park cash there overnight. Downstairs are the public's books: deposits at banks, plus paper cash. When you pay rent or buy groceries, you move downstairs money. Banks settle with each other upstairs.

> **Picture this.** Think of a big market hall. Upstairs is the wholesale floor, where only licensed traders deal, and they settle in special tokens issued by the hall's owner, the Fed. Downstairs is the shop floor, where everyone shops with receipts issued by the traders, which are bank deposits. Some deals happen only upstairs, between traders and the owner. Some happen only downstairs. And some go through the staircase and change the amount of receipts on the shop floor. This chapter is a guide to the staircase.

**Check yourself.** Q: Can a family spend bank reserves at a shop?
A: No. Reserves are accounts at the Fed that only banks and a few institutions hold. A family spends deposits or cash. Reserves only matter to the family when a move also changes deposits.

### Step 1. Banks create deposits, and not only by lending

When a bank lends you $100, it adds a $100 loan to its assets and a $100 deposit to its debts at the same moment. New downstairs money appears. When you repay, both disappear. That is Level 1 chapter 6. The second way is less known. When a bank buys a $100 bond from a pension fund, it pays by crediting the fund's account with a new $100 deposit. No loan was made, but money was created all the same. When a bank sells a bond to a nonbank, money is destroyed.

> "Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money." (Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy, 2014)

1. A bank lends $100 to a household: the household's deposit rises $100. New money.
2. The household repays: the deposit and the loan both shrink $100. Money destroyed.
3. A bank buys a $100 bond from a pension fund: the fund's deposit rises $100. New money without a loan.
4. Upstairs, nothing changed in total. Reserves only move between banks when the money is spent at another bank.

**Check yourself.** Q: Your bank buys a building from a developer for $1 million and pays into the developer's account at the same bank. Did the money supply change?
A: Yes, it rose by $1 million. The bank gained an asset (the building) and a new deposit liability (the developer's balance). Any purchase by a bank from a nonbank creates a deposit.

### Step 2. The Fed creates reserves when it buys, and the seller decides the rest

The Fed holds $4.57 trillion of Treasuries. It got them by buying in the open market from the 25 primary dealers, paying with reserves it creates on the spot, and by rolling over bonds as they mature. That is real money creation, but upstairs. Whether it reaches downstairs depends on who sold the bond in the end.

| Who sells the bond to the Fed | Upstairs (reserves) | Downstairs (deposits) |
|---|---|---|
| A bank | Up $100 | No change. The bank swapped a bond for reserves. |
| A pension fund, insurer, money fund or foreign investor | Up $100 | Up $100. The seller's bank gets reserves and credits the seller's deposit. |
| A dealer selling from its own stock | Up $100 | Up $100 at first. If the dealer uses the cash to repay a loan from a bank, the deposit disappears again. |

*[Picture on the web page: Two paths for the same $100 purchase. From a bank, only reserves rise. From a pension fund, reserves and deposits both rise.]*

> "When the Federal Reserve purchases securities from a nonbank seller, it creates new bank deposits." (Castro, Cavallo and Zarutskie, Federal Reserve FEDS Notes, 2022)

> The primary dealer in the middle is only the pipe. Many of the 25 primary dealers belong to big banking groups, and many act for customers. To know whether a Fed purchase added money you can spend, follow the cash to whoever holds it at the end. In 2020 to 2022 much of the selling came from nonbanks, which is why quantitative easing then added deposits as well as reserves.

**Check yourself.** Q: The Fed buys $1 billion of Treasuries, and the sellers turn out to be three banks. How much new money can the public spend?
A: None directly. Bank reserves rose $1 billion, which is upstairs money. Public deposits change only if the sellers are nonbanks, or later when banks spend or lend.

### Step 3. The Treasury moves money between the floors

The Treasury keeps its account at the Fed, upstairs. When a fund buys a new Treasury bond at auction, the fund's deposit falls, its bank's reserves fall, and the Treasury's account rises. Money has gone upstairs. When the Treasury pays a Social Security check, the reverse happens: the account falls, and the retiree's deposit and the bank's reserves rise. Taxes work like an auction: deposits go up the stairs. You can watch it happen. In the week to 21 October 2026, on weekly averages, the Treasury's account rose $69 billion and bank reserves fell $60 billion.

*[Picture on the web page: Auctions and taxes lift money into the Treasury's account at the Fed. Spending sends it back down. A deficit means more goes down than comes up.]*

So does a budget deficit create money? It depends on who buys the new bonds. In fiscal 2026 the deficit was $1.99 trillion.

| Who buys the new debt | Effect on the public's deposits |
|---|---|
| Households and funds, paying from their deposits | None overall. The deposits taken at auction come back when the Treasury spends. The buyers now hold a bond instead. |
| Banks | Deposits rise. Banks pay with reserves, the spending lands in someone's account. |
| The Fed, indirectly, buying later from nonbanks | Deposits and reserves both rise. This is the legal route to printing money for a deficit. |
| Money funds using cash parked at the Fed | Deposits rise. Cash that was upstairs comes down when the Treasury spends. |

**Check yourself.** Q: The Treasury runs a $100 deficit and a pension fund buys the whole $100 bond with its bank deposit. What happens to deposits?
A: Nothing overall. The fund's deposit fell $100 at the auction, and the Treasury's spending added $100 to someone else's deposit. The private sector holds $100 more in bonds, not more money.

### Part 2 of 3. Sponges, shredders, brakes and other printers

### Step 4. Drains, sponges and new pipes

- The overnight reverse repo facility: money funds can lend cash to the Fed overnight. While it sits there, it is out of the banks. It peaked at about $2.4 trillion in late September 2022. On 21 October 2026 it held $0.2 billion, so this drain is empty.
- The Treasury General Account: every rise drains reserves and often deposits, every fall adds them back.
- The standing repo facility (since July 2021): a door that works the other way. Banks and dealers can swap Treasuries for new reserves at a set rate whenever they need them, which limits how far short-term rates can spike in a squeeze.
- Stablecoins: buying a dollar token moves your deposit to the issuer, who buys Treasury bills. Bank deposits do not grow, and if the bill comes from a bank or the Treasury, they shrink. The token is a new private dollar outside M2.
- Interest on reserves: the Fed pays banks interest by creating new reserves, about $115 billion a year at current rates (desk estimate). Deposits grow only if banks pass it on.
- Fed profits and losses: when the Fed earns more than it pays, it sends the profit to the Treasury. From late 2022 it paid more than it earned and built up a $245.9 billion IOU to itself by January 2026. That IOU is now shrinking ($232.7 billion), so it again earns slightly more than it pays, and payments to the Treasury restart once the IOU is cleared. The 'taxes go to the Fed's owners' story has it backwards.

**Check yourself.** Q: A money fund pulls $50 billion out of the Fed's reverse repo facility to buy new Treasury bills, and the Treasury spends the cash. What happens downstairs?
A: Deposits rise about $50 billion. The cash was upstairs at the Fed, it passed through the Treasury's account, and the spending landed in public accounts.

From June 2022 to October 2026 the Fed's bonds fell $2.01T, yet bank reserves fell only $0.39T. Where did the shrinkage go? Into the sponge. Money funds pulled $1.97T out of the Fed's reverse repo pool to buy Treasury bills, and the cash came back into the banks as the Treasury spent it. QT mostly emptied the sponge, not the banks. Now the sponge is dry, so any further drain hits bank reserves one for one. That is a large part of why the Fed stopped shrinking its Treasuries in December 2025 and has added $0.34T since.

### The shredders: six ways money is destroyed

1. Repaying a bank loan. The loan and the deposit both vanish.
2. Paying taxes. The deposit goes upstairs into the Treasury's account and comes back only when the Treasury spends.
3. Buying a new Treasury at auction with a deposit. The same as taxes, until the Treasury spends.
4. Fed QT. When a bond the Fed holds matures and the Treasury repays it with money raised from a nonbank, a deposit and reserves both disappear.
5. A bank selling a bond to a nonbank, or buying back its own shares or bonds. The buyer pays with a deposit, which the bank cancels.
6. Money moving into the Fed's reverse repo pool. It leaves the banks while it sits there. In 2020 to 2021 the pool rose $1.64T, more than twice the $0.68T of new bank loans in those two years.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: The Fed shrank its bonds by $2.01T from mid 2022, yet bank reserves fell only $0.39T. Where did the shrinkage go?
  A: Into an empty sponge: money funds drained $1.97T from the Fed's reverse repo pool to buy bills (Fed H.4.1). The sponge is now dry. (builds on [When markets fall: buckets, collateral and rotation](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-buckets.md))
- Q: When you pay your taxes, does the money still exist?
  A: It leaves the public's books and sits upstairs in the Treasury's account at the Fed, $945B on 21 October 2026, until it is spent again. (builds on [Nation Inc.: taxes, interest and the hubs](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-nation.md))
- Q: The Fed lost money for three years. Did anyone have to rescue it?
  A: No. It booked an IOU to itself, a deferred asset, which peaked at $245.9B in January 2026 and is now shrinking ($232.7B). It is earning slightly more than it pays again. (builds on [What money really is](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-01.md))

### Why the Fed cannot just buy from the Treasury

Section 14 of the Federal Reserve Act says US government bonds may be bought "only in the open market". Congress added that rule in 1935 so that the Treasury would have to face market buyers and could not finance itself at its own central bank. A wartime exception from 1942 allowed up to $5 billion of direct purchases. It was renewed several times, last in 1979, and lapsed in 1981. The Fed can still roll over bonds it already owns by taking new ones at auction, which gives the Treasury no new cash.

> A ban on direct buying is not a ban on financing. From 1942 to 1951 the Fed promised to keep bill rates at 0.375% and long bond yields under 2.5%, buying whatever the market sold at those prices. That financed the war debt cheaply without a single direct purchase. The Treasury-Fed Accord of 4 March 1951 ended the deal.

### The brakes on each printer

| Printer | Hard limit (law or rule) | Soft limit (price or demand) |
|---|---|---|
| Banks | Capital rules: owners' money of at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets plus buffers. Liquidity rules: enough safe assets for 30 days of stress. Reserve requirement: 0% since 26 March 2020 | Borrowers must want loans and pass checks. A loan must beat the bank's funding cost, which the Fed anchors by paying 3.90% on reserves |
| The Fed | May buy Treasuries only in the open market (Federal Reserve Act section 14, since 1935). Its mandate: maximum employment, stable prices, moderate long-term rates | Inflation. Buying bonds while inflation runs high fights its own mandate. The current chair calls QE a tool to use sparingly, if at all |
| The Treasury | Congress must approve spending. The debt limit caps total debt | Auctions must clear. The bond market sets the price, and net interest passed $1T in fiscal 2026 |
| Government plus Fed together | Both keys must turn: Congress spends, the Fed buys from nonbanks | Inflation and the bond market. 2020 to 2021 shows how fast it runs when both keys turn |

Notice the reserve requirement: zero since March 2020. The old textbook picture of banks lending out each deposit ten times over has no legal base in the US today. What stops banks is owners' capital, liquidity rules, the price of money and borrowers who must repay. What stops the emergency printer is a law that makes the Fed shop in the open market, a mandate that puts inflation first, and the bond market's price.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: US banks have had to hold zero reserves since March 2020. So what stops them lending forever?
  A: Capital rules (owners' money absorbs losses), liquidity rules, the 3.90% the Fed pays on reserves, which sets the cost of money, and borrowers who must want and repay loans. (builds on [How money is born today](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-06.md))
- Q: The Fed has been banned from buying bonds straight from the Treasury since 1935. How did it end up with more than half of the new debt of 2020 and 2021?
  A: It bought in the open market from dealers and investors, days or months after the auctions. In wartime, 1942 to 1951, it got the same result by capping yields. (builds on [The seasons of money](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-08.md))

### Money outside the banks

| Money-like claim | Size | Counted in M2? |
|---|---|---|
| Money market fund shares | $7.98T (Sep 2026) | Retail funds yes, institutional funds no |
| Repo, cash lent against bonds | about $12.6T outstanding on an average day (Q3 2025) | No |
| Stablecoins | about $308B (Aug 2026) | No |
| Dollars borrowed outside the US by non-US firms and governments (eurodollars) | $14T, 55% as bonds (Sep 2025) | No |

Who prints the $14 trillion of dollars borrowed outside America? Bond investors hold over half of it (55% at the end of September 2025, BIS). The rest is loans written by banks abroad, in London, Singapore or Kuala Lumpur, which create dollar loans and deposits on their own books exactly as a US bank does. They settle through accounts at US banks, and in a crisis they need dollars from the Fed's swap lines. So the Fed is the last-resort supplier, while private foreign banks and bond markets are the everyday sources of offshore dollars.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: Money market funds hold $7.98T, about 40% as much as all US bank deposits. Is that money?
  A: Partly. Retail funds are counted in M2, institutional funds are not, and both are mostly lent to the government. They work like money for their owners, but they rest on Treasuries and on bank deposits. (builds on [What money really is](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-01.md))
- Q: Who prints the dollars borrowed outside America?
  A: Mostly banks and bond markets abroad, about $14T at the end of September 2025 (BIS). The Fed steps in only in a crisis, through swap lines with other central banks. (builds on [How the world gets its dollars](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-04.md))

### Other printers abroad

The two-floor picture works everywhere, but the doors differ. In Malaysia a large share of changes in central bank money comes through the currency door: when Bank Negara buys dollars to steady the ringgit, it pays with new ringgit reserves, then usually soaks them back up. In Japan the central bank owns so much of the bond market that who sold a bond matters less than how much the Bank of Japan chose to buy.

### Read a country's debt mix: clues to where its money was born

A table of debt by country went round social media in late 2026. It splits each country's debt into what households, firms and the government owe, as a share of one year's output. It looks like a league table of who is most in debt. Read with Step 1 and Step 2 in mind, it is something more useful: a set of clues to where each country's money was born. It is not a full birth record, for reasons the next box sets out.

> **The statement.** Where household and firm debt is bank loans, each loan created a deposit on the day it was made, and each repayment destroyed one (Step 1). So the bank loan part of those two columns hints at who borrowed some of today's money into existence. The government column is different. It created money only where banks or the central bank bought the bonds (Step 2). Where pension funds or savers bought them, it moved money that already existed, though the buyers now own a bond the government owes. Three limits keep this from becoming a new simple story. The table is a stock built up over decades, like the water level in a bath. Money is created and destroyed as a flow, year by year, like the tap and the drain. The columns also count bonds bought by pension funds and other non-banks, and loans from abroad, which created no deposits at home. And one door is missing. When Bank Negara buys dollars to steady the ringgit, it creates ringgit at first with no local debt behind it.

> **Vietnam against Malaysia.** The post that carried the table asked: Vietnam's household debt is 18% of output, Malaysia's 70%, and both own about the same fridges and washing machines, so who is really wealthier? The short answer: Malaysia is clearly ahead on income, but this data cannot say who holds more household wealth. Low debt is not the same as being wealthy. Three checks lead there.

- Check 1. Vietnam's number is the weakest in the picture. A 2018 study found no official household debt series, this project found none since, and Vietnam is not in the BIS data. A central bank based measure that counts family businesses was 47% in 2016. On the World Bank's bank credit measure Vietnam was 125% of output against Malaysia's 113% in 2022, the latest year with both. Vietnam borrows a lot overall, and more of it may be counted as business debt. So the low household figure does not show that families there owe little.
- Check 2. Debt is one side of a balance sheet. Malaysian households owe about 85% of output on Bank Negara's wide count and hold financial assets of 177%, about 2.1 times their debt. Three quarters of their debt was borrowed for homes and cars (Bank Negara, 2025). No comparable asset figure was found for Vietnam.
- Check 3. Income. Output per person is 2.6 times higher in Malaysia (US$13,125 against US$5,066 in 2025, World Bank).

The post is right that household debt in Malaysia is a real burden: the typical Malaysian borrower's repayments take about a third of income (median debt service ratio 33% on existing loans, Bank Negara, 2025). On income Malaysia is clearly ahead, but on household wealth the data found cannot settle the post's question. Low debt in a table can mean thrift, a narrow way of counting, or little access to credit.

### Part 3 of 3. Who printed, year by year, and what the critics say

### What the critics add

| School | Its strongest point | What it adds to this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| MMT and post-Keynesian | Treat the Fed and the Treasury as one. The state spends first, taxes and bond sales drain afterwards. The limit is inflation, not money | Deficit spending is the only source of net financial assets for the non-government sector as a whole, which means the domestic private sector plus foreigners (Stephanie Kelton, The Deficit Myth, 2020). A country's private sector can also gain them from a trade surplus. |
| Austrian and hard money | New money enters unevenly. The first receivers, banks and asset owners, gain before prices rise. Credit without savings breeds booms that end in busts | Ask who receives new money first, not only which floor it lands on (Murray Rothbard, The Case Against the Fed, 1994) |
| Monetarist | The quantity of money matters for inflation, with a lag of one to two years | M2 rose about 40% in 2020 to 2021 and inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022. Then M2 fell in 2022 and 2023 (Tim Congdon, 2025) |
| Official view (Fed, Bank of England) | Central banks set the price of money. Banks and borrowers choose the quantity | QE works mainly through bond yields and portfolios, not only through the deposit count |

We found no school whose strongest objection points to a channel missing from this chapter. Each points to a missing consequence. The Austrian asks who got the money first. The monetarist asks what it did to prices. The MMT economist asks why we pretend the Treasury needs to borrow its own currency. A complete Level 2 reader can hold all three questions at once.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: M2 fell in 2022 and 2023, the first full-year falls since the record starts in 1959. Why was there no deflation?
  A: Prices respond with a lag, and the stock of money was still about 35% to 40% above early 2020. Inflation slowed but stayed positive. (builds on [The deflation debate: would anyone spend a money that grows?](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-defl.md))

### Putting sizes on it

*[Picture on the web page: Every channel that creates or destroys the public's deposits, with rough sizes for the latest year and for 2020 to 2021.]*

In an ordinary year, bank lending is the biggest engine. In the pandemic it was the government and the central bank: the Treasury spent borrowed money straight into household accounts, and the Fed and banks ended up holding about 68% to 72% of the new bonds, so nothing drained the deposits back. M2 rose about 40% in two years, from $15.5 trillion in February 2020 to $21.7 trillion in February 2022.

- **Myth:** All money is created by banks making loans.
- **Reality:** Loans are the main channel in normal years (87% to 96% of the two printers from 1971 to 2007), but banks also create money when they buy securities, the Fed creates reserves whenever it buys and deposits when the seller is a nonbank, and Treasury spending adds deposits unless the public funded it from its own accounts. In 2020 to 2021 the Treasury and Fed channel created most new deposits.

- **Myth:** The Fed prints money and hands it to the government.
- **Reality:** The Fed may not lend to the Treasury or buy at auction. It buys bonds from dealers in the open market and pays the sellers. That holds down yields and can turn bonds held by the public into deposits, which finances deficits indirectly. Its profits go back to the Treasury, and since 2022 it has been running losses.

### So who is printing? The everyday printer and the emergency printer

Put all of this together. In a normal year, banks are the main printer: new spendable money appears mostly because people and firms borrow. In the year to September 2026, bank loans grew by about $1.0 trillion and deposits by about $1.2 trillion. But the government and the Fed together can do the same job, and in some years they do it far more than the banks. That combination is printing money in everything but name.

*[Picture on the web page: Two ways new spendable money is made. The banks run every day. The government and the Fed switch on in emergencies, and then they are bigger.]*

1. The government spends more than it collects in taxes. When it pays a salary, a pension or a stimulus check, a new deposit lands in someone's bank account. Spending is what actually puts the money in public hands.
2. To cover the gap, the Treasury sells bonds.
3. Whether new money is left over depends on who ends up holding those bonds. If households and funds buy them with savings they already had, money only moves around and nothing new is left. If banks buy them, or the Fed buys them in the market from dealers and pension funds, the public keeps the new deposits and the bonds sit with the banks or the Fed. New money remains.

| Who ends up holding the new bonds | Is new spendable money left over? |
|---|---|
| Households and funds, paying with savings they already had | No. Money moves to the government and comes back |
| Banks | Yes. The public has the deposit, the bank has the bond |
| The Fed, buying from dealers and pension funds in the market | Yes. The Fed has paid for it with new reserves it created |

> **Picture this.** Think of two printers in an office. The banks' printer runs all day, printing a little each time someone signs a loan, and it shreds the pages again as loans are paid back. The government and the Fed share a second printer kept in a locked room. It needs two keys: Congress must approve the spending, and the Fed must choose to buy the bonds. When both keys turn, as in 2020, it prints faster than anything the banks can do.

The clearest case is 2020 to 2021. Bank deposits jumped $4.7 trillion, about 36% (December 2019 to December 2021). Most of it came from government spending on stimulus checks, business support loans and unemployment benefits, while the Fed ended up with about 56% to 59% of the new marketable Treasury debt, depending on the source. Bank loans rose only $0.68 trillion, a seventh of the deposit rise.

### Then why do people say the government cannot just print?

- The Fed decides, not the Treasury. The law lets the Fed buy only in the open market, for its own reasons such as interest rates or market stability, and it can stop or reverse. Since 2022 it has been shrinking its bond holdings, which pulls money back out.
- The bond market votes. If investors think a government is leaning on the printer, they demand higher interest rates. That is part of why the US 10-year yield is near 5% and the interest bill passed $1 trillion a year.
- There is friction. Congress must approve the spending, the debt ceiling has to be dealt with, and every auction has to find buyers.

> The two kinds of money behave differently over time. Bank-loan money is temporary: when the loan is repaid, the money disappears. Deficit money can stay, because the government rarely pays its debt down overall and rolls the bonds over. So the old worry that governments finance themselves by creating money is right when banks or the Fed end up holding the bonds, as in 2020 to 2021 (about 68% to 72% of the new debt). It is wrong when savers buy the bonds with money they already had.

**Check yourself.** Q: In 2020 bank deposits rose by trillions while bank lending barely grew. Which printer was running, and what two things had to happen?
A: The emergency printer. The government had to spend far more than it taxed, and the Fed and banks had to end up holding much of the new debt. Together that left trillions of new deposits in public hands.

- 1971 to 2007: the banks did almost all of it. In every decade the Fed's share of the two printers was only 4% to 13%. Its bond buying in those years was about the size of the paper cash people came to hold: the Fed's bonds rose about $0.72T from 1971 to 2007, and currency in circulation was about $0.83T at the end of 2007.
- 2008 to 2014: the first emergency. Bank loans actually fell by $481 billion in 2009, while the Fed bought bonds worth $3.44T over the period. Upstairs took about 75% of the work.
- 2015 to 2019: the banks took over again, adding $2.43T of loans while the Fed shrank its holdings by about $449B.
- 2020 to 2021: the biggest switch-on ever. The Fed bought $4.52T of bonds against $681B of new bank loans, about 87% upstairs. M2 jumped $6.15T in two years, the fastest rise in the record.
- 2022 to 2025: the printer ran backwards. The Fed shrank its bonds by about $2.00T while banks lent $2.60T. M2 rose only $853B over four years, and fell in 2022 and 2023, the first full-year falls in the official M2 record, which starts in 1959.
- 2026 so far: both printers are adding again, banks about $784B and the Fed about $201B.
- The whole period: banks added about $13.79T of loans and the Fed about $6.43T of bonds. Roughly two thirds downstairs and one third upstairs, but the upstairs third came in two short bursts. M2 grew from about $0.63 trillion at the end of 1970 to about $23.3 trillion in August 2026, roughly 37 times.

> How to read the bars fairly. They are the two main engines, not a complete accounting, so they do not add up exactly to the change in M2. Banks also create deposits by buying Treasuries themselves (about $1.9 trillion over the period), the Treasury's cash balance at the Fed swings up and down, and people move money in and out of cash. The blue bar is also the upper limit of upstairs money reaching the public, because Fed buying adds spendable deposits only when the seller is not a bank. Bank loans here are commercial banks only.

**Check yourself.** Q: Looking at the chart, in which two periods did the upstairs printer do most of the work, and what happened to the money supply right after the second one?
