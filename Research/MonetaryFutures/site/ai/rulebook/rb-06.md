*Most new money is not printed by the government. It is typed into existence by a bank when it makes a loan.*

With gold gone, where does new money come from? Most people imagine a government printing press. That picture is mostly wrong. This chapter shows the actual machinery, step by step, with a simple set of books. It is the chapter to reread if any later one feels confusing.

### Three kinds of dollars

Not all dollars are the same kind of thing. There are layers, each a promise from a different promiser.

| Layer | Who promises | Who can hold it | Rough size, US, 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base money: paper cash and bank reserves | The Federal Reserve | Cash: anyone. Reserves: only banks and a few institutions with Fed accounts | Bank reserves about $2.96 trillion (week to 21 October 2026) |
| Bank deposits | Your commercial bank | Anyone with a bank account | About $19.6 trillion (September 2026) |
| Treasury securities | The US government | Anyone, through a broker or bank | About $31.8 trillion of marketable debt held by the public |

The money you use day to day is almost all the second layer: bank deposits. When you pay with a card or an app, you are moving bank deposits. Paper cash is a small share.

### Loans create deposits

In 2014 the Bank of England, the central bank of the United Kingdom, published a short paper that surprised many readers. It described what bankers already knew.

> "Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money." (Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy, 2014)

A bank does not lend out a pile of savings it was holding. It writes two new entries in its books at the same moment. Let us watch it happen.

> **Worked example.** Maria borrows $20,000 from her bank to buy a car. Before the loan, the bank's books show nothing about Maria. The moment the loan is approved, the bank adds an asset: a $20,000 loan that Maria owes the bank. It also adds a liability: a $20,000 deposit in Maria's account that the bank owes Maria. Both sides grow by $20,000. Maria's own books mirror this: she now has a $20,000 deposit (an asset) and a $20,000 debt (a liability). No one else's deposit went down. The economy has $20,000 more money than it had a minute ago.

*[Picture on the web page: Double-entry: the bank records a new loan as an asset and a new deposit as a liability at the same instant. The deposit is new money.]*

1. Maria pays the car dealer. Her deposit moves to the dealer's account, maybe at another bank.
2. Between the two banks, the payment settles in reserves at the Federal Reserve. Banks keep reserve accounts at the Fed for exactly this.
3. The dealer now has $20,000 in deposits. The money keeps circulating, paying wages, suppliers and taxes.
4. As Maria repays the loan, her payments cancel both her debt and an equal amount of deposits. Repaying a bank loan destroys money, just as making one created it.

> When you deposit your paycheck, you are lending money to your bank. Your deposit is the bank's debt to you. You are an unsecured lender, protected by government deposit insurance up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. This is why bank runs happen: depositors are creditors who can all ask for their money back at once.

### What limits banks, then?

If banks can create money by lending, why not lend infinitely? Because lending has real limits, just not the ones in the old textbook.

- Capital rules: regulators require banks to fund a share of their loans with their owners' money, so losses hit shareholders first.
- Borrower demand: someone has to want the loan and be judged able to repay it.
- Profit: the loan must earn more than it costs the bank to fund and manage.
- Interest rates: the Fed sets the price of reserves, which ripples out to the price of every loan. Higher rates mean fewer borrowers.

- **Myth:** Banks can lend about ten times their reserves, so every dollar of base money becomes ten dollars of loans.
- **Reality:** This is the old money multiplier from textbooks, and the owner's notes used it, for example multiplying gold or Bitcoin by ten. The Bank of England's 2014 paper, which the owner also cites, says the reverse: banks lend first, and the central bank supplies reserves as needed at its chosen interest rate. In the United States, reserve requirements have been zero since March 2020. There is no fixed multiplier. The binding limits are capital, demand, profit and the interest rate. The owner's instinct that money is debt is correct. The fixed ten times rule is not. And when the Fed buys bonds, new public deposits appear only if the seller is a nonbank, such as a pension fund. ### The government's part: the Treasury

The US Treasury is the government's money manager. It keeps its main checking account, the Treasury General Account, at the Federal Reserve. In the week to 21 October 2026 it held about $952 billion. When the government spends, say on a Social Security payment, money moves from that account to the recipient's bank. The recipient's deposit rises and the bank's reserves at the Fed rise. Government spending adds money to the private economy.

When you pay taxes, the reverse happens. Your deposit falls, your bank's reserves fall, and the Treasury's account at the Fed rises. Taxes drain money out of the private economy.

Because the government spends more than it taxes, it borrows the difference. It does this through primary auctions. Treasury announces how many bills, notes and bonds it will sell. A group of 25 large banks and dealers, called primary dealers, must bid. Pension funds, money market funds, foreign central banks and individuals bid too, directly or through the dealers. Buyers pay with deposits and reserves, which move into the Treasury's account.

> The Fed is not allowed to buy new Treasuries straight from the government at auction, apart from rolling over bonds it already owns. When the Fed does buy Treasuries, it buys them from banks and investors in the open market. That is what quantitative easing was: the Fed creating reserves to buy bonds that were already out there.

### The owner's loop, corrected and kept

The owner sketched the money circuit as Treasury to Fed to banks to public to IRS (the tax office) to Treasury. That is a real loop, and the core insight, that modern money is a circuit of debts, is right. Here it is with the parts in their correct places.

1. Congress decides spending and taxes. Treasury spends from its account at the Fed, which adds deposits for the public and reserves for their banks.
2. Treasury covers the gap between spending and taxes by auctioning Treasury securities to dealers, banks, funds, foreigners and individuals, not to the Fed directly.
3. Banks create most new money by making loans to households and businesses. The Fed does not hand banks money to lend. It sets the interest rate on reserves and lends to banks against good collateral when needed.
4. The public spends, saves and repays loans. Repaying bank loans destroys deposits.
5. The public pays taxes to the IRS, which moves deposits and reserves back into Treasury's account at the Fed.
6. The Fed sometimes buys or lets run off Treasury securities in the open market, and when it makes a profit it sends it to the Treasury.

The owner also wrote that the Fed controls the speed of credit expansion, not the amount. That is close to right. The Fed sets the price of money, the interest rate, and banks and borrowers decide the quantity at that price. In September 2026 the Fed, now chaired by Kevin Warsh, raised its target rate to 3.75 to 4.00%, a sign that it is trying to slow the speed.

**Check yourself.** Q: Maria repays her entire $20,000 car loan. What happens to the money supply?
A: It shrinks by $20,000. Her repayment cancels both her loan, an asset of the bank, and an equal amount of deposits, a liability of the bank. Money created by a loan is destroyed when the loan is repaid.

**Check yourself.** Q: Does the Federal Reserve lend money to the Treasury when the government runs a deficit?
A: Not directly. The Treasury borrows by selling securities at auction to dealers and investors. The Fed may later buy some of those securities in the open market, which is one reason people say the Fed finances deficits indirectly.
