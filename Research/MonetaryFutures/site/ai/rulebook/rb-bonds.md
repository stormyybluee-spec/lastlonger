# Bonds: a ticket you can resell, and where it touches your life

*A bond is a ticket you can resell: you pay RM100, it pays you RM4 a year, and you get RM100 back at the end. This chapter builds everything from that one ticket, then shows where bonds touch your own life, how to check your own exposure once a month, and how to weigh scary bond headlines before you feel them.*

**In short**
- A bond is a ticket: you pay RM100, get RM4 a year, and get your RM100 back at the end.
- You can keep it or resell it through a dealer. That resale market is the secondary market.
- Yields up, prices down. A single bond held to the end still pays back its RM100. A bond fund never ends, so its dips are real when you sell.
- Bonds touch you through EPF, loans, the ringgit, prices and taxes, even if you never buy one.
- Rising yields hurt people holding long bonds today and help people who will save for years, if yields stay above inflation.
- Once a month, check four dials and six questions, and weigh scary headlines on the SCALES.

### Part 1 of 3. The ticket, the seesaw and the resale stall

### 1. Should these four people worry?

Imagine tomorrow's headline: "Bond market crash". Meet four people. They are made up, but their situations are common.

- Aisyah, 23, Kuala Lumpur. First job. RM15,000 in EPF (Malaysia's compulsory retirement fund) and RM5,000 in a fixed deposit, her only cash. A fixed-rate car loan. Plans to study abroad in three years.
- The Rahmans, Penang. A RM400,000 floating home loan (its rate moves with Bank Negara's rate) over 35 years, taken in 2023. RM250,000 in EPF, three months of spending in cash, and RM20,000 in a bond and sukuk fund for school fees in five years.
- Encik Hamid, 60, Ipoh. Retired. RM150,000 in EPF and RM150,000 in fixed deposits that he lives on. No loans.
- Linda, 68, United States. Retired. $240,000 in a broad bond fund she draws on, $300,000 in shares and $60,000 in short US government bills.

Guess now: which of them should care? By the end you can answer for each of them, and for yourself.

### 2. A bond is a ticket

Forget finance for a moment. The government sells you a ticket for RM100. It pays you RM4 every year. In ten years the government buys it back for RM100.

*[Picture on the web page: One ticket carries the whole chapter: RM100 in, RM4 a year, RM100 back.]*

That ticket is a bond. You lent the government RM100, so it owes you, and RM4 a year is your reward for waiting. Two limits of the picture: a concert ticket is worthless after the show, but a bond ends with your RM100 back. And a bond fund holds many tickets and never ends.

**Five words to start with.** 
- **Bond:** A ticket that pays a fixed amount each year and your money back on a set date.
- **Face value:** The amount paid back at the end. RM100 in our ticket.
- **Coupon:** The fixed yearly payment. RM4 in our ticket, or 4%.
- **Maturity:** The end date, when the ticket is bought back.
- **Yield:** What a ticket earns per year if you buy at today's price and hold to the end. It is not the coupon: buy the RM4 ticket for RM92.28 and your yield is about 5%, not 4%.

> **Malaysia note.** About half of Malaysia's government debt is Islamic paper, mostly GII (Government Investment Issues, the government's sukuk, or Islamic bonds). Most GII is built on a deferred sale of a commodity to the government, so holders earn profit, not interest. The ticket picture still works.

### 3. The seesaw: why your ticket's price moves

A month later, new tickets pay RM5 a year: the going yield has risen from 4% to 5%. Nobody will pay RM100 for your RM4 ticket now. Its price drops until a buyer also gets a yield of about 5%.

1. Worked example. Your ticket pays RM4 and has 10 years left. New tickets yield 5%. A buyer pays about RM92.28. They get RM4 a year plus a RM7.72 gain at the end, a yield of about 5%.
2. Your turn, half done. New tickets yield 3% instead. Is your RM4 ticket worth more or less than RM100? (More, about RM108.53, because it pays more than new tickets.)
3. Your turn. A ticket with only 2 years left, when new tickets yield 5%. Does it fall more or less than the 10-year ticket? (Much less, about 1.9%, because only two years of too-small payments are left.)

*[Picture on the web page: Yields and prices sit on a seesaw. When yields rise, old tickets are worth less, and the other way round.]*

*[Interactive tool on the web page: Try it. Drag the yield on new bonds and watch three $1,000 bonds reprice.]*

Two rules fall out of the seesaw. Yields up, prices down. And the longer the ticket, the bigger the swing. A point means one percentage point, say from 4% to 5%. For a one-point rise, a 2-year ticket falls about 2% and a 30-year ticket about 15% (a model). A bond's duration tells you its swing: a fund with a duration of 6 falls about 6% per one-point rise. Fund factsheets print it.

- **Myth:** Government bonds are safe, so you cannot lose money on them.
- **Reality:** They are safe from default (the borrower failing to pay), not from price. In 2022 the main US bond index lost 13.01%, its worst year since the index began in 1976. Hold a single bond to the end and you get every ringgit back. Sell a fund's units after a dip and the loss is real.

### 4. The resale stall: the secondary market

There are two markets. The first sale is the government's auction, a ticket booth. In Malaysia, Bank Negara, the central bank, runs it and 20 principal dealers must bid. Every later sale is at a resale stall, the secondary market: one investor sells to another through a dealer. The government gets nothing from resales, but the resale yield tells it what yield it must offer at its next auction.

*[Picture on the web page: The booth sells new tickets. Old tickets change hands at the resale stall, and the dealer earns the gap.]*

1. To sell, you contact a dealer: a bank's trading desk or a broker.
2. The dealer shows two prices: it buys from you at RM99.50 and sells to buyers at RM99.60. The difference is the dealer's gap, its fee for standing ready. Ordinary buyers usually pay a wider gap than big investors, so ask for both prices.
3. The price shown is the clean price. The buyer also pays you the interest earned since the last payment.

The dealer's gap is what you pay to be able to sell quickly. It widens when everyone wants out. In March 2020 even US government bonds were hard to sell, and the Fed, the US central bank, bought $1.27 trillion in five weeks.

### Part 2 of 3. Where bonds touch your life

### 5. How you can own a bond

If you have EPF savings, you already own bonds. In Malaysia, EPF is the biggest route.

| Route in Malaysia | How much to start | Can you resell? |
|---|---|---|
| EPF (KWSP) | Automatic from your salary. 44.7% of EPF's RM1.41 trillion was in bonds and similar at the end of 2025, mostly government paper | No. You own a share of the fund |
| Bond and sukuk unit trusts | Depends on the fund | Yes, sell units back to the fund at the daily price |
| Bonds and sukuk on Bursa Malaysia, or seasoned bonds through banks | From about RM1,000 | Yes, but trading is often thin, so a quick sale may cost you |

Before buying anything sold as a bond or sukuk with a 'guaranteed' return, check the seller is licensed and not on the Securities Commission's Investor Alert List or Bank Negara's Financial Consumer Alert List.

### 6. Where bonds touch you

Rising yields in the news usually means the 10-year government bond yield. Here is what a rise does to a normal person.

*[Picture on the web page: Places bonds reach you. The colour says whether a rise in yields tends to help, hurt, do both or change little. The table below says the same in words.]*

| Where | How it works | If yields rise |
|---|---|---|
| Your EPF | Holds a giant drawer of government bonds for you | Prices dip on paper. New money earns more. Savers with decades ahead gain, if yields stay above inflation |
| Fixed deposits | Rates follow the OPR (Overnight Policy Rate), set by Bank Negara, now 2.75% | Help only if the OPR rises too |
| Home loan in Malaysia | Floating loans taken since August 2022 move only with the OPR. Older ones follow each bank's base rate | Mostly through the OPR: about RM60 a month per 0.25 point step on RM400,000 |
| Home loan in the US | 30-year fixed rates follow the 10-year yield | Hurt new buyers. Existing fixed loans are protected |
| The ringgit | Foreigners hold about a fifth of Malaysian government bonds | Can weaken, raising import prices. Not automatic: in 2026 it held near 4.08 ringgit per US dollar |
| Taxes and services | Interest on government debt competes with schools and hospitals | Hurt slowly. Malaysia's debt service is about 16% of revenue |

> **The one idea to keep.** The same rise in yields hurts people holding long bonds or borrowing now, and helps people who will save for years, as long as yields stay above inflation.

### Go deeper: What makes yields move: seven forces, and 2026 as an example

Why would new tickets suddenly pay RM5 instead of RM4? Think of a tug of war. The central bank's own rate, expected inflation, how many tickets the government must sell, how many buyers turn up, how fast the economy grows, and how much people trust the borrower all pull on the price of a new ticket.

*[Picture on the web page: Seven forces pull on the yield of a new ticket.]*

| Force | What it means | Which way it pushes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The Fed's rate path | Short yields track where the Fed sets overnight rates now and where markets expect it to go | Expected hikes push yields up, expected cuts pull them down |
| 2. Expected inflation | Lenders want compensation for prices rising while they wait | More expected inflation means higher yields |
| 3. Term premium | Extra pay for locking money up for years when the future is uncertain | More uncertainty about deficits, inflation or policy pushes long yields up |
| 4. Supply | How much the Treasury must borrow and in what lengths | Bigger deficits and more long bonds push yields up |
| 5. Demand | Who must or wants to buy: foreign central banks, banks, money funds, pensions, stablecoins | Captive or eager buyers pull yields down, a big seller pushes them up |
| 6. Growth | A strong economy competes for money, a weak one frees it up | Booms raise yields, recessions lower them |
| 7. Trust | Confidence in repayment and in the value of the currency repaid | Any doubt raises yields, a crisis elsewhere can lower them as money flees to safety |

*[Picture on the web page: Split the 10-year yield in two. The gold part pays you back for expected inflation. The blue part is the real reward for waiting.]*

You can see several forces at once in 2026. The Fed hiked in September (force 1). Oil prices kept inflation worries alive, and the bond market expected about 2.4% a year of inflation over ten years (force 2). A $1.99T deficit meant heavy issuance (force 4). Japan's recorded holdings of Treasuries fell $171B from February to August while it spent a similar sum defending the yen (force 5). Meanwhile French stress pushed some European money into Treasuries for safety (force 7 in reverse). The result: a 10-year yield of 5.13% on 21 October 2026, after a peak of 5.31% on 5 October.

### 7. Hidden risks, and what protects you

| Risk | How it reaches you | A real number | What reduces it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price risk | Your bond fund falls when yields rise | The main US bond index lost 13.01% in 2022 | Holding a single bond to the end. Shorter bonds |
| Inflation risk | Your fixed RM4 buys less | US bonds bought at 0.52% in 2020 paid far less than 2022's 9.1% inflation | Inflation-linked bonds. Short bonds that reset quickly |
| Forced-seller risk | You must sell at the worst moment | UK pension funds, September 2022: the Bank of England bought £19.3 billion of bonds to stop a spiral | Not borrowing to hold bonds. Emergency cash |

**Chart: Bonds against shares in two bad years (total return, %)**
| Item | US bonds (main index) | US shares (S&P 500) |
|---|---|---|
| 2008, a recession shock | 5.24 | -37.0 |
| 2022, an inflation shock | -13.01 | -18.1 |

Why the difference? 2008 was a recession: people fled to safety, yields fell and bond prices rose. 2022 was an inflation shock: yields rose fast and bonds fell with shares. Higher yields today also mean more income tomorrow: in October 2026 a US inflation-linked bond paid about 2.6 points above inflation, against about minus 1 point at the end of 2021.

### What is left after inflation

Every yield you see is before inflation, Level 1's rubber ruler. Malaysian individuals generally pay no income tax on interest and profit from government bonds and approved sukuk, so the real return is roughly the yield minus inflation: about 3.95% minus 1.8%, or about 2.1 points (October 2026). In the US, take tax off first.

### Go deeper: The US real-return walk, with tax

On 21 October 2026 the US 10-year yielded 5.13% and the 10-year inflation-linked Treasury 2.76%, so about 2.4 points of the 5.13% was pay for expected inflation.

1. Start with the nominal yield: 5.13% on a 10-year Treasury in October 2026, or $51.30 a year on $1,000.
2. Take out tax. In a 24% federal bracket about $39 is left. Treasury interest is free of state income tax, but not of federal tax.
3. Take out inflation. If prices rise 2.4% a year, about what the bond market expects, $24 of that $39 only keeps up with prices. Your real gain is about 1.5% a year.
4. If inflation turns out higher than expected, the real return drops below zero, even though every payment arrives on time.

### Go deeper: When the safe asset broke: 1979 to 2026, and how a bank failed holding safe bonds

A Treasury is safe from default in dollars. It is not safe from price. Every few years the market reminds people of the difference.

| Episode | 10-year yield, low to high | Price change if the whole jump hit a new 10-year bond at once (model) | What a holder gained or lost, coupons included (model) | What broke |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1979 to 1981, Volcker | 8.76% to 15.84% | -35% | about -14% (price -32%) | Inflation above 13%. The Fed let rates rise until it broke |
| 1994, the bond massacre | 5.19% to 8.05% | -19% | about -13% (price -18%) | A surprise Fed hiking cycle. Orange County went bankrupt |
| 2008, the crisis | 4.27% to 2.08% | +20% (a gain) | about +21% (a gain) | Everything else fell and Treasuries rose. The safe asset worked |
| March 2020, dash for cash | 0.54% to 1.18% in seven trading days | -6% | about -6% | Even Treasuries could not be sold easily. The Fed bought $1.27T |
| 2021 to 2023 | 1.35% to 4.98% | -28% | about -21% (price -24%) | Stocks and bonds fell together. UK pension funds needed the Bank of England in September 2022 |
| 2023, SVB |  |  |  | US banks carried $620B of unrealized bond losses (end 2022). One became a run |
| April 2025, tariff shock | rose sharply within days |  |  | Long yields rose while the dollar fell, a rare case of the safe asset and its currency falling together. Hedge funds unwinding the basis trade were widely blamed, though that is contested |
| 2026 | 3.97% to 5.31%, the highest close since 2002 | -10% | about -8% (price -10%) | A Fed hike, oil, heavy issuance. The 30-year hit 5.67% on 7 October |

Two models, both using the Fed's H.15 daily closes. The first column reprices a 10-year bond as if the whole jump in yields hit it at once. The second follows a real holder who bought a new 10-year at par at the low, still owned it at the high with fewer years left, and collected the coupons along the way (coupons not reinvested, computed for this chapter). Notice the one gain: in 2008 Treasuries did their job as everything else fell. Notice too that 2022 was different. Stocks and bonds fell together, which broke the old idea that one always rescues the other.

How can a bank fail holding bonds that never missed a payment? Walk through a bank shaped like Silicon Valley Bank.

| Step | The bank owns | The bank owes |
|---|---|---|
| 2021: deposits flood in | $100 of 10-year bonds bought at 1.5%, plus $8 of cash and loans | $100 of deposits, and $8 of owners' money |
| 2022: yields rise to 4.5% | The bonds are now worth about $78, plus the $8 | Still $100 of deposits. Marked to market, owners' money is -$14 |
| 2023: depositors leave | Bonds must be sold to pay them | Many deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit, so they left fast. The hidden loss becomes real. Owners' money is gone |

Accounting rules let the loss stay hidden while the bank promised to hold the bonds to the end. A run forced a sale, and the paper loss became real in a day. The bonds were never in default. SVB's held-to-maturity losses were about $15.1B, close to all of its owners' money. Two days after SVB closed, the Fed opened the Bank Term Funding Program, which lent to banks against Treasuries at their face value, not their market value. A bond's price can fall, but in that panic its value as collateral was held at par by policy.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: How much could a risk-free 10-year Treasury lose?
  A: On price alone, about 32% for someone who bought a new 10-year at 8.76% in July 1979 and still held it in September 1981, when yields hit 15.84%. Counting the coupons collected, the loss was about 14%. From 2021 to 2023 a holder lost about 24% on price and about 21% with coupons (models, Fed H.15 data). Risk-free means free of default, not free of price swings. (builds on [Eye-openers: what most people never notice](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-13.md))
- Q: Someone bought a 10-year Treasury at 0.625% in 2020. What is it worth now, and did they lose money?
  A: About $84 per $100 at today's yields with four years left (model). They lose nothing in dollars if they hold to 2030, but they lose buying power: inflation peaked at 9.1% in 2022. (builds on [The yardstick problem](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-10.md))
- Q: A bank failed in 2023 holding bonds that never missed a payment. How?
  A: Its bonds had lost value as yields rose, about $15.1B at SVB, close to its owners' money, and US banks together carried $620B of such losses at the end of 2022 (FDIC). When depositors ran, the paper loss became real. (builds on [The musical chairs question](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-07.md))

### Go deeper: How governments quietly shrink debt with inflation

This is not theory. In the 1940s the Fed held long-term yields near 2.5% while inflation ran in double digits after the war, quietly shrinking the war debt in real terms. In the 1970s inflation often ran above bond yields. And anyone who bought a 10-year Treasury at its record low closing yield of 0.52% in August 2020 earned about half a percent a year, while US consumer inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022.

> This is the quietest way governments shrink debt. Keep the nominal yield below inflation for long enough and the real value of the debt melts, paid for by bondholders and savers who only look at the nominal number. Level 1's "The seasons of money" called it financial repression. The cure is the split in step 3: always separate a yield into the part that only keeps up with inflation and the part that is truly yours.

### Go deeper: The government's side, in one page

Why do governments sell tickets? Much of each year's new borrowing repays old tickets that are ending. The rest covers the gap when spending is more than taxes. For a government that issues its own money, borrowing is also a rule it sets for itself: in the US the Fed may not buy new Treasuries straight from the government (Level 1, "How money is born today"), and safe bonds help a central bank steer interest rates. Banks, pension funds and central banks also need a safe ticket to hold. Each new ticket is sold at an auction where dealers must bid. The resale price then works like a vote that never stops: if buyers doubt a government's plans, yields rise and its interest bill grows.

That bill is already large. Malaysia's debt service is projected at RM57.3 billion in 2026, 15.8% of revenue, above the 15% limit Malaysia set itself in its 2023 fiscal law (Act 850). In the US, net interest passed $1 trillion in fiscal 2026, more than defense.

### Go deeper: Why the whole system rests on bonds (the backbone test)

*[Picture on the web page: Picture a champagne tower at a wedding. Government bonds are the bottom row of glasses. Every glass above rests on them.]*

- The Fed's money rests on them. The Fed creates bank reserves and cash, and the main asset it holds against them is Treasuries: $4.57T of its $6.47T of securities, about 71% (21 October 2026).
- They are the financial system's pawnshop ticket. In repo markets, banks and funds hand over Treasuries to borrow cash, often overnight. About $12.6T of US repo was outstanding on an average day in the third quarter of 2025 (Office of Financial Research).
- Banks and money funds park cash in them, because rules treat them as the safest liquid asset.
- Stablecoins must hold them. Under the GENIUS Act, compliant dollar tokens must be backed by cash-like reserves: deposits, central bank money, government money funds, repo, or Treasuries with 93 days or less left. Short bills are the dominant choice.
- Foreign central banks keep their savings in them. That is how the world holds dollars without holding cash.
- They set the base price of money. Mortgages, car loans and company loans are priced as the Treasury yield plus a margin for risk.

Level 2 does not take a title on trust. Here is a number on each job a Treasury does.

| Job | Measure | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Base under the Fed's money | Treasuries as a share of the Fed's securities | 71% ($4.57T of $6.47T, 21 Oct 2026) |
| The world's savings | Treasuries held by foreign governments, as a share of all world currency reserves | about 28% ($3.76T of $13.22T, mid 2026) |
| The pawnshop ticket | US repo outstanding on an average day, 69% backed by Treasuries | about $12.6T (Q3 2025) |
| Bank survival kit | Treasuries, agency bonds and cash at US banks | $7.88T, 30% of bank assets (Sep 2026) |
| Money funds | Total money fund assets, mostly government paper | $7.98T (Sep 2026) |
| New rails | Treasury bills held by the largest stablecoin issuer, Tether | about $115B (mid 2026, press) |
| Price of all other credit | Mortgages and company loans are priced as a Treasury yield plus a margin | every day |

The title passes, with one correction. Bonds are the backbone of the system's plumbing: collateral, reserves, liquidity and stablecoin backing. They are not what most bank deposits lean on. At US banks, loans are $14.1T of $25.9T of assets, Treasuries and agency bonds $4.8T. In normal years most new deposits are born from loans. In 2020 to 2021 most came from the Fed buying bonds and the government spending (Level 2, "Who really creates money"). Either way, the system settles, pledges and saves in Treasuries.

### Go deeper: How a bond is born: one real US auction

1. Announcement. The Treasury offered $39.0B of a 10-year note with a 4.625% coupon, maturing 15 August 2036, for auction on 7 October 2026.
2. Bidding. Bids came in for $109.0B, 2.77 times the amount offered. The 25 primary dealers are required to bid for their share every time, so every auction is fully covered by design.
3. One price for all. Every winner pays the same yield, the highest one accepted: 5.300%, a price of $94.86 per $100. It was the highest yield at a 10-year auction since November 2000.
4. Who won. Indirect bidders (foreign and domestic investors bidding through dealers) took 79.6%. Direct bidders took 17.0%. The dealers kept only 2.5%, the smallest share in the data.
5. Settlement on 15 October. Buyers' banks pay, and the money goes up into the Treasury's account at the Fed. If the buyer was a fund or a person, their deposit is gone until the Treasury spends it (Level 2, "Who really creates money", Step 3).

What the Treasury sells changes what happens to your money. Bills, about 22% of tradable debt, are bought heavily by money market funds. From June 2022 to October 2026 those funds pulled $1.97T out of the cash they had parked at the Fed, much of it to buy bills, and that cash came back into bank deposits when the Treasury spent it. Notes and bonds bought by investors from their own bank deposits drain those deposits until the Treasury spends. Level 2, "Who really creates money", Steps 3 and 4, walks through both cases.

### Go deeper: Repo: how a bond is pawned overnight

Repo is a loan secured by a bond. You sell a bond for cash today and agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The difference is the interest. The lender keeps a cushion called a haircut, for example lending $98 against $100 of bonds. Here a hedge fund buys a $100 Treasury and funds it in repo through a dealer, and a money market fund supplies the cash.

| Who | What they now own | What they now owe | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hedge fund | $100 Treasury (pledged) | $98 repo loan, plus $2 of its own money | It holds a $100 bond with only $2 of its own money. In practice this is usually one leg of a hedged trade (the cash-futures basis trade), not a bare bet |
| Dealer (a bank's arm) | $98 loan to the fund | $98 borrowed from the money fund | It is the middleman and nets the two |
| Money market fund | $98 repo loan, safe and overnight | nothing new | It swapped a bank deposit for a loan backed by a Treasury |
| The two floors | Reserves move between banks | Deposits move from the fund to the bond's seller | Public money only moved. Nothing was created |

The US repo market had about $12.6T of loans outstanding on an average day in the third quarter of 2025 (Office of Financial Research). That is why a Treasury is worth more than its coupons: it can be turned into cash overnight at a small haircut. It is also where the system breaks. When haircuts jump or dealers run out of room, funds must sell bonds, prices fall, haircuts rise again. That loop drove March 2020.

### Go deeper: Duration and convexity: the bond's lever

Duration is the number that tells you how hard a bond swings. Roughly, a bond's price moves by its duration, in percent, for each one point move in yields, the other way. It is also about the average time until you get your money back, with near payments counting more than far ones. A 2-year Treasury has a duration near 2, a 10-year near 8, a 30-year near 15.

| Bond (yields of 21 October 2026) | Price per $100 | Duration (the lever) | If yields rise 1 point | If yields fall 1 point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2-year, 4.75% coupon, yield 4.81% | $99.89 | 1.9 | -1.9% | +1.9% |
| 10-year, 4.625% coupon, yield 5.13% | $96.09 | 7.9 | -7.5% | +8.3% |
| 30-year, 5.00% coupon, yield 5.42% | $93.81 | 15.0 | -13.4% | +16.8% |

Look at the last two columns. For the same one point move, the gain when yields fall is bigger than the loss when they rise: +16.8% against -13.4% on the 30-year. That bend is called convexity. It is why long bonds are loved in a crash and feared in an inflation. Duration figures are a model computed for this chapter with half-yearly coupons. Coupons for the 2-year and 30-year are illustrative, the 10-year is the real note auctioned on 7 October 2026.

### Go deeper: The yield curve, and the bond market as a voter

*[Picture on the web page: One move in the 10-year yield, four ripples.]*

Take a $400,000 mortgage over 30 years. At 6.40% the monthly payment is about $2,502. At 7.40%, one point higher, it is about $2,770. One point on the bond market costs that family about $267 a month, about $3,210 a year, for thirty years.

*[Picture on the web page: The yield curve: yields lined up from short to long. Normal curves slope up. An inverted curve, drawn here as an example, often warns of a slowdown.]*

Line up yields from short to long and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes up, because waiting longer deserves more pay. In October 2026 it does: about 4.81% at 2 years, 5.13% at 10 years and 5.42% at 30 years. When short yields rise above long ones (an inverted curve), markets are betting the Fed will have to cut, usually because a slowdown is coming. Inversions have come before many US recessions, though the timing is loose. The longest inversion of the 2s and 10s on record, 541 trading days from April 2022 to September 2024, has not been followed by a recession so far, and by 22 October 2026 the curve sloped up again by 0.31 points.

> "I used to think if there was reincarnation, I wanted to come back as the president or the pope or as a .400 baseball hitter. But now I would want to come back as the bond market. You can intimidate everybody." (James Carville, adviser to President Clinton, 1993)

> The bond market is a voter that votes every second. When it dislikes a government's plans, yields rise and the government's own interest bill rises with them. US net interest reached $1,051.8B in FY2026, more than defense. That bill is the bond market's running verdict, and it is why Treasury secretaries watch auctions more closely than polls.

### Go deeper: Where the ticket picture breaks

- A concert ticket is worthless after the show. A bond ticket ends with a RM100 buy-back.
- Resale ticket prices swing on hype. Bond prices swing mostly on yields and on trust in the borrower.
- A ticket buyer is a customer. A bond buyer is a lender: the borrower owes them.
- Most Malaysian government sukuk (GII) rest on a deferred sale of a commodity, so holders earn profit, not interest.
- Bond prices are less visible than app prices. Most trading is between dealers.
- A central bank can buy tickets with newly created money. No concert has a buyer like that.
- For a single bond held to the end, the resale price matters only if you sell. A fund never ends, so its price always matters when you sell units.

### Go deeper: Who pays whom when a bond is bought: four cases

| Case | What changes | Public money (deposits and cash) |
|---|---|---|
| A person buys a new bond at auction | Their deposit falls. Their bank's reserves move up into the government's account at the central bank | Falls until the government spends it, then comes back |
| A person buys from another person on the resale market | One deposit falls, another rises. The dealer earns the gap. The government is not involved | Only moves. Nothing created or destroyed |
| A fund such as EPF buys a bond from a bank | The fund's deposit falls and the bank's bond holding falls | Falls. A bank selling a security to a nonbank destroys a deposit, the mirror of a bank buying one |
| A foreign fund sells Malaysian bonds and takes its money home | A local buyer pays ringgit. The fund swaps the ringgit for dollars at a bank, or Bank Negara sells dollars from reserves | Resident deposits fall if a nonbank bought. The ringgit weakens, reserves fall, or yields rise until a local buyer steps in |
