# What money really is

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 01. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-01.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-01 . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Money is not a thing you hold. It is a promise that a whole society agrees to keep track of.*

Ask ten people what a dollar is and most will say paper, or numbers in an app, or the stuff you buy things with. All three answers describe what money looks like. None of them says what money is. This chapter fixes that, because every later chapter stands on it.

Here is the short version. Money is a record of who owes what to whom, written in a unit that everyone agrees to use. The paper, the coin and the app are only the places where the record is kept. Once you see money as a record, a lot of confusing things in finance suddenly make sense.

### The story you were taught

Most textbooks tell a neat story. Long ago people bartered. A baker wanted shoes, but the shoemaker did not want bread. Trade was clumsy. So people picked one handy good, like silver or shells, that everyone would accept. That good became money. Later, banks and credit were built on top of it.

Adam Smith told a version of this story in The Wealth of Nations in 1776, with his butcher, brewer and baker. A century later the economist William Stanley Jevons gave the problem a name: the double coincidence of wants. Barter only works when each side happens to want what the other has, at the same moment.

> **Picture this.** Imagine a school lunchroom with no money. You have a sandwich and want an apple. The kid with the apple wants cookies, not your sandwich. You would need to find a third kid, trade the sandwich for cookies, then trade cookies for the apple. Lunch would be over before you ate. That is the barter problem, and it is real. The question is whether people ever actually lived that way.

### What the records actually show

When historians and anthropologists went looking for whole societies that ran on barter, they did not find them. What they found, again and again, was credit. People kept running tabs with neighbors. They settled up later, often at harvest time, and they measured the debts in a shared unit.

- Mesopotamia, about 5,000 years ago: temple and palace clerks kept clay tablet accounts. Debts and prices were written in weights of silver and measures of barley. Most farmers never touched the silver. The silver was a unit for keeping score, not a coin in every pocket.
- Medieval England: the royal treasury, the Exchequer, recorded tax debts on notched hazel sticks called tallies. The stick was split in two, and each side kept half. Tallies were used from the 1100s until 1826. When officials burned the old sticks in 1834, the fire got out of control and burned down most of the Palace of Westminster.
- The island of Yap in the Pacific: people used huge carved stone discs called rai. Big stones rarely moved. Everyone simply remembered who owned which stone. One stone was said to lie at the bottom of the sea after a canoe accident, and it still counted as wealth, because the community agreed it existed.

Each case says the same thing. The core of money was the shared memory of who owed what. The physical object, when there was one, was a token of that memory.

> "No example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described, let alone the emergence from it of money." (Caroline Humphrey, anthropologist, 1985)

- **Myth:** Money was invented to fix barter, and credit came later.
- **Reality:** In most of the record, credit and accounts came first, and coins came much later. Barter does show up, but mostly between strangers who do not trust each other, or when a money system breaks down, as in prisoner of war camps where cigarettes became currency, or in Russia in the 1990s. Some economists still defend the barter story as a useful thought experiment rather than history. That is a fair use, as long as nobody mistakes it for what happened.

### The three jobs of money

Whatever form it takes, money does three jobs. Learn these three words well. They come back in every chapter.

| Job | Plain meaning | Everyday example | What breaks it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medium of exchange | The thing people hand over to settle a trade | Tapping your card for groceries | Nobody will accept it, so you cannot spend it |
| Store of value | A way to carry buying power from today into the future | Savings you will use next year | Prices rise fast, so the same money buys less later |
| Unit of account | The measuring stick for prices, wages and debts | Your rent is written as $1,500 a month | The unit keeps changing size, so contracts become guesses |

*[Picture on the web page: Money does three jobs at once: it settles trades, it carries value through time, and it measures everything. A good money does all three. Many things do one or two.]*

> **Picture this.** Think of money as the scoreboard in a game that the whole country plays at once. The scoreboard does not make the points. Players make the points by working, building and trading. The scoreboard just records them so everyone can see the score, compare it and carry it into the next round. If someone can secretly change the scoreboard, the game stops being fair. Hold on to this picture. We will use it again and again.

> The quietest job is the most powerful one. Whoever controls the unit of account controls the terms of every contract written in it. Your wage, your mortgage, your rent and the national debt are all written in dollars. If the dollar shrinks in value, every one of those contracts changes meaning without anyone signing anything new. That is why governments defend the unit of account harder than anything else.

### Trust is the hidden ingredient

A dollar bill is worth something to you because you expect the next person to take it. That person accepts it because they expect the person after them to take it. Money is a long chain of expectations. Break the chain and money becomes paper again.

Governments help hold the chain together in a simple way. They demand taxes in their own money. If you must pay the tax office in dollars, you need dollars, so dollars have value. The German economist Georg Friedrich Knapp built a whole theory on this idea in 1905. Economists still argue about how much taxes matter compared with habit, law and the sheer convenience of using what everyone else uses. Most agree that all of these help.

> "It exists not by nature but by law." (Aristotle, on money, Nicomachean Ethics, about 350 BC)

When trust breaks, it breaks fast. Germany in 1923 and Zimbabwe in 2008 saw prices doubling in days. People stopped using the local money and fled to foreign currency, to gold, or to goods. The paper did not change. The promise behind it did.

### The owner's four phases, read carefully

The author of this project, writing a newsletter called Ledger Age, described four phases of value. Phase 1 was physical (gold and silver, stored labor plus scarcity). Phase 2 was symbolic (paper as a promise of gold). Phase 3 was political (fiat money, a promise of control). Phase 4 is digital (credit, crypto and ledgers, a promise of data). In the owner's words, Phase 4 did not end the game, it removed the referee.

This is a strong way to see the big arc, and the rulebook keeps it. Two gentle corrections make it sharper. First, the phases overlap rather than follow one another, because credit and ledgers are the oldest layer, not the newest. Mesopotamian clerks were running Phase 4 style ledgers in clay. Second, the referee has not left. Central banks, courts and regulators still referee the game, as later chapters show. What digital ledgers changed is speed and reach: the scoreboard now updates in seconds, everywhere at once.

**Check yourself.** Q: Your friend says money is valuable because it is backed by gold. Using this chapter, what is a better answer?
A: Money is valuable because people trust that others will accept it, because the government demands it for taxes, and because debts and prices are written in it. Gold backing is one way to build that trust, but the dollar has not been backed by gold since 1971.

**Check yourself.** Q: Which of the three jobs of money is failing when prices rise 50% in a year?
A: Mainly the store of value, because savings lose buying power. The unit of account also suffers, because prices and contracts become hard to compare over time.
