# Commodity money and the gold standard

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 02. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-02.

*Tying money to a metal makes it honest. It also makes it stiff, and stiff money breaks people who owe it.*

Chapter 1 showed that money is a promise. The oldest way to make a promise believable is to tie it to something hard to fake. For thousands of years that something was metal, mostly silver and gold. This chapter is about what metal money did well and why it was finally abandoned.

### Coins: a promise you can bite

The first known coins appeared around 600 BC in Lydia, in what is now Turkey. They were lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, stamped with a mark. The stamp was the important part. It said: the king guarantees the weight and purity of this metal. You no longer had to weigh and test every piece.

Metal money has a built-in brake. Nobody can create gold by decree. It must be mined, which takes work and time. That scarcity is why people trusted it as a store of value.

> **Picture this.** Think of a coin as a sealed jar of honey with a royal label. The label saves you from opening and tasting every jar. But the label is only as good as the person who sealed the jar. If the king starts adding water and keeping the label, the jars still look the same while the honey gets thinner.

### Debasement: watering the honey

Rulers who needed more money than they had found a trick. Melt the coins, mix in cheaper metal, strike more coins with the same face. This is called debasement. It is the ancient version of printing money.

Rome is the classic case. The silver denarius, introduced around 211 BC, was nearly pure silver for a long time. Under Nero in the 60s AD its weight and silver content were trimmed. Through the crisis years of the 200s AD, emperors who had to pay armies kept cutting. By the 260s and 270s the denarius and its successor coins held only a few percent of silver, a thin coating over bronze. Prices soared. In 301 AD the emperor Diocletian issued an edict fixing maximum prices for over a thousand goods, on pain of death. It failed. Goods vanished from markets, and the edict was quietly ignored.

> Debasement and modern inflation share a pattern. The first people to spend the new coins, the emperor and his soldiers, got full value. By the time the extra coins spread through the economy, prices had risen, and the last people to receive them lost. Chapter 16 calls this the Cantillon effect.

### Gresham's law: bad money drives out good

When two coins have the same face value but one contains more real metal, people spend the bad one and hoard the good one. The good coins disappear from circulation. This is Gresham's law, named after Sir Thomas Gresham, a financial agent to Queen Elizabeth I. The idea is older. Nicole Oresme in the 1300s and Nicolaus Copernicus in 1526 described it. The name was attached in 1858.

You can see Gresham's law today. In the United States, silver dimes and quarters minted before 1965 vanished from pockets within a few years after cheaper copper-nickel coins arrived. People kept the silver ones.

### The classical gold standard, about 1870 to 1914

Britain drifted onto a gold standard after 1717, when Isaac Newton, as Master of the Mint, set a gold price for the guinea that left silver undervalued. Britain made it law in 1821. After 1871 the newly united Germany switched to gold, and much of the world followed. The United States stopped coining silver dollars in 1873, resumed paying gold for its paper in 1879 and made gold the legal standard in 1900.

The rule was simple. Each currency was defined as a fixed weight of gold. Banks and central banks promised to swap paper notes for gold at that fixed rate. Because every major currency was tied to gold, they were also tied to each other. A pound was worth about $4.86 for decades.

*[Picture on the web page: Under the gold standard, paper notes are claims on gold at a fixed price. Because each currency is fixed to gold, exchange rates between currencies are fixed too.]*

The system had an automatic balancing idea, first described by the Scottish philosopher David Hume in 1752. A country that imported too much paid in gold. Gold flowed out. Its money supply shrank. Prices and wages fell. Its goods got cheaper, exports recovered, and gold flowed back. Elegant on paper. Painful in real life, because falling wages and prices mean layoffs and bankruptcies.

| What the gold standard did well | What it did badly |
|---|---|
| Kept long-run prices fairly stable | Could not expand money when the economy needed it |
| Made exchange rates predictable for trade and lending | Forced painful deflation on countries losing gold |
| Limited a government's ability to print its way out of trouble | Tied money growth to mining luck, not to the needs of the economy |
| Built trust for international investment | Turned every banking panic into a scramble for scarce gold |

### Why fixed money hurt debtors

Here is a key idea of the whole rulebook. When money is fixed but the economy grows, there is more stuff to buy with the same amount of money. Prices fall. That is deflation. Falling prices sound pleasant until you owe money.

> **Picture this.** A farmer in Kansas borrows $1,000 in 1880 when wheat sells for $1 a bushel. The debt is worth 1,000 bushels. By 1894 wheat sells for about half that. The farmer still owes $1,000, but now it takes about 2,000 bushels to pay. The farmer works twice as hard to pay the same debt. The lender, without lifting a finger, is repaid in money that buys twice as much wheat.

That is roughly what happened. From 1873 to 1896, prices in the United States and Britain drifted downward for more than two decades. The economy grew, but farmers and other debtors were crushed by rising real debts. The American Populist movement demanded more money, especially free coinage of silver, to stop the squeeze.

> "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." (William Jennings Bryan, Democratic National Convention, 1896)

Bryan lost the 1896 election. Then something unplanned ended the deflation anyway: new gold. Huge finds in South Africa and the Klondike, plus a new chemical process for extracting gold from ore, expanded the world's gold supply. From 1897 prices rose gently, and farm distress eased. The money supply had been set by miners, not by anyone's plan.

- **Myth:** Gold-backed money is always fair to everyone.
- **Reality:** Gold money is fair to lenders and savers in a growing economy, because it gains buying power. It is harsh on borrowers, because their debts get heavier as prices fall. Whether a money is fair depends on whether you owe it or own it, and on which way prices are moving. Chapter 8 calls these the seasons of money.

> The owner's project tested a popular claim that the world's gold, times ten, equals the world's debt. The ratio is real today, about 12 times, but in the past it wandered from about 35 times in 1971 to about 53 times in 2000 and 23 times in 2010. A real mechanism would show a stable ratio. This one moves mainly with the gold price, so it is a coincidence, not a rule. No legal link between gold and dollar credit has existed since 1971.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why did falling prices from 1873 to 1896 hurt farmers more than bankers?
A: Farmers owed fixed dollar debts while the prices they received for crops fell, so each dollar of debt took more crops to repay. Bankers were owed those fixed dollars, which bought more as prices fell.

**Check yourself.** Q: What is Gresham's law, in one sentence?
A: When two moneys have the same face value but different real value, people spend the worse one and hoard the better one, so the bad money circulates and the good money disappears.
