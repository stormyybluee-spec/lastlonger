*In Monopoly the bank hands every player $1,500. In real life nobody does. So how does a new player get onto the board?*

The owner asked a question that sits under every fear about the future of money.

> "If a new board game runs but no players have the starting money, how can a new player with no royal lineage and no inherited wealth start?" (The owner of this project, in their own words)

This chapter answers it in three parts: how money actually enters the board, the ladder of rungs a newcomer climbs, and what history says about times when the ladder was rebuilt. Then it looks at the routes open in 2026, using this project's own research.

### How money enters the board

In Monopoly, every player starts with $1,500 from the bank. In the real economy there is no starting allowance. Chapter 6 showed the real doors through which money arrives.

- Wages and sales: a business pays you for your work or buys what you make. This is the main door for most people.
- Bank loans: a bank creates a deposit when it lends to you. This door opens only if a lender believes you can repay, often because you already have income or something to pledge.
- Government spending and transfers: the government pays wages, contracts and benefits, which adds money to private hands. Programs like child tax credits, education grants and, in some proposals, universal income payments enter here.

> **Picture this.** The real board game has no starting cash pile. Instead there are three ways onto the board. You can work for a player already on it, who pays you. You can persuade the bank to lend you chips, which usually requires showing chips you already have or wages coming in. Or the game's organizer can hand out chips, which happens rarely and in small amounts. Most players start through the first door, then use the chips they earn to climb.

### The ladder of rungs

1. Job: trade time and skill for income. This gives you a flow of money.
2. Savings: keep part of that flow. This gives you a stock of money and a buffer against shocks.
3. Credit: a record of reliably repaying small debts. This lets lenders trust you with larger ones.
4. Collateral: an asset you can pledge, like a house deposit, equipment or a business's sales history. This unlocks cheaper and larger loans.
5. Ownership: a share of something that earns without your time, like a home, a business or stocks. This is where wealth compounds.

Each rung converts what you have into what you need for the next one. Wages become savings. Savings plus a repayment record become credit. Credit plus savings buy collateral. Collateral and its income become ownership. The owner's Ledger Age series describes three mindsets: the Worker chases today's cash, the Investor studies the maze, and the Architect borrows tomorrow's cash today, puts it to work and owns the maze. The ladder is the path from Worker to Architect.

- **Myth:** The Architect's play is simple: borrow at about 6% and invest in stocks that return about 13.6% a year.
- **Reality:** Borrowing to own assets is how many fortunes were built, and the owner is right that ownership, not wages, decides who keeps up. But the 13.6% is a recent ten-year average, not a promise. From 2000 to 2009 the S&P 500 returned roughly nothing. Borrowed money must be repaid on schedule even when assets fall, which is how the 2008 crisis ruined many leveraged owners. The Architect's rule of survival is to borrow only what you can service through a bad season without selling.

### When the ladder was rebuilt before

| Program | What it gave non-owners | Scale | Who was left out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homestead Act, 1862 | Free land, the main productive asset of the age, for living on and farming it | About 270 million acres, about 10% of US land. Nearly 4 million claims, about 1.6 million completed deeds | Native nations whose land it was, and many Black Americans in practice |
| Public high school movement, about 1910 to 1940 | A free credential that raised earning power | The share of young people with a high school diploma rose from about 9% to about half | Many Black students in segregated, underfunded schools |
| GI Bill, 1944 | Paid education and government-backed home loans for veterans | About 7.8 million of 16 million veterans trained. Nearly 2.4 million home loans from 1944 to 1952 | Many Black veterans, through local discrimination, and most women |

Each reopening gave non-owners a productive asset or a credential that was large relative to the economy, and paired it with credit. Each also had exclusions, and the people left out became the next generation shut outside the board. The research of Raj Chetty and colleagues found that 90% of Americans born in 1940 out-earned their parents, against only about 50% of those born in the 1980s.

### Today's routes, from this project's research

| Rung | What the 2026 data say |
|---|---|
| First job | Unemployment for ages 20 to 24 was 8.0% in September 2026, down from 9.2% a year earlier. Entry roles in fields exposed to AI are thinning, and recent graduates face 5.6% unemployment against 4.2% overall. |
| Credit file | Under age 21, a credit card needs independent income or a cosigner. Realistic paths: authorized user on a parent's card, a secured card, or a credit-builder loan. |
| Savings and housing | The broken rung. Median first-time home buyer age is a record 40. Saving a 10% down payment on a median home at 10% of young pay took about 6.2 years in 1990 and about 10 years now. About 49% of adults under 30 live with a parent. |
| Launching a business | The cheap but steep rung. A digital brand can launch for about $525 to $2,850, a small physical brand for about $1,900 to $7,400. But median solo founder revenue fell 23% in 2025 while the top tenth rose 19%, and platforms charge a growing toll for attention. |
| Ownership | The bottom half of US households hold about 0.58% of directly held stocks and funds. Since late 2019, the top 10% took about 67.5% of the $78 trillion rise in household wealth, and the bottom half about 3.1%. |
| Child accounts | Trump Accounts give a $1,000 seed to children born 2025 to 2028. The seed alone grows to about $2,400 in today's money by 18 at 5% real return. Adding $50 a month reaches about $19,500. Today's 18-year-olds get no seed. |

> The owner worried that people living on a universal income would lose access to credit and assets, locking out their children. Under current law, this project found that worry is not supported: a steady benefit can count as income for a loan, roughly $69,000 of mortgage capacity per $1,000 a month. The real dividing line is not income source but ownership. Wage earners and benefit receivers are squeezed in the same places, housing, health cover and energy. The three tiers behave like two classes: owners, and people who depend on an income stream.

### What actually moves a newcomer up

- Get an income door open early, ideally in work that AI complements or that needs physical trust and licensing.
- Build a thin credit file before you need it, and never miss a payment.
- Own something from your first paycheck, even a small, diversified slice of the stock market. Ownership compounds while you sleep.
- Treat a cheap business launch as a skills and equity lottery ticket, not a plan to quit everything.
- Watch housing supply politics in your city. Broad, by-right zoning, as in Auckland after 2016, lowered rents in a measurable way. Land, not the house, is most of the price in expensive cities.

A: Wages or sales from someone already on the board, loans from a bank that creates new deposits, and government spending or transfers.
