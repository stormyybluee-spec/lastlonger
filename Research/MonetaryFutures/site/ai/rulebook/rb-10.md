# The yardstick problem

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 13. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-10.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-10 . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*We measure prices with money, but money itself keeps changing size. It is like measuring a room with a rubber ruler.*

Chapter 8 talked about prices rising and falling. But compared to what? Every price is measured in money, and money's value is itself moving. This chapter gives you the tools to see through the moving ruler: nominal versus real, time prices, and the difference between consumer prices and asset prices.

> **Picture this.** Imagine measuring your child's height every year with a tape measure made of rubber that stretches a little each year. The marks say your child grew 10 centimeters. But the tape stretched 4. Your child really grew about 6. Money is that rubber tape. Every price, wage and savings balance you see is read off a tape that keeps stretching.

*[Picture on the web page: A stretching yardstick: if the ruler (money) stretches, numbers rise even when the real thing has not grown. Real values strip out the stretch.]*

### Nominal and real

- Nominal means the number on the label, in today's money. Your salary is $50,000. A house costs $417,500.
- Real means the number adjusted for the change in money's buying power, so you can compare across time.
- The basic formula: real value equals nominal value divided by (1 plus inflation). The same idea works for growth rates: real growth is roughly nominal growth minus inflation.

> **Worked example.** You earn $50,000. You get a 3% raise, to $51,500. Prices rose 5% over the same year. In last year's dollars your new pay is $51,500 divided by 1.05, which is about $49,048. Your nominal pay went up $1,500. Your real pay went down about $950, or about 1.9%. You can buy less than last year, even though your paycheck says you got a raise.

The same logic applies to savings. This project found that the average US savings account paid about 0.38% in 2026. With inflation running around 3.5% that year, the real return on that savings account was roughly minus 3% a year. A Treasury bill paying about 4.25% at the same time had a real return of a little under plus 1%. Both are very safe. The real results are very different.

> When the news says the economy grew 5%, ask: nominal or real? In the second quarter of 2026 US nominal GDP grew at about an 8.5% annualized rate, but real GDP grew only about 2.2%. Most of the nominal growth was the ruler stretching.

### Time prices: measuring in hours of life

One way around the rubber ruler is to measure prices in hours of work. How many hours must an ordinary worker labor to buy something? This sidesteps the changing size of the dollar, because wages and prices are both in the same money. The owner wrote, time is the real currency for now. Time prices take that seriously.

| Item | Then | Now | Change in hours of work |
|---|---|---|---|
| All consumer prices (the CPI basket) | 1990 | 2026 | About 19% fewer hours |
| Food at home | 1990 | 2026 | About 24% fewer hours |
| New cars, adjusted for quality | 1990 | 2026 | About 54% fewer hours |
| Televisions | 1993 | 2026 | About 99.5% fewer hours |
| Median existing home, full price | 1990 | 2026 | About 42% more hours (about 9,000 to 12,800 hours) |
| Monthly payment on a median home | early 2020 | 2026 | About 40 to 68 hours a month |
| Family health insurance premium | 1999 | 2025 | About twice the hours (429 to 861) |
| Published public 4-year college tuition | 1995 to 1996 | 2025 to 2026 | About 58% more hours |

These figures come from this project's research, using the average hourly wage of production and nonsupervisory workers: about $10.20 in 1990 and $32.60 in September 2026. A few 1990 inputs are from memory sources and not perfectly verified, so read the house rows as approximate. The pattern, though, is strong and confirmed by independent studies.

> Goods that can be made in factories and traded across borders keep getting cheaper in hours of work. Things tied to land, local services and institutions, like housing, health care and college, keep getting more expensive. Your hour buys more food and electronics than in 1990, and less of a house than in 2020. Most arguments about whether life is getting better or worse are really people looking at different halves of this table.

### Asset inflation versus consumer inflation

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures the prices of things people buy to use: food, rent, fuel, clothes, medical care. It does not include the prices of things people buy to own: stocks, bonds and houses as investments. For housing, CPI counts rent and an estimate of what owners would pay to rent their own homes, not the purchase price.

That gap matters. From February 2020 to 2026, average hourly pay rose about 32.5% and consumer prices about 30.3%, so real pay barely moved. Over a similar period, house prices rose about 59% and the S&P 500 stock index about 140%. If you only watch CPI, you miss that the price of entering ownership ran far ahead of pay.

| Measure, about 2020 to 2026 | Rise |
|---|---|
| Average hourly pay | About 32.5% |
| Consumer prices (CPI) | About 30.3% |
| House prices | About 59% |
| S&P 500 stock index | About 140% |

- **Myth:** If inflation is low, your money is holding its value.
- **Reality:** Low consumer inflation means your money holds its value for buying groceries and gadgets. It says nothing about whether your money can still buy a house, a business or a share of the stock market. When new money flows mainly into assets, consumer inflation can stay modest while ownership slips out of reach. This is asset inflation, and it is invisible in the headline number.

### Other rulers people propose

Some people say: measure everything in gold. Gold is not a stable ruler either. In the year to October 2026 gold went from a high of about $5,586 an ounce to about $4,131. Others say: measure everything in energy, such as kilowatt-hours. This project worked through the arithmetic. Because solar panels have become dramatically cheaper (module costs fell from about $132 per watt in 1975 to about $0.27 in 2024, in today's dollars), a fixed kilowatt-hour tends to buy less over time. Goods priced in kilowatt-hours would show inflation, not stability, while energy keeps getting cheaper.

There is no perfect ruler. The practical skill is to know which ruler you are using, and to check important decisions with more than one: dollars, real dollars, hours of work and perhaps a hard asset.

**Check yourself.** Q: Your rent rises from $1,500 to $1,575 and your pay rises from $4,000 to $4,100 a month. Did rent take a bigger or smaller share of your pay?
A: Bigger. Rent went from 37.5% of pay to about 38.4%, because rent rose 5% while pay rose only 2.5%.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why can house prices double while official inflation stays moderate?
A: Because the consumer price index counts rents and an estimate of owners' rental cost, not the purchase price of homes as assets. Asset prices can surge without showing up in the headline inflation number.
