# The world's balance sheet: what it owns, what it owes, and how countries are built

Rulebook Level 2 (The machinery), chapter 03. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-worldbs.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-worldbs . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*You may have heard that the world owes $300 trillion, or $500 trillion, or more. Each can be true, depending on what you count, and the world owes all of it to itself. This chapter shows what the world really owns, why its debts cancel but still matter, how the numbers grew, and how each big country is built.*

**In short**
- Which debt? On the usual meaning, loans and bonds, the world owes about $348T (end 2025) to over $365T (mid 2026, IIF, via press). Its net wealth is about $518T (UBS, households) to $600T (McKinsey, all sectors, via press). Count everything owed on paper, deposits and other claims included, and the debt side is about $1,200T (computed from McKinsey).
- You cannot subtract one from the other. Every debt is somebody's asset, so at world level the debts cancel. What is left is real things, about two thirds of them real estate (McKinsey's 2021 study, Level 1).
- The debts cancel for the world, not for each family. Who owes whom, in which currency, and how soon decides the danger.
- In 2025 about four fifths of the rise in household wealth was higher prices and inflation, and about a fifth was new investment (McKinsey, via press).
- Debt grew from about 1 year of world output in 1971 (a model estimate) to about 2.5 years (3 counting the financial sector's own debts). It jumps in crises, 2008 and 2020. After 2020 the debt ratio fell while dollar debt kept rising, except in 2022, when a strong dollar shrank it.
- The dollar is the ruler. Japan's debt fell by a third in dollars from 2020 to 2025 while it rose in yen. Nobody repaid anything.
- Countries are built differently. The US is the richest country and the largest net debtor to the world. Japan's state owes mostly its own central bank and savers (foreigners about 13%). Italian families owe little (36% of output) while their state owes 137%. China borrowed to grow and saved at home.

### Part 1 of 3. What the world owns and owes

### The question that started this chapter

> "If the world wealth is 300T+ and 500T+ world debt, now show others a brief timeline of how others, maybe the G7 or G10, are different in structure, so users can understand and quantify the numbers around the world." (The owner of this guide, October 2026 (lightly edited))

It is a sharp question, and the honest answer starts with a question back: which debt? Both numbers can be true, depending on what you count. Count loans and bonds, the usual meaning of debt, and the world owes about $348 trillion to $365 trillion, while its net wealth is about $518 trillion to $600 trillion. On that count the order in the question flips. Count everything owed on paper by every part of the world, bank deposits and other claims included, and the total is about $1,200 trillion, far above $500 trillion. So "300T+" is right as a floor for wealth, and "500T+" is too high for loans and bonds and too low for everything owed. One thing holds on every count: none of these debts can be subtracted from wealth, because the world owes all of them to itself. The words below come first, then the numbers.

**Seven words to pin down first.** 
- **Net wealth:** What you own minus what you owe. A $500k house, $100k of savings and a $300k mortgage make $300k.
- **National net worth:** The net wealth of a whole country or the world, all sectors together.
- **Financial asset:** A claim on someone else: a deposit, a bond, a loan, a share. One side's asset is the other side's debt.
- **Gross debt:** Every IOU counted once, from the borrower's side, with nothing subtracted.
- **Nonfinancial debt:** Debt of governments, firms and households only. The financial sector's own borrowing is left out, so the same lending is not counted twice: a bank borrows in order to lend to the other three.
- **Yardstick:** World totals are in dollars. When the dollar moves 10%, non-US numbers move about 10% with nothing real changing.
- **Net foreign position:** What a country owns abroad minus what it owes abroad, shares and firms included. It is not the same as foreign debt.

Where could "500T+" come from? We do not know which source the question had in mind, so here are five candidates. As for "300T+", it is right as a floor for world wealth on every count. It is also roughly how world debt was reported from 2021 to 2024, about $300 trillion to $320 trillion (IIF), the figure many popular videos quoted.

- An estimate this guide's owner once made: the world's gold times ten, about $500 trillion. Level 1's "How money is born today" explains why a fixed times-ten multiplier does not hold.
- The financial firms of the world, central banks, banks and other funds, held $503.7 trillion of financial assets at the end of 2024 (Financial Stability Board). That is a count of claims. It includes much of the world's debt seen from the lender's side, plus shares and claims on each other.
- People's net wealth: $517.7 trillion at the end of 2025 (UBS). The number may simply belong on the other side of the ledger.
- Everything owed on paper by every sector: about $1,200 trillion (computed from McKinsey). On that count "500T+" is true, more than twice over.
- Derivatives: over-the-counter contracts with a notional value of $846 trillion at the end of June 2025 (BIS). Notional is the size of the thing bet on, not money owed. Their gross market value, closer to what is actually at stake, was $21.8 trillion. Never add notional derivatives to debt.

> **Popular claim.** "The world owes $500 trillion and is only worth $300 trillion." What it gets right: the world's paper claims are enormous, and on the widest count of everything owed (about $1,200 trillion) they are far bigger than $500 trillion. What it gets wrong: on the usual meaning of debt, loans and bonds, the world owes about $348 trillion to $365 trillion, while its net wealth is $518 trillion to $600 trillion. And on any count the two cannot be subtracted, because the world owes all of it to itself.

> Every one of these numbers comes from a different counter with a different purpose. UBS counts people's wealth for a wealth manager's report. The IIF is a bank industry body and counts the financial sector's own debts, so its figure is the biggest of the usual debt counts. The FSB counts financial firms' assets to watch for risk. McKinsey counts every balance sheet, which is how it reaches nearly $1,800 trillion. When two headlines disagree, first ask which counter they used.

Two kinds of pension sit in different places. Private pension funds are inside household wealth: the savings belong to people. Unfunded state promises, such as public pensions and health care paid from future taxes, are in none of these debt counts. They are real claims on future taxpayers, and hard-money writers often add them to debt. This chapter does not, and has no checked figure for them yet.

### The great cancellation: why the world cannot owe anyone outside itself

Every debt has two sides. When a family owes a bank $300,000, the bank owns a $300,000 claim. When the US Treasury owes $1,000 on a bond, a pension fund, a bank, the Fed or a foreign central bank owns that claim. Add up every balance sheet on Earth and each IOU appears once as a debt and once as an asset. They cancel. There is no bank on Mars that the world owes.

> Imagine a family of four who lend each other money all year: the teenager owes the parents for a phone, a parent owes the grandmother for a car, the grandmother owes the teenager for help with the shop. At the end of the year the IOUs on the fridge add up to $50,000. Is the family $50,000 poorer? No. The IOUs are owed from one member to another. The family is worth its house, its car and its shop. The IOUs only matter if one member cannot pay another.

The debts cancel for the world, not for each family. Creditors and debtors are different families, so interest flows from one group to another. In the United States, the top 10% of households took 67.5% of the rise in household net worth since the end of 2019, and the bottom half took 3.1% (Federal Reserve distributional accounts, mid 2026).

Walk through two real kinds of deal. First, a young family buys an existing flat from a retired couple with a $300,000 mortgage.

| Who | Owns | Owes | Net wealth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young family | + the flat, $300,000 | + mortgage, $300,000 | No change |
| Bank | + the mortgage loan, $300,000 | + the sellers' new deposit, $300,000 | No change |
| Retired couple | - the flat, + a $300,000 deposit | nothing | No change. The asset changed form |
| The world | + $300,000 of claims | + $300,000 of debt | Unchanged. Debt rose, wealth only moved |

Debt went up and nobody got richer or poorer. Now the second deal: the US government runs a $1 trillion deficit, and half the new bonds are bought by a US pension fund, half by Japanese investors.

| Who | What happens | Net wealth |
|---|---|---|
| US government | Owes $1T of new bonds, spends the money on wages and transfers | Falls $1T |
| US households | Receive $1T of new deposits, then move $0.5T into a pension fund that buys bonds | Rise $1T: $0.5T in deposits and $0.5T in pension claims backed by bonds |
| Japanese investors | Buy $0.5T of bonds with dollar deposits they already held from selling goods to the US | No change, they swapped a dollar deposit for a bond |
| The US as a nation | Owes Japan the same amount as before, in a new form: a bond instead of a deposit | No change in this deal. Its foreign position fell earlier, when the US paid for the goods |
| The world | Every IOU has an owner | Unchanged |

Inside the US, the government's new debt is the private sector's new asset, so "we owe it to ourselves" is right for that half. The foreign half is a claim of Japan on future US income, and it was created when the US bought more from the world than it sold. That is why only countries, not the world, can owe outside. The US's net foreign position was about minus $26.5 trillion at the end of 2024 (IMF) and minus $27.5 trillion at the end of 2025 (BEA), while Japan's was about plus $3.5 trillion (end 2024). A net foreign position is not the same as foreign debt: part of the US figure is foreigners owning US shares and companies, and stock prices move it a lot (BEA).

So what survives the cancellation? Three things. Real assets: land, houses, offices, factories, machines, roads and software, worth roughly the world's $600 trillion of net worth (McKinsey, via press), about two thirds of it real estate (Level 1, "The titled world"). Assets that are nobody's IOU, like gold bullion and bitcoin, which are small next to property. And, in household wealth only, the market's view of companies' future earnings above what their books show, because shares are counted at market price. In national net worth that extra cancels, because a share is also a claim on the company. World wealth, measured in dollars, is mostly the price of real things.

- Q: If every debt is somebody's asset, who is the world in debt to?
  A: Itself. The $348T of debt the IIF counts is matched by $348T of claims held by people, pension funds, banks and central banks. Only countries can owe outside: the US's net foreign position was about minus $26.5T at the end of 2024, Japan's about plus $3.5T. (builds on [The musical chairs question](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-07.md))
- Q: Why is the richest country also the biggest debtor?
  A: Because lenders lend against assets and income, and because the world wants dollar assets. The US holds about 35% of world household wealth and owes about 29% of world nonfinancial debt. Its net foreign position, about minus $26.5T at the end of 2024, is part debt and part foreign ownership of US shares and firms, and the US can owe it in its own currency. (builds on [The country leaderboard: deep markets and the game nations play](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-leader.md))

### Part 2 of 3. How it grew, and how to hold it in your head

### Price, not pile

Debt and wealth can both grow at once, for four reasons. A loan that builds a new house adds a debt and a new real asset. A loan that buys an existing house adds a debt and a deposit, and more credit chasing the same houses lifts their price, which shows up as more wealth. Inflation lifts both in dollars. And the two are counted differently: debt is a gross count of IOUs, net worth is what is left after they cancel.

How much of new wealth is new things? McKinsey's 2026 report, as summarized in the press, found that only about 20% of the rise in household wealth in 2025 came from net new investment, and about 58% came from higher valuations above inflation. The rest was inflation itself. The report was not opened for this chapter, so treat the split as approximate. The direction matches Level 1's "The titled world": the same buildings became worth more.

Here is every way world debt and world wealth can grow or shrink, with a size for 2025 and for a stress year.

| What moves | Debt | Wealth | 2025 | Stress year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New borrowing | Up | No change on the day, up if it builds something | +$28.8T in dollars, flattered by a weaker dollar (IIF, $319.5T to $348.3T) | 2020: a record +$35T (IIF) |
| New real investment | None or up | Up | about $8T, a fifth of the $40T household gain (McKinsey, via press) | not sized |
| Higher prices of existing assets | None | Up, or down when prices fall | about $23T, 58% of the household gain (McKinsey, via press) | 2022: household wealth fell 2.4%, to $454.4T (UBS and Credit Suisse, via press) |
| Inflation | Up in dollars, down as a share of output | Up in dollars | The IIF ratio fell for a fifth year, to about 308% | 2020 to 2024: the BIS ratio fell from 285% to 235% |
| The dollar ruler | Up when the dollar falls | Up when the dollar falls | The euro rose almost 9% against the dollar (UBS) | 2022: IIF debt fell about $4T as the dollar rose |
| Repayment and default | Down | Moves from lender to borrower, no change for the world | not sized | not sized |

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: World wealth rose about 10.8% in 2025 (UBS). Did the world build 10% more things?
  A: No. Only about a fifth of the household gain came from new investment, and most of the rest from higher prices. A weaker dollar also lifted the dollar value of non-US wealth. (builds on [The yardstick problem](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-10.md))
- Q: Real estate is about two thirds of world net worth. What happens to world wealth if house prices fall 20%?
  A: Measured wealth falls by roughly 13% of net worth (two thirds times 20%, a model using McKinsey's 2021 finding that real estate is about two thirds of net worth). No house is lost and no debt is repaid. Debt stays at face value, so everyone's debt looks bigger next to what they own. (builds on [The titled world: why abundance runs on credit, and who keeps the balance](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-titled.md))

### The ruler moves: the dollar resizes the world

Every world total is counted in dollars. When the dollar rises, everything outside the US looks smaller, and when it falls, everything looks bigger, with nothing real changing. Japan shows how large this effect can be.

| Japan's nonfinancial debt | End 2020 | End 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| In yen | 2,273 trillion | 2,340 trillion | +3% |
| In US dollars | $22.05T | $14.94T | -32% |
| As a share of Japan's GDP | 410% | 351% | -59 points |

Japan's debt rose 3% in yen and fell 32% in dollars, because the yen fell from about 103 to about 157 per dollar. Its debt also fell as a share of its economy, because inflation returned and lifted nominal output. Nobody repaid anything. The same ruler worked the other way in 2025: the euro rose almost 9% against the dollar, which is part of why wealth in Europe, the Middle East and Africa grew about 17.5% in dollars that year (UBS).

In 2022 the ruler worked the other way for the whole world. The dollar rose, and world debt measured in dollars fell about $4 trillion, to just under $300 trillion, the IIF's first annual fall since 2015. World household wealth fell too, about 2.4% to $454.4 trillion on the UBS and Credit Suisse count (via press), as higher interest rates cut asset prices. Nobody repaid anything.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: Japan's debt fell by a third in dollars from 2020 to 2025. Did Japan repay?
  A: No. In yen it rose 3% (2,273 to 2,340 trillion yen, BIS). The yen fell against the dollar, so the ruler shrank the number. (builds on [The yardstick problem](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-10.md))
- Q: World wealth rose 10.8% in dollars in 2025. Measured in ounces of gold, did it rise or fall?
  A: It fell, by about a third. Gold rose about 66% in 2025 (press), so 1.108 divided by 1.66 is about 0.67 (a model). Neither ruler is the right one alone. Check big claims with more than one ruler. (builds on [The yardstick problem](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-10.md))

| Mid 2026 | Health, energy, AI and defense spending | over $365T (IIF, via press) | about 305% in Q1 2026 (IIF) | no estimate yet |

Three things stand out. Debt grew faster than output in almost every period since 1971, from about one year of world output (a model) to about two and a half. It grew in jumps, in 2008 to 2010 and above all in 2020, when a record $35 trillion was added in one year (IIF). And after 2020 the world ratio fell for years while the dollar amount kept climbing, apart from 2022, when it fell about $4 trillion as a strong dollar shrank everything counted outside the US. Inflation and the ruler did that, not repayment. The fall did not last in dollars: debt rose about $46 trillion from the end of 2024 to mid 2026 ($319.5 trillion to over $365 trillion, IIF), and the BIS ratio rose again in 2025.

This was not the first time. After the Second World War the US and the UK shrank their government debt as a share of output without repaying it, by growing and by keeping interest rates below inflation (Level 1's "The seasons of money", citing Reinhart and Sbrancia, 2011). The years after 2020 were a short replay.

> The sources do not always agree on direction. In 2025 the BIS measure of world debt to output rose (235% to 246%) while the IIF's fell (to about 308%). The likely reason is how each converts currencies when the dollar falls during a year. It is a reminder that a single year's move in a world ratio can depend on who is counting.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: The world debt ratio fell for years after 2020 while dollar debt kept climbing. How can both be true, and what happened in 2022?
  A: Inflation raised nominal output faster than debt. On the BIS measure world nonfinancial debt went from $222T (285% of output) in 2020 to $237T (235%) in 2024. Nobody paid it down. In 2022 dollar debt even fell, by about $4T on the IIF count, because a strong dollar shrank everything measured in dollars outside the US. (builds on [The seasons of money](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-08.md))
- Q: When did world debt jump most: 1971, 2008 or 2020, and who borrowed?
  A: 2020: a record $35T in one year, with governments borrowing about $14T of it (IIF). In 2008 to 2010 the BIS ratio rose from 202% to 229%. In 1971 there was no jump: debt sat near 100% of world output (IMF). (builds on [1971, oil and the petrodollar](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-05.md))

### Make the numbers brain-sized

A trillion is too big to picture, so shrink it. One trillion seconds is about 31,700 years. Counting the IIF's $348 trillion at a dollar a second would take about 11 million years. Here are three better ways to hold the world in your head.

> **The US and Japan.** The US is the richest country and the largest net debtor to the rest of the world, about minus $26.5T at the end of 2024. It can borrow abroad in its own currency because the world wants dollars. The bigger currency risk in world debt sits outside the US: about $14T of dollar credit owed by borrowers abroad at the end of September 2025 (BIS), which gets heavier for them when the dollar rises. Japan's government owes about twice its output, yet Japan as a nation is owed $3.5T by the world. The Bank of Japan held about 43% of the government's bills and bonds at the end of 2025 (41% by June 2026) and foreigners about 13%, so Japan's debt is mostly owed from one Japanese pocket to another, in yen. Its government debt is 193% of output on the BIS basis, about 207% gross on the IMF's, and about 136% net of the government's own financial assets (IMF). Each figure is right on its own basis. The risk shows up as a weak yen, not as foreign creditors walking away.

> **Germany and Italy.** One currency, opposite structures. Germany's state owes 63% of output, Italy's 137%. Yet the typical Italian adult is richer than the typical German one: median wealth $131,001 against $53,485 (UBS, modelled). Italian families owe little, 36% of output against 48% in Germany. The usual explanation is home ownership, which is more common in Italy, though this chapter has not checked the figures. Italy's state is the debtor, its families much less so. Neither country prints its own currency, so investors can charge either one a risk premium when they worry. Countries with their own central bank pay such premia too, as Britain found in 2022.

> **France.** Same euro, a different worry. The French state owes about 116% of output, less than Italy's 137% (BIS), and French companies carry the heaviest debt in this table, 155% of output. Investors outside France, including other euro area investors and the ECB, hold about 54% of French government debt (Eurostat), and France owes the world a net $0.7T (end 2024). Yet in early October 2026 investors charged France more than Italy to borrow for ten years: about 1.4 points over Germany for France, against about 1.2 for Italy (press). They were reacting to France's deficit and politics more than to the size of its debt. A debt ratio is where the analysis starts, not where it ends.

> **The UK and Canada.** House-rich households with heavy mortgages. Canadian household debt is about 100% of output and British about 74%, among the highest in the G7. When interest rates rise, these families feel it first, because their mortgages reset within a few years. Canada is a large net creditor abroad, partly thanks to big pension funds investing overseas, while the UK is a small net debtor.

> **Switzerland.** A tiny state and heavy private balance sheets. The Swiss government owes only 25% of output, Swiss households 123%, the highest in the table, much of it mortgages that owners often keep rather than repay. Yet Swiss adults are the richest on Earth on average ($910,382) and the country owns far more abroad than it owes. High gross debt, very high net wealth: proof that gross debt alone says little.

> **China.** Debt-built growth, saved at home. China's total nonfinancial debt is about 300% of output, above the US, and its companies, including local government financing vehicles, carry most of it (143%). Yet China is a net creditor abroad, because its savings are lent at home, through Chinese banks, in yuan. Falling property prices have dragged on its household wealth.

> **Malaysia.** A smaller currency with a foreign window. Malaysia's nonfinancial debt is about 224% of output: government 65%, households 70% (high for an emerging market, including mortgages and car loans), companies 89%. It owes the world about as much as it owns abroad. Foreigners hold about a fifth of Malaysian Government Securities (20.1% in July 2026), enough that a jump in US yields can pull money out and push the ringgit down, as Level 1's "The view from a smaller currency" described.

**Check yourself.** Q: A friend says the world is $500 trillion in debt and only worth $300 trillion. What would you ask first, and what two things would you tell them?
A: Ask: which debt? Then, first: on the usual meaning, loans and bonds, the world owes about $348T to $365T and is worth $518T to $600T net, so the order flips. **Check yourself.** Q: Why can Japan owe twice its output and still be a creditor nation?

**Check yourself.** Q: World debt to output fell in 2023 and 2024 while the dollar amount rose. What did the work?
A: Inflation. Nominal output grew faster than debt, so the ratio fell without anyone paying debt down.
