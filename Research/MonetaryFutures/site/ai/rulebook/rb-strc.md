# Digital credit, dissected: Saylor's 12% and money moving between baskets

*Michael Saylor pitches Bitcoin as digital capital, STRC as digital credit paying 12%, and a future dollar token paying 8%. This chapter takes the pitch apart claim by claim, follows the money step by step, and asks what really moves when money moves between baskets.*

**In short**
- In October 2026 Michael Saylor of Strategy (once MicroStrategy) pitched a ladder of new money in five layers: Bitcoin as digital capital, STRC as digital credit paying 12%, the company's shares as digital equity, one day a dollar token paying 8%, and digital yield, income paid as often as daily.
- STRC is a preferred share, not a loan or a deposit. It has no end date, no right to your $100 back and no vote, and the company sets its 12% each month. Bitcoin covers it about 4 to 6 times today, and new borrowing would lower that.
- Of 28 claims checked, 11 hold, 11 are spin (true facts in a misleading frame), 3 are wrong and 3 cannot be checked. The numbers about today hold. The labels are where it slips: "strongest credit in the world" for a junk-rated issuer, and a dollar token paying 8% that US law does not allow.
- The flywheel runs both ways. In 2025 Strategy bought more Bitcoin than was mined. In the third quarter of 2026 it raised $5.41 billion, spent only $0.59 billion on Bitcoin and sold Bitcoin to help pay dividends.
- Money moving between baskets? Read three meters. Dollars: trades only move them, bank loans against Bitcoin create new ones. Ownership and risk: these cross, Bitcoin into listed companies, its risk onto savers. Market value: it changes with no dollar crossing.
- Before you believe any account paying 12%, ask who promises the $100, where the 12% comes from, and what happens if the asset halves. Nothing here is advice.

This chapter comes in four parts. Parts 1 to 3 take about ten minutes each. Part 4 is about twice as long. Each part ends with a stop box. The six words you need are in the box below. Nothing here is advice. The aim is to make you a sharper judge of a big pitch.

**Six words you need.** 
- **Preferred stock:** A share paid before the ordinary shares (also called common stock, traded as MSTR) but after every lender. Not a loan, so nothing has to be repaid.
- **Par:** The $100 STRC aims to trade at. A target the company works to hold, not a promise.
- **Perpetual:** No end date. You get money back only by selling to someone else.
- **Convertible note and put:** A loan the lender can swap for shares at a set price, the conversion price. The put lets the lender ask for cash back on set dates instead. Lenders do that when the share price is below the conversion price, because swapping would give them less.
- **mNAV:** What the market says the company is worth (the price of all its shares, with or without its debt and preferred added) divided by the value of the Bitcoin it holds. Above 1, the market values the company at more than its Bitcoin. Today it is about 1.1 counting its debt and preferred, about 0.9 counting the ordinary shares alone (4 November 2026).
- **Gross and net coverage:** How many times over the Bitcoin could pay the claims, meaning what the company owes to lenders and preferred holders. Net first subtracts the company's cash from the claims, so it reads higher.

### Part 1 of 4. The pitch, and what STRC really is

### 1. The pitch in five layers

In a talk recorded in October 2026, Michael Saylor, executive chairman of Strategy, laid out a ladder. It is worth knowing because it is one of the boldest ideas about future money in circulation.

| Layer | What he means | His example |
|---|---|---|
| Digital capital | Bitcoin as digital gold: scarce, capped at 21 million, something a robot cannot make | Bitcoin itself |
| Digital credit | A security paying a steady income, backed by Bitcoin, the way gold once backed credit | STRC, paying 12% a year |
| Digital equity | Whatever is left after the credit is paid: the company's ordinary shares | MSTR, Strategy's common stock |
| Digital money | A future dollar token with no price swings, paying 6% to 8% | Not yet built |
| Digital yield | Income from all of the above, paid as often as daily | STRC has paid daily since 3 November 2026 |

His argument in one line: if gold rising 10% a year let you sell credit paying 5%, then Bitcoin rising 20% a year lets you sell credit paying 10%. Strategy sells that credit to the public, buys Bitcoin with the money, and hopes Bitcoin rises faster than the 12% it pays. Any gap goes to its ordinary shareholders, and the gap can be negative. The 12% itself is owed in dollars. Part 2 tests this argument against history.

### 2. What STRC really is

Read the contract, not the label. STRC is preferred stock: a share in a company that gets paid before the ordinary shares but after every lender. It is not a loan, a bond or a bank account.

| Question | The answer in STRC's own documents |
|---|---|
| Does it end? | No. It is perpetual. There is no date when you get your money back |
| Can I demand $100 back? | No. Only if someone takes over the company. Otherwise you sell at the market price |
| Who sets the 12%? | The company, each month, at its "sole and absolute discretion". It can cut by up to 0.25 point a month. If one-month SOFR (a short-term dollar rate, about 4%) also falls, it can cut by that fall too. It can never go below SOFR |
| Must it pay? | Only "when, as and if declared" by the board. Missed dividends pile up and must be paid before any dividend on shares ranked below STRC, such as the ordinary shares |
| Where does it rank? | Behind about $6.75 billion of debt, almost all convertible notes, and behind another preferred, STRF. Strategy's other preferreds (STRE, STRK and STRD) rank behind STRC |
| Can I vote? | No, not even to elect directors if dividends stop. Only on changes that hurt STRC and some mergers |
| Can claims be put ahead of mine? | Yes. Any new debt ranks ahead of STRC without asking you |
| What backs it? | 849,327 Bitcoin (about $68 billion at $80,500) and a $4.79 billion dollar reserve. Nothing is pledged to STRC alone |
| How big is it? | About $8.2 billion on 1 November 2026, down from about $10.5 billion in July, after buybacks |
| How is the issuer rated? | B- from S&P, a credit rating agency (October 2025). That grade is called junk: riskier than the safer investment grades |

So why does it trade so close to $100? The company sells new STRC only above $100, buys it back below, and raises the rate when buyers lose interest. That $100 is a target, not a promise. And the board that sets your rate owes its main legal duty to the ordinary shareholders. Your rights are only what the contract says.

- **Myth:** STRC is like a bank account that pays 12%.
- **Reality:** A bank account is a debt the bank must repay, and in Malaysia PIDM insures it up to RM250,000 (in the US, the FDIC insures up to $250,000). STRC is a share with no repayment date and no insurance, and its price at the end of trading on 26 June 2026 was $74.57. It is well backed today, but it is not a deposit.

What STRC does better than most of this table: its backing is published every Monday in SEC filings, it is large today against the claims ahead of it, it is not pledged to anyone else, and it trades more each day than any other US preferred stock checked (about $156 million a day).

Accounting and law, briefly. Since 2025 Strategy carries Bitcoin at market value, so reported profit swings with the price: $19.53 billion of net income in the third quarter of 2026 came from unrealized Bitcoin gains, not cash. The preferred shares sit in its accounts as temporary equity. Delaware law lets a company pay dividends only out of surplus, which on today's numbers binds only if Bitcoin falls to about $17,700 to $23,500. The founder's class B shares carry ten votes each, about a third of all votes on the company's share counts (to be confirmed in the proxy).

### Part 2 of 4. Claim by claim

We checked 28 of the talk's claims against Strategy's own SEC filings (reports to the US market regulator), market data, history and the law. "Spin" means the facts are true but the frame misleads. The claims about today's numbers hold. The words wrapped around them are where it slips.

|---|---|---|

> **The pattern behind the verdicts.** The claims that hold are about today's numbers on the company's own method. The claims that fail come in three kinds: labels that borrow trust from safer products (credit, bank account, stablecoin, strongest), stories with the dates bent (the return table above), and sums that skip a step (the claim that selling $10 billion of credit "made" $10 billion). The full audit box has more of each. So ask first what a thing is in law, then what it pays.

And in fairness, the critics' sharpest claim, that it is a Ponzi scheme, also fails the test. A Ponzi scheme pays old investors only with new investors' money and hides that it has no real asset. Strategy owns a real, public asset worth several times STRC, and discloses every week where the dividend money comes from. The true point inside the criticism is narrower: the dividends depend on the company continuing to raise new money, because its software business earned only $14.3 million of cash from its normal business in nine months. So the 12% is paid in dollars from three places: the dollar reserve, money from selling new shares (ordinary or preferred) and, when those run short, selling Bitcoin. Bitcoin's rise pays nothing unless Bitcoin is sold.

### Part 3 of 4. The flywheel, and what turns it backwards

Saylor calls it a triple flywheel. Sell credit, buy Bitcoin, the price rises a little, the company's shares are worth more than the Bitcoin it holds, so selling shares buys even more Bitcoin per share, the cushion under the credit grows, and the credit gets cheaper to sell. Each link pushes the next.

*[Picture on the web page: Eight links. In a bull market each pushes the next. In a bear market each link can run backwards, and the question is which brake holds first.]*

The key link is the premium, mNAV: the company's value compared with its Bitcoin. Above 1, selling new shares buys more Bitcoin per share. For example, if each share stands for $100 of Bitcoin but sells for $110, each new share brings in $110 of Bitcoin, so every share ends up with a little more. Below 1, it does the opposite: a new share sold for $90 brings in less Bitcoin than each old share already holds, so every share ends up with a little less. The company has said it may then sell Bitcoin instead (reported December 2025). The premium was about 2.5 to 3.5 at the late-2024 peak (press estimates). On 4 November 2026 it was about 1.1 on the company's measure, which adds its debt and preferred to the share value and takes away its cash, and about 0.9 on the shares alone.

| Brake | Where it bites | Reading in early November 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Bitcoin against what Strategy paid | Below $75,451 the whole pile is worth less than it cost | $80,500, only 6.7% above cost. The 2026 low was about $57,700 on 1 July |
| The premium (mNAV) | Below 1, selling shares to pay dividends shrinks Bitcoin per share, and the company says it may sell Bitcoin instead | About 1.1 on the company's measure, 0.9 on the shares alone (4 November) |

| Convertible notes | Holders can demand cash when the share price is below the conversion price | $5.91 billion can be put back for cash from September 2027 to September 2028. Every conversion price is above today's share price |

> **The real stress point.** Not the 12% dividends. The convertible notes. Two years of dividends (about $3 billion) plus the $5.91 billion of notes holders can cash in come to about $8.9 billion, against $4.92 billion of dollars on hand, a gap of about $4 billion. Strategy can refinance, sell shares or sell Bitcoin. Closing the gap by selling Bitcoin would take about 99,000 coins if Bitcoin halved, 60% of a year's new supply. When note holders ask for their cash, it tests whether Strategy can borrow again or sell new shares to pay them. It is not a default, as long as lenders and share buyers are still willing.

### It already ran backwards once

In the third quarter of 2026 Strategy raised $5.41 billion by selling shares, but spent only $0.59 billion on Bitcoin. The rest went into its dollar reserve, buying back STRC below $100, and dividends. It sold $348.6 million of Bitcoin in the quarter (at the value on its books), $239.9 million of it to pay dividends. The share sales were defending the credit instead of buying Bitcoin. The talk did not mention it.

### Part 4 of 4. Money moving between baskets: three meters

Here is the big idea to test. It comes from the person who runs this site, not from Saylor: future money moving between baskets. The pitch says digital credit pulls money out of the old basket (money funds, government bills, bank deposits) and into the new one (Bitcoin). To see what really moves, read three meters, not one.

| Meter | What moves it | Digital credit | Stablecoins |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Dollars | Trades move dollars between people. A bank loan, or a bank buying from a non-bank, creates new ones. Repaying destroys them | Buying STRC and Bitcoin moves dollars to the Bitcoin seller. A bank loan against Bitcoin creates new ones | Buying a bill moves dollars to the bill's seller |
| 2. Ownership and risk | Who holds which asset and carries its swings | Bitcoin into a listed company, its risk onto savers | Bills behind crypto tokens, the issuer's risk onto holders |
| 3. Market value | The last trade sets the price of every unit. If one buyer pays $1,000 more for one coin, all the coins are counted as worth $1,000 more each, though only one buyer paid | Buying can lift all 20 million mined coins by more than was spent, or fail to | Near $1 while the backing holds |

*[Picture on the web page: Follow one $10,000. The saver's dollars pass through the wrapper (the legal package around an asset, here STRC and Strategy) to whoever sold the Bitcoin and stay in the dollar basket. What crosses the line is Bitcoin, into a listed company, and Bitcoin's risk, onto the saver. A bank loan against Bitcoin, not drawn here, would add new dollars.]*

1. A saver moves $10,000 from a money market fund into STRC. The fund sells $10,000 of bills. If a household or another fund buys them, dollars only move. If a bank buys them, it creates a new deposit to pay. The saver's dollars go to Strategy.
2. Strategy buys $10,000 of Bitcoin from a miner or an early holder. The dollars go to the seller, who parks them in a bank, a bill or a money fund. No dollar enters Bitcoin. Bitcoin enters a listed company.
3. Strategy pays the saver a daily dividend from its dollar reserve, which was raised by selling new shares. In effect new shareholders pay the preferred holders.
4. In a bear market Strategy sells Bitcoin to pay the dividend. A Bitcoin buyer's dollars go to the saver, and Bitcoin leaves the wrapper. This happened in the third quarter of 2026.
5. A different door: a bank lends $10,000 to someone who pledges Bitcoin. The bank creates a new $10,000 deposit, so the new basket helps create old-basket dollars. Repaying destroys it. Saylor calls this a tailwind. Its size today is unknown: no public total was found.

> **What really crosses the line.** In steps 1 to 4 dollars change hands inside the dollar basket, adding a little only if a bank buys the bills in step 1. What moves is ownership and risk: a listed company holds the Bitcoin in boom years, outside buyers in bust years, and the saver's claim on bills becomes a share resting on Bitcoin. In step 5 this site's picture is literally true: pledged Bitcoin brings new dollars into being. And market value moves with no dollar crossing. So money does move between baskets, as ownership, risk and value, and at the edges as new money.

### Go deeper: The ledger in detail: both floors, and the smaller channels

Two floors. Upstairs are bank reserves and the Fed's reverse repo. Downstairs are the deposits the public holds. A money fund that pays out by drawing on the Fed's reverse repo pushes new reserves and deposits into being, but money funds and other non-banks used only about $0.2 billion of it on 28 October 2026, so today the pipe barely touches the upstairs floor.
