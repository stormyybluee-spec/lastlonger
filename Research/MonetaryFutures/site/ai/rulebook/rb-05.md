# 1971, oil and the petrodollar

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-05 . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*On a Sunday night in August 1971, the last thread between the dollar and gold was cut, and oil quietly took gold's place in the loop.*

Chapter 3 ended with a timer. Foreign governments held more dollars than the US held gold. Chapter 4 showed why the world kept piling up dollars anyway. This chapter is the moment the timer ran out, and the surprising thing that happened next.

### The run on the bakery

Through the late 1960s, US spending rose on two fronts at once: the Vietnam War abroad and the Great Society programs at home. Inflation crept up. Foreign central banks looked at their growing piles of dollars and at America's shrinking pile of gold, and started asking for gold. In the summer of 1971 Britain reportedly asked to protect a large part of its dollar holdings. The bakery in our Chapter 3 picture had a crowd at the door.

### August 15, 1971

President Richard Nixon gathered his advisers at Camp David and then went on national television on a Sunday evening. He announced a package: a 90-day freeze on wages and prices, a 10% surcharge on imports, and the step that changed the world's money.

> "I have directed Secretary Connally to suspend temporarily the convertibility of the dollar into gold or other reserve assets." (Richard Nixon, televised address, August 15, 1971)

Temporarily turned out to mean permanently. Foreign central banks could no longer swap dollars for gold. The dollar was now backed by nothing physical at all. It was backed by the US government's promise, the US economy, and the fact that the whole world already used it.

### From fixed to floating

Governments tried to patch the system. In December 1971 the Smithsonian Agreement devalued the dollar to $38 an ounce of gold and reset other pegs. In February 1973 the official price was raised again to $42.22. By March 1973 the major currencies gave up pegs and began to float, meaning their prices against each other were set every day by markets.

> That $42.22 is still in the US government's books today. The Treasury values its 261.5 million ounces of gold at exactly $42.2222 an ounce, as of September 2026, while the market price is around $4,100. A number set in a 1973 emergency is still sitting in the national accounts.

- **Myth:** Gold still backs the dollar.
- **Reality:** It does not. No one can swap dollars for gold at a fixed price, and no law ties the amount of dollars to the amount of gold. The US government still owns a lot of gold, but it is an asset on the shelf, not a backing for the currency. What anchors the dollar today is the taxing power of the US government, the size of the US economy, and the Federal Reserve's management of interest rates.

### Oil enters the loop

Two years after Nixon's announcement, in October 1973, Arab members of OPEC (the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries) imposed an oil embargo on the United States and other countries over the Arab Israeli war. Oil prices roughly quadrupled within months, from about $3 a barrel to about $12. Suddenly, oil exporters were collecting a flood of dollars.

Where would that flood go? In 1974, US Treasury Secretary William Simon traveled to Saudi Arabia. The two governments reached an understanding. Saudi Arabia would invest a large share of its oil earnings in US Treasury securities, through special arrangements that kept its purchases out of the normal auctions and kept its holdings secret. In return the US offered military cooperation and security. Oil continued to be priced in dollars, which was already the norm.

The details stayed hidden for decades. In 2016 a Freedom of Information Act request by Bloomberg News uncovered the arrangement, and the US Treasury published Saudi Arabia's Treasury holdings for the first time.

> **Picture this.** Recall the neighbor who deposits the family's IOUs right back with the family. Now give that neighbor a gas station that every driver in the world must use, with prices quoted in the family's IOUs. The neighbor's pile of IOUs grows enormously, and the neighbor parks it with the family. That is the petrodollar: oil priced in dollars, and the oil money recycled into US government debt.

### What the petrodollar was, and what it was not

- It was three things: oil invoiced in dollars, Gulf oil surpluses recycled into Treasuries and US banks, and a security relationship between Washington and Riyadh.
- It was not the dollar's backing. The dollar would remain the world's main currency without it, because of the pillars in Chapter 4.
- It is not suddenly over. A viral story claimed a 50-year petrodollar pact expired in June 2024. This project's research found that claim false. There was no such dated pact to expire.
- It is fading slowly. Saudi Arabia now holds about $141 billion of Treasuries, about 1.5% of foreign holdings, and in 2026 Gulf states borrowed in markets more than they recycled. Electric vehicles are slowly eating into oil demand.

> In the 1970s, banks also recycled petrodollars by lending them to developing countries in Latin America. When US interest rates jumped in the early 1980s, those borrowers could not pay. Mexico defaulted in 1982 and much of Latin America lost a decade. Recycled money always lands somewhere, and the landing is not always soft.

### The Great Inflation

With no gold anchor and two oil shocks (1973 and again after the Iranian revolution in 1979), US inflation climbed through the decade. Consumer price inflation peaked near 14.8% in early 1980. Savers who held cash or long-term bonds watched their wealth melt. Workers saw wages chase prices in a race they kept losing. Borrowers with fixed-rate loans, by contrast, quietly gained, because they repaid in cheaper dollars.

Policymakers tried wage and price controls, which caused shortages, and stop and go policy, which tightened just long enough to cause pain and then loosened again before inflation was beaten. People came to expect inflation, and expectations started to drive it.

### Volcker breaks the fever

In August 1979 President Jimmy Carter appointed Paul Volcker to chair the Federal Reserve. On October 6, 1979, Volcker announced a new approach that let interest rates rise as high as needed to slow money growth. The federal funds rate, the Fed's key rate, reached close to 20% in 1980 and 1981. Mortgage rates went above 18%.

The cure was brutal. The US went through two recessions in quick succession. Unemployment reached 10.8% at the end of 1982. Farmers drove tractors to Washington to protest. But inflation fell to around 3% by 1983, and the Fed earned back its credibility. A 40-year era of falling interest rates began, which Chapter 8 calls the long bond summer.

| Year | Event | What it meant for money |
|---|---|---|
| 1971 | Gold window closed | The dollar became a pure promise |
| 1973 | Floating rates, oil embargo | Currencies traded freely, oil prices quadrupled |
| 1974 | US and Saudi arrangement | Oil dollars recycled into Treasuries |
| 1979 | Second oil shock, Volcker appointed | Inflation peaked, then rates soared |
| 1982 | Deep recession, Mexico default | Inflation broken, debt crisis abroad |

The owner's Ledger Age series listed five effects of 1971: the dollar became a pure promise, unlimited printing became possible, inflation became the default, the petrodollar arrived, and cheap credit flowed to those who could borrow and buy assets. All five are well supported, with one sharpening. Printing became possible without a gold limit, but it is not unlimited in practice. The limits moved from metal to politics and markets: how much inflation voters will tolerate, and how much debt the bond market will absorb at what price. Volcker proved the limit is real when it is enforced.

**Check yourself.** Q: After 1971, what does the dollar's value rest on, if not gold?
A: On trust in the US government and its taxing power, the size and openness of the US economy, the Fed's management of interest rates and inflation, and the network effect of the whole world already using dollars.

**Check yourself.** Q: Who gained and who lost in the 1970s inflation?
A: Borrowers with fixed-rate loans and owners of real assets like gold, land and oil gained. Savers in cash and bonds, people on fixed incomes and workers whose wages lagged prices lost.
