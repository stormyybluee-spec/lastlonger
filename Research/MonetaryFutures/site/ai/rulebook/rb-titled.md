# The titled world: why abundance runs on credit, and who keeps the balance

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 19. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-titled.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-titled . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Divide the world's wealth by everyone and it looks like plenty. Here is why most of that plenty is a price set on future credit, why the base layer was titled long ago, and what role each big country plays in keeping a crowded system from tipping over.*

This is the last chapter before the Rulebook, and it pulls the whole book together around one thought experiment. Take everything the world owns, divide it by everyone alive, and ask: if there is so much, why do so many people feel they are locked out?

### The thought experiment: divide it all

UBS estimates the world's personal wealth at about $470 trillion at the end of 2024. Divided by about 8.2 billion people, that is roughly $57,000 for every man, woman and child, or about $230,000 for a family of four. On paper the world is rich. Yet the same report finds that about 42% of adults own less than $10,000, while about 1.5% own more than $1 million.

*[Picture on the web page: The equal share on paper against what adults actually hold. Brackets from the UBS Global Wealth Report 2025, end of 2024.]*

### Now divide the base layer instead

The $57,000 is a nominal number. It is a price, measured in dollars at today's interest rates, not a quantity of things. To see the real pie, divide the base layer instead.

*[Picture on the web page: Each person's equal share of the world, first in paper, then in real things. Cropland from FAO (2023), energy from the Energy Institute (2025).]*

Each person's share of the world's cropland is about 0.2 hectares, roughly a quarter of a football pitch, to grow all of their food. Their share of all the energy humanity used in 2025, about 600 exajoules, is about 20,000 kWh a year, the same as 23 old 100-watt bulbs burning day and night, to run every farm, factory, truck, home and data center on their behalf. Savills' value for all the world's farmland works out to about $5,800 a person. Measured in things, the world is far less abundant than $57,000 suggests. Measured in claims, it looks rich, because claims can multiply faster than farmland or power plants.

> This is why the price of the base layer tends to climb in a credit-driven world. Each new round of credit creates more claims chasing the same farmland, the same city plots and the same energy. The claims multiply and the base layer does not, so its price rises. Owners of the base layer get richer without making anything new.

### Why the pie is smaller than it looks

- Most wealth is a price, not a pile. About two thirds of the world's net worth is real estate, and Savills values all the world's property at about $393 trillion at the start of 2025. Those values come from the last sales, as chapter 17 showed with market caps. If everyone tried to sell at once to share out the money, the price would collapse.
- Prices are future income divided by the interest rate. When rates fell from the early 1980s to 2021, the same buildings and companies became worth far more without producing more. McKinsey found that global net worth rose from about $156 trillion in 2000 to about $514 trillion in 2020, while for every $1 of new investment, liabilities grew almost $4, about $2 of it debt. Most of the new wealth was revaluation, not new things.
- Every financial asset is someone else's debt or someone's future income. Global debt reached about $365 trillion in mid-2026, about 310% of world output, according to the Institute of International Finance. A bond, a deposit or a loan is a claim on a borrower's future earnings. Count the claim as wealth and you must also count the debt.
- What can really be shared each year is output, not prices. The world produces roughly $115 to $120 trillion of goods and services a year, about $14,000 per person, and most of that already pays for wages, rents, interest and taxes.

*[Picture on the web page: A wide stack of claims balanced on a narrow base of real things. The higher the layer, the more it depends on future credit.]*

So the abundance we see is partly real, in machines, knowledge, networks and food, and partly a bet on future production, financed by credit and priced at today's interest rates. That is the owner's insight in plain terms: much of the world's apparent abundance is priced on future credit. When rates rise, as they did in 2026, the bet is repriced and the paper abundance shrinks, even though not one building or factory disappears.

### Money is debt, and debt is money

There is a deeper reason the paper pie and the real pie differ. Almost all modern money is somebody's debt. Chapter 6 showed that a bank loan creates a deposit. Your bank balance is the bank's debt to you. A dollar bill is a liability of the Federal Reserve, which holds mostly government bonds against it, and those bonds are the government's debt, to be paid from future taxes. Money is debt, and debt is money. If every loan in the world were repaid tomorrow, most of the money would vanish with them.

*[Picture on the web page: Every dollar someone owns is matched by a debt someone else owes. Only real things, gold and Bitcoin sit outside the mirror.]*

- To grow the money supply, the system must grow debt. That is why total wealth and total debt keep rising together.
- Paying debt down shrinks money. When too many people do it at once, as in 1929 to 1933, money disappears and prices fall.
- Add up the whole world and every financial asset cancels out against someone's debt. What remains, the world's true net worth, is the value of its real things: land, buildings, machines and the rest of the base layer.
- The exceptions are things that are nobody's IOU: gold, Bitcoin, land and goods. That is exactly why people run to them when they start to doubt the debts.

> So the $57,000 is not $57,000 of stuff. Much of it is deposits, bonds and pension claims, each matched by a debt somewhere else, owed by a company, a bank or a government that must pay it from future income. Net out the IOUs and what is left is the base layer plus the machines and buildings on top, priced at whatever today's interest rates allow.

### A world already titled

Underneath the claims sits the base layer: land, water, minerals, energy, ports, sea lanes, the best city plots, radio spectrum. Almost all of it was claimed long ago, recorded in a title registry or held by a state. Chapter 14 asked how a new player gets onto a board where no one gives out starting money. The harder truth is that the squares are already owned. Newcomers do not arrive on an empty board. They arrive as tenants.

When the base layer was up for grabs, it was often fought over with armies. European powers divided Africa among themselves after the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885. Japan pushed into Southeast Asia in 1941 after the United States cut off its oil. Iraq invaded oil-rich Kuwait in 1990. Today the contest is mostly fought with money instead: tariffs, sanctions, export controls on chips and rare earths, and who gets to price oil, finance mines and hold the world's savings. The wars over title turned into wars over price and access, though the older kind has not disappeared.

### The generational seat at the table

Within countries, the same pattern runs between generations. In the United States, households headed by baby boomers, born from 1946 to 1964, hold about 52% of household net worth in mid-2026, while millennials and Gen Z together hold about 11%, according to analyses of the Federal Reserve's distributional accounts. Households headed by someone under 40 held about 6.6% in early 2026, down from about 12% in 1989. Older people always own more than younger ones, but the gap has widened, because the assets the older generation bought were revalued upward by falling rates, and the young must buy the same assets at the higher price, usually with credit.

> **Picture this.** A Monopoly game where the first players bought every street while it was cheap. Late players start with only cash and pay rent on every lap. The bank keeps lending the late players money so the game can go on, because a game in which nobody can pay rent ends for the owners too.

> That last line is the key to the whole system. Owners need newcomers. Rents, mortgages, share prices and pension promises only hold if new buyers and tenants keep paying. So the system cannot lock newcomers out completely. It lets them in through wages, credit and a little inflation, on terms that keep the owners' titles valuable. This is the quiet balance the money managers of the world are actually running.

### Four levers that keep the balance

1. Credit: lend newcomers the price of a seat. It keeps asset prices up and lets the young buy in, at the cost of decades of payments to lenders.
2. Inflation: quietly shifts value from savers and lenders to borrowers, who are often younger. It shrinks old debts and lifts wages in money terms. Chapter 9 showed why indebted systems choose it.
3. Taxes and transfers: pensions, health care, child accounts and income support move part of the owners' income to people without assets. Chapter 14 and the Desk looked at what a universal income can and cannot carry.
4. New frontiers: new industries create new things to own before they are titled. Railways, oil, the internet and now AI, compute, energy and space are where newcomers can get title early.

When all four levers fail at once, history offers darker resets. The historian Walter Scheidel argued in The Great Leveler (2017) that across recorded history only four forces have reliably shrunk inequality on a large scale: mass-mobilization war, transformative revolution, state collapse and deadly pandemics. The levers above exist, in large part, to keep those four away.

### Who plays which role

*[Picture on the web page: A simplified map of the roles. Real countries mix several roles, and roles change over time.]*

| Who | Role in the system | What it gets | What it risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | Issues the reserve currency, borrows and buys from the world, guarantees security | Cheap borrowing, consumption, power | Debt, hollowed industry, division at home |
| China | The world's workshop and a giant saver, now the main rival in hardware | Jobs, technology, influence | Dependence on foreign buyers, low household spending |
| Japan, Germany, Korea, Taiwan | High-tech exporters that lend their surpluses back | Jobs and steady surpluses | Weak home demand, exposure to US policy |
| Gulf states and other energy exporters | Supply the base-layer fuel and recycle surpluses through state funds | Wealth and security ties | Oil price swings, dependence on protection |
| Resource exporters such as Australia, Brazil, Canada, Indonesia and Africa's miners | Supply minerals and food | Income in boom years | Price swings and fights over title |
| Hubs: UK, Switzerland, Singapore, Hong Kong, UAE | Custodians, bankers, courts and meeting places | Fees and talent | Dependence on trust and openness |
| India and other large, young economies | Labor and tomorrow's buyers | Growth | Entering on terms set by others |
| IMF, World Bank, BIS, WTO | Referees and lenders of last resort | Stability | Questions about whose rules they enforce |

Each role balances the others. Exporters need the US to keep buying. The US needs them to keep lending. Energy and resource exporters need both. Hubs need everyone to keep trusting the pipes. Countries also change roles: Britain went from workshop to banker, and China from workshop to rival. Every role change is a moment of stress for the whole system, which is why this desk watches the China truce, the Gulf and Japan so closely.

### Sovereign wealth funds: the countries' investment arms

Chapter 18 drew every country as a company with an investment arm. That arm is the sovereign wealth fund: a state-owned fund that turns a country's surplus, usually from oil, gas or trade, into ownership of assets around the world. Global SWF counted sovereign funds holding more than $15 trillion at the end of 2025. There are well over a hundred of them, but about a dozen giants hold most of the money, so twelve is a good number to remember.

*[Picture on the web page: The five jobs a sovereign fund can do. Most big funds do two or three at once.]*

| Fund | Country | Money from | Main job | Size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Government Pension Fund Global | Norway | Oil and gas | Saver. Owns about 1.5% of all listed companies | $2.39T (June 2026, published) |
| China Investment Corporation | China | Foreign reserves | Reserve investor | $1.57T total assets (end 2024, published) |
| SAFE Investment Company | China | Foreign reserves | Reserve investor, publishes nothing | about $1T or more (estimate) |
| Abu Dhabi Investment Authority | UAE | Oil | Saver with a global portfolio | about $1.18T (estimate) |
| Kuwait Investment Authority | Kuwait | Oil | Saver. Roots in 1953, the oldest of its kind | about $1T (estimate) |
| Public Investment Fund | Saudi Arabia | Oil and state assets | Developer and strategic arm: new cities, sports, games | about $905B (2025, published) |
| GIC | Singapore | Government reserves | Reserve investor | hundreds of billions (estimate) |

Sizes are approximate. Norway publishes its value every quarter, but several giants, including ADIA, GIC and China's SAFE, publish no official figure, so trackers estimate them and do not always agree. The UAE alone runs four funds above $250 billion: ADIA, Investment Corporation of Dubai, Mubadala and ADQ.

- Stabilizer: a shock absorber that saves in boom years and spends when oil or export prices crash.
- Saver: turns a resource that will run out into a permanent stake in the world's companies, for generations not yet born.
- Reserve investor: earns more on a country's surplus dollars than Treasury bills would.
- Developer: builds new industries at home so the country is not stuck selling one raw material. Malaysia's Khazanah Nasional plays this role.
- Strategic arm: buys stakes, ports, chip plants, football clubs and game studios abroad, which bring influence and access as well as returns.

> Read these funds through this chapter's lens. They convert a base layer that will run out, oil and gas, into titles on the rest of the world: shares, buildings, ports and companies. Norway turned its oil into about 1.5% of every listed company on earth. A small country cannot hold the deep-market crown, but it can buy a slice of the board itself. It is also how much of the petrodollar from chapter 5 comes back to the US and Europe, not only into Treasuries but into their companies.

The fund idea also touches the universal income debate. Alaska's Permanent Fund, built from oil revenue and worth about $89 billion, pays every eligible resident a yearly dividend: $1,200 in 2026, made up of $1,000 plus a $200 energy relief payment. It is the closest thing in the United States to a citizen's share of a titled base layer. The United States has historically had no national fund of this kind. A February 2025 executive order asked for a plan for one, and whether it becomes real is a tell worth watching.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why would an oil country rather own a slice of the world's companies than spend all its oil money at home?
A: Because oil runs out and its price swings. Spreading the money across the world's companies turns a temporary resource into permanent, diversified income, and avoids flooding the home economy with cash it cannot absorb, which would cause Dutch disease.

### Decoding the balance: a plan, or incentives?

> **The decode verdict.** A secret global plan by money managers to keep newcomers out forever: D3, folklore, about 10% as stated. Policy that consistently protects asset prices, because owners vote, lend, fund campaigns and hold the pension savings: D1, decoded-strong, about 80%. After 2008 and again in 2020 central banks moved quickly to support asset prices. Nobody needs to coordinate. Each actor's incentive points the same way: keep the titles valuable, keep the newcomers paying, and keep the four horsemen away.

> This is why the future this desk studies matters so much. Robots, AI and cheap energy could enlarge the base layer itself. If newcomers can own a share of that new capacity before it is titled, the gap can close. If the same owners title it first, the gap widens. Who gets title to the new base layer of energy, compute, data and orbit is the ownership question of this generation.

2. Banks create most money as loans, so the system needs ever more credit (chapter 6 and Level 2 chapter 2).
3. The seasons decide who wins, borrowers or savers, and debt decides which season governments choose (chapters 7 and 8).
4. Nations compete for the deep-market crown, and smaller currencies live by its rules (chapters 11 and 12).
5. The ruler we measure with keeps shrinking (chapter 13).
6. Newcomers start on a board with no free money, and new rails are laid on old anchors (chapters 14 and 15).
7. Paper values are claims on the future and can fall fast (chapters 16 and 17).
8. Every country runs like a company on that leaderboard, with taxes as its subscription fee and hubs competing for its talent (chapter 18).
9. Underneath it all, money is debt, the abundance on paper rests on credit, and the base layer was titled long ago. The system's real job is balancing owners and newcomers without calling the horsemen.

- **Myth:** If the world's wealth were shared out, everyone would be comfortable.
- **Reality:** Only on paper. Most of that wealth is the price of homes and companies at today's interest rates. Sell it all to divide it and the price collapses. What could really be shared is each year's output, about $14,000 per person, and most of it already pays for wages, rents, interest and taxes.

**Check yourself.** Q: Global personal wealth is about $470 trillion. Why can't it simply be handed out as $57,000 to each person?
A: Because most of it is the price of existing assets, valued at the last trade and at today's interest rates, and much of it is matched by debts. Selling it all to share it out would crash the price. What is really available each year is output, about $14,000 per person.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why does a system that favors owners still lend money to newcomers?
A: Because owners need newcomers to keep paying rent, interest and share prices. If newcomers could pay nothing, the owners' titles would lose their value. Credit keeps the game going.
