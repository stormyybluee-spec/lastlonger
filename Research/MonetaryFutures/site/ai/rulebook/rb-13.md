# Eye-openers: what most people never notice

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 16. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-13.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-13 . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Sixteen quiet facts about money that, once seen, cannot be unseen.*

Each chapter so far has hidden a few surprises. This chapter gathers them in one place, with a few new ones. Each is something that sits in plain sight in the official numbers, yet most people go their whole lives without noticing it. Read them slowly. For each one, ask yourself where it touches your own money.

### About your own money

> 1. Your bank deposit is a loan you made to the bank. The balance in your account is the bank's debt to you. You are an unsecured lender, protected by deposit insurance up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Above that, you are trusting the bank's books. That is why depositors ran from Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023.

> 2. Banks create money when they lend. They do not lend out your savings. A new loan creates a new deposit at the same instant, as the Bank of England explained in 2014. In the United States, reserve requirements have been zero since March 2020. Bank lending is limited by capital, demand, profit and the interest rate, not by a fixed multiple of reserves.

> 3. Treasury bills are the cash of the rich. In 2026 the average US savings account paid about 0.38%, while a three-month Treasury bill paid about 4.25%. On $50,000 that is about $190 a year against about $2,125. Wealthier savers and companies keep their cash in bills and money market funds. Many ordinary savers leave it in accounts that pay almost nothing, and the bank keeps the difference.

> 4. Most money is not cash at all. Paper notes and coins are a small share of the money supply. Most money is bank deposits, which are entries in ledgers. When people say the government prints money, what usually grows is ledger entries at banks and at the Fed.

### About the government's money

> 5. Inflation is a silent tax. No law is passed and no vote is taken, yet inflation moves buying power from people holding money and fixed-rate claims to people who owe money, and the largest debtor is the government. In 2026 the average interest rate on US government debt was about 3.2 to 3.5%, while nominal income had grown about 5.7% a year over the decade. That gap quietly shrinks the debt relative to the economy, paid for by bondholders and savers.

> "There is no subtler, no surer means of overturning the existing basis of society than to debauch the currency." (John Maynard Keynes, The Economic Consequences of the Peace, 1919)

> 6. Seigniorage: issuing money is profitable. A $100 bill costs a few cents to print and buys $100 of goods. More importantly, the Fed buys interest-paying bonds with money it creates, earns the interest, and in normal years sends its profits to the Treasury. Stablecoin issuers have found a private version: they take your dollar, buy Treasury bills and keep the yield.

> 7. The Fed can lose money and keep running. From late 2022, the Fed paid more interest on bank reserves than it earned on its bonds. Instead of asking for a bailout, it recorded the losses as a deferred asset, an IOU to itself to be repaid from future profits before it resumes sending money to the Treasury. A central bank that creates its own currency cannot run out of it. It can only lose credibility.

> 8. Interest on the national debt now exceeds defense. In fiscal year 2026 the US spent about $1,052 billion on net interest and about $965 billion on national defense. Interest was higher in 2024, 2025 and 2026. Interest took about 19.5% of all federal revenue. About 36% of the government's marketable debt reprices within a year, so if rates stay high, the bill keeps climbing.

> 9. US gold is still booked at $42.22 an ounce. The Treasury owns about 261.5 million ounces of gold and records it at the 1973 legal price, about $11 billion in total. At market prices it is worth about $1.08 trillion. Revaluing it would raise about one year of interest payments, once. It is a one-time gain, not a fix for deficits that recur every year.

> 10. Workers carry most of the federal tax load. In 2026 about 86% of federal receipts came from individual income and payroll taxes, and only about 7% from corporate taxes, down from about 11% two years earlier as companies expensed huge investments in AI. As machines take a larger share of income, this tax base is under quiet pressure.

### About the world's money

> 11. Sanctions run through dollar plumbing. Most international payments pass through dollar accounts at US banks. That gives Washington the power to cut a country, bank or person out of the system. When Western governments froze about $300 billion of Russia's reserves in 2022, every central bank noticed. Central banks then bought gold at a record pace, about 863 tonnes in 2025, because gold held at home cannot be frozen by someone else.

> 12. The Cantillon effect. In the 1700s the banker and writer Richard Cantillon noticed that new money does not reach everyone at once. Those who receive it first spend it before prices rise. Those who receive it last face higher prices first. This project's data refine the idea: in 2020 and 2021, checks sent directly to households lifted the bottom half's share of wealth from 1.7% to 2.4%. But owners of assets captured most of the dollar gains. Since late 2019 the top 10% took about 67.5% of the $78 trillion rise in household wealth.

> 13. Stablecoin issuers keep the interest. Under the GENIUS Act, holders of compliant US stablecoins cannot be paid interest by the issuer. The issuer invests the reserves in Treasury bills and keeps the yield, often sharing it with distributors. At a 4.25% bill rate, the whole market's float is worth about $11.7 billion a year. Each $1,000 left idle in a stablecoin gives up about $42.50 a year.

> 14. Mortgage rates are set more by the bond market than by the Fed. The Fed sets a short-term rate. Mortgage rates follow long-term Treasury yields, which reflect inflation fears, debt supply and foreign demand. In October 2026 the Fed's rate was 3.75 to 4.00%, while the 10-year Treasury yield was about 5.13% and the 30-year about 5.42%. In late 2024 the Fed cut rates and long-term yields rose anyway.

> 15. Safe assets are only safe in a given season. Long-term US Treasury bonds have essentially no risk of default, yet they lost close to half their market value from 2020 to 2023 as rates rose. Silicon Valley Bank failed in 2023 largely because it held safe bonds bought in a different season. Safety is a property of an asset in a season, not of the asset alone.

> 16. Platforms are the new toll booths. Producing goods has not become cheaper in dollar terms since 2019, but the cut taken by platforms has grown. Advertising rose from about 23.5% to about 41.9% of Amazon's seller services revenue between 2019 and early 2026. Etsy's take rate rose from 16.5% to 24.2%. None of this shows up as a separate line in the consumer price index.

**Check yourself.** Q: Pick the eye-opener that most affects you this month, and say why.
A: Any is fine. For many readers it is number 3: moving idle savings from a near-zero account to a Treasury bill or government money market fund can earn many times more for the same safety. For others it is number 14, because mortgage rates do not simply follow the Fed.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why can the Fed lose money without failing?
A: Because it creates the currency its debts are paid in. It records losses as a deferred asset to be covered from future profits. Its real risk is a loss of credibility, not running out of money.
