# How the world gets its dollars

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 04. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-04.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-04 . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Americans buy more than they sell, foreigners earn the difference in dollars, and those dollars come home as loans to the US government.*

Chapter 3 ended with a puzzle. The world wants dollars. How does it get them? The answer is the most important circuit in modern finance. Once you see it, you will notice it in the price of your phone, your mortgage rate and the news about sanctions.

### The loop in one breath

1. Americans, as a group, buy more goods and services from the world than they sell to it. This is the trade or current account deficit.
2. Paying for those imports sends dollars abroad. Foreign exporters now hold dollars.
3. The exporters, or their central banks, need somewhere safe to keep those dollars that also pays interest.
4. The deepest, safest place is US government debt: Treasury bills, notes and bonds. So they buy Treasuries, or other US assets such as stocks, companies and property.
5. The dollars flow back into the US financial system, help fund the US government's deficit and keep US interest rates lower than they would otherwise be.
6. With rates lower and goods cheap, Americans keep buying, and the loop turns again.

*[Picture on the web page: The recycling loop: the US deficit sends dollars abroad, foreigners earn them by exporting, and they send them back by buying US Treasuries and other US assets.]*

> **Picture this.** Imagine a family that shops every day at a neighbor's store and pays with IOUs. The neighbor does not cash the IOUs. Instead, the neighbor deposits them right back with the family, which pays a little interest. The family keeps shopping, the neighbor keeps selling, and the neighbor's pile of IOUs keeps growing. Both are happy as long as the neighbor trusts the family's IOUs. That is the dollar loop, in kitchen terms.

### Level one: you, the individual

The loop touches your life in at least two ways, even if you never think about foreign exchange.

- Cheap imports. Because foreign makers are happy to be paid in dollars and to hold them, Americans can buy goods from all over the world at low prices. The project's time price data show a television costs roughly 99.5% fewer hours of work than in 1993. Clothing, electronics and toys are cheap partly because of this loop.
- Lower borrowing costs. When foreigners buy Treasuries, they push up their prices and push down their yields. Since mortgage rates and car loan rates follow Treasury yields, American borrowers benefit. One well known study by Francis and Veronica Warnock estimated that foreign buying lowered the 10-year Treasury yield by close to a percentage point in the mid-2000s.

The flip side for individuals is quieter. If your job was in a factory that competed with imports, the same loop may have cost you that job. More on that below.

### Level two: businesses

Picture a Japanese carmaker selling cars in Ohio. It is paid in dollars. It needs yen to pay its workers at home, so it can sell the dollars. But if every exporter sold dollars at once, the yen would soar and Japanese goods would become expensive. So a lot of those dollars stay as dollars. Some are invested by the company in US factories and assets. Some are swapped with Japanese banks and pension funds, who buy Treasuries. Some are absorbed by Japan's central bank and Ministry of Finance as reserves.

The same pattern holds for a Chinese electronics maker, a German machine builder or a Gulf oil company. The project's files show a modern version: Gulf sovereign funds now recycle their dollars into US artificial intelligence infrastructure as owners, for example a roughly $40 billion deal by MGX and BlackRock for a large data center company.

> When a foreign company sells you a product, it often ends up lending you, the US taxpayer, the money you paid it. Your dollars leave as payment and come back as a loan to the US government. The trade deficit and the foreign holding of US debt are two sides of the same coin.

### Level three: governments

At the top level, foreign governments hold dollars as reserves: a rainy day fund to defend their currency, pay for imports in a crisis and reassure investors. As of August 2026, Japan held about $1,068 billion of US Treasuries, the United Kingdom about $1,010 billion and China about $591 billion, according to US Treasury data gathered by this project. Foreign official accounts held about $2.55 trillion in custody at the New York Fed in mid-October 2026.

For the US government the loop brings two powers. First, it can borrow cheaply and at enormous scale, because the world needs its debt as a safe asset. Second, because most international payments pass through dollar accounts at US banks, the US can cut a country or a company out of the system. In 2022, after Russia invaded Ukraine, Western governments froze roughly $300 billion of Russia's central bank reserves. That showed every government on earth that dollar reserves come with a political condition.

### Why the model works

| Pillar | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Deep markets | The US has about $31.8 trillion of marketable Treasury debt. You can buy or sell billions in minutes without moving the price much. No other safe asset is that large or liquid. |
| Rule of law | Courts enforce contracts and property rights fairly reliably, even for foreigners. Investors fear arbitrary seizure less in the US than in most alternatives. |
| Network effects | Oil, commodities, loans and trade are already priced in dollars, so everyone needs dollars, so more things get priced in dollars. Like a language, the more people use it, the harder it is to switch. |
| Open capital account | Money can enter and leave the US freely. China, the largest rival economy, still restricts this, which limits the yuan as a reserve. |
| Military and alliances | The US security umbrella over allies such as Japan, South Korea, Germany and the Gulf states gives them extra reasons to hold dollars. |

### What the model costs

- Hollowed industry. A strong dollar makes US exports expensive and imports cheap. Economists David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson and coauthors estimated that the surge of Chinese imports in the 2000s cost around a million US manufacturing jobs, and perhaps two million jobs in all. Whole towns felt it.
- Persistent deficits. The US has run trade deficits almost every year since the mid-1970s. Foreign claims on the US keep growing.
- Borrowing becomes easy, maybe too easy. With the world eager to buy its debt, Washington faces little market pressure to balance its budget, until the price of borrowing finally rises, as Chapter 16 shows it now has.
- Dependence on foreign buyers. If foreign governments slow their buying or sell, US rates rise. In 2026 Japan's Treasury holdings fell about $171 billion from February to August as it defended the yen, and long-term US yields rose.

- **Myth:** The trade deficit shows the US is losing.
- **Reality:** Economists disagree about what drives the deficit. One view, associated with Ben Bernanke's 2005 'global saving glut' speech, says foreigners save too much and push their savings into the US, so the deficit is partly caused abroad. Another view, argued by Michael Pettis and others, says surplus countries hold down their own consumption, which forces deficits onto the US. A third view blames low US saving. All three agree the deficit is the mirror image of foreign capital flowing into the US, and that this brings benefits and real costs to different groups of Americans.

> "The dollar is our currency, but your problem." (John Connally, US Treasury Secretary, to European finance ministers, 1971)

Connally said this as the Bretton Woods system cracked. It captures the deal in one line. Because the world holds dollars, American decisions about the dollar land on everyone else. The next chapter shows the moment that line was spoken in earnest.

**Check yourself.** Q: If foreign central banks stopped buying US Treasuries, what would likely happen to US mortgage rates, and why?
A: They would likely rise. Fewer buyers for Treasuries means lower prices and higher yields, and mortgage rates follow Treasury yields.

**Check yourself.** Q: Name one benefit and one cost of the dollar loop for an ordinary American.
A: Benefit: cheaper imported goods and lower loan rates. Cost: jobs lost in industries that compete with imports, and a government that can borrow without much discipline.
