# How money moves: a field guide for investors

Rulebook Level 4 (Money in motion), chapter 01. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-moves.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-moves . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*A pre-owned luxury watch can lose a third of its price and nobody puts a dollar figure on it. A share slips 5% and $236 billion of market value is wiped off the screen. This chapter follows the money behind both: who moves it, what season steers it, and the arithmetic that decides who keeps it.*

**In short**
- A share is re-priced every second, a watch only when one sells. On 10 November 2026 the market value of NVIDIA, a leading maker of AI chips, fell about $236 billion while about $73 billion of its shares changed hands. Fewer than 2 in 100 of its shares traded that day.
- No cash vanished in that fall. Something real was still lost: borrowing power against shares, spending by people who feel poorer, pension values, and the price firms pay for fresh money.
- Money moves in three ways. It is created (bank loans, central bank buying), moved (every trade, wage and buyback) and re-priced (the last trade re-marks everything). Headlines mix the three.
- The movers are named institutions, each with a verb and a size. The Fed sets the price of overnight money. Banks create deposits. Index funds buy by rule, and companies buy back their shares.
- Seasons decide which bucket fills. Read them from six dials, not one headline.
- A growing pot follows its typical rate, not its average. A 50% fall needs a 100% rise. Rebalancing can earn from swings, and borrowing can turn a winner into ruin. Nothing here is advice.

### Part 1 of 4. The watch and the share

Picture two things in a drawer. One is a vintage Rolex your uncle left you. The other is a slip showing ten NVIDIA shares. Both are worth something. Both can lose value. Yet when the share falls, the news can put a dollar figure on it that same evening. When the watch falls, nobody can.

The difference is not value. It is how often each one gets a price, and how many other people are tied to that price.

|  | A vintage watch | A listed share |
|---|---|---|
| How often it gets a price | Only when a similar watch sells, in dealer and auction sales | Every second the market is open. 429 million NVIDIA shares traded on 10 November 2026 |
| Who sees the price | Dealers, auction houses and a few price trackers | Everyone, live, for free |
| Are units identical? | No. Condition and papers differ | Yes. Every share is the same |
| Who must hold it | Nobody | Every fund that tracks an index it belongs to |
| Used to borrow against | Rarely, through a pawn or specialist lender | Every day: Americans owed $1.48 trillion on loans against their shares (September 2026) |

Watches did fall. A widely followed index of pre-owned luxury watch prices, WatchCharts, lost roughly a third from its spring 2022 peak to 2024, by press reports. Higher interest rates cooled demand. In the single year to December 2024 the index fell 5.1%. The index then recovered about 9% in the year to June 2026, as reported. But no index fund was forced to sell, and no headline said the world lost $X billion. Nobody multiplies one sale price by every watch in every drawer. A watch owner who did not sell had a paper loss that no screen showed.

### The day NVIDIA lost $236 billion

1. On Monday 9 November 2026 NVIDIA's shares closed at $178.67. On Tuesday they closed at $168.89, down 5.47%.
2. NVIDIA has about 24.2 billion shares (market value divided by price, not a filing figure). A fall of $9.78 on each one re-marks the company's value down by about $236 billion.
3. How much cash changed hands that day? 429 million shares, at about $170 each: about $73 billion.
4. Every one of those dollars went from a buyer to a seller. None vanished.
5. About 98 in every 100 shares did not trade at all. They were simply re-marked at the new price.

**Chart: NVIDIA: market value change against cash that changed hands, billions of dollars**
| Item | Fall in market value | Cash that changed hands |
|---|---|---|
| 5 Nov 2026 | 448 | 57 |
| 9 Nov 2026 | 193 | 82 |
| 10 Nov 2026 | 236 | 73 |
On 5 November, the day with the biggest fall in value, the value tag moved almost eight times as much as the cash. The tag is a price times all shares. The cash is only what was traded. Source: Closing prices and volumes from stockanalysis.com (fetched 11 November 2026). Cash traded is volume times the day's typical price, a project estimate.

### So was anything lost?

Yes, and this is where many explainers stop too early. Nobody received the $236 billion, because it was never cash. But the holders of the shares that did not trade really can sell for less today. And a price on a screen is wired into other people's lives in ways a watch price is not.

| What is wired to the price | What happens when it falls | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Loans against shares | Lenders lend less. Some borrowers must sell or add cash (a margin call) | US margin debt $1.48 trillion, September 2026 (FINRA) |
| Spending | People who feel poorer spend less | About 3.2 cents less a year for every $1 of share wealth lost (a 2021 study in the American Economic Review) |
| Pensions and national funds | They count holdings at market value every quarter | Norway's oil fund reported a loss of about 278 billion Norwegian kroner (-0.5% for the quarter) in July to September 2026 |
| The company itself | Selling new shares raises less, and staff paid in shares get less | A lower price means new shares raise less money. No figure was found |

> **The real difference.** A watch has these wires too, only thinner. A pawn lender lends against it, and its owner may feel poorer. What turns a price fall into a headline is how visible the price is and how many people are wired to it, not whether value changed.

> **Closer to home.** For a Malaysian reader, the nearest wire is retirement savings. Deposits and savings in the EPF, Malaysia's national retirement fund, make up 68.4% of household financial assets (Bank Negara, Financial Stability Review, 2025). EPF invests part of them in shares, so a worker who never bought a share is still wired to share prices.

### One headline, two causes

The story going round said NVIDIA fell because of news about its chief executive. On Sunday 8 November the board said independent directors, with outside counsel, would investigate reports about the chief executive. The reports said the chief executive had been the target of a "foreign intelligence effort." The board said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets were harmed (board statement, 8 November 2026). It had reached no finding when this chapter was written. This chapter leaves out reports about anyone's private life, because the lesson does not need them. Now line the days up against other large chip makers.

| Day | NVIDIA | Chip fund (SMH, NVIDIA included) | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 Oct | -4.2% | -7.0% | The chip fund fell more than NVIDIA |
| 5 Nov | -9.3% | -11.2% | The chip fund fell further, two days after the midterm elections |
| 9 Nov | -4.3% | +5.1% | NVIDIA alone. Rival chip makers rose: AMD +13.3%, Broadcom +8.8% |
| 10 Nov | -5.5% | -0.8% | NVIDIA fell about 5 points more than the fund that holds it |

The board's statement came on the Sunday before the last two days, when NVIDIA fell about 9.5% from $186.64 to $168.89. That is timing, and timing alone does not prove cause. The biggest single day, 5 November, hit the chip fund harder than NVIDIA, two days after the midterm elections and three days before the board's statement. Related press reports had appeared from 29 October, so even the timing is not clean. From its close of $239.24 on 6 October the share had already fallen 22% by 6 November. If a headline gives one cause for a fall that had at least two, it is telling you a story, not the full record.

> **Where the money went.** On 9 November NVIDIA fell while its rivals jumped. That fits money rotating inside one bucket, from one chip maker to others. But this project did not measure where the cash went, and the whole market rose that day.

**Check yourself.** Q: NVIDIA's market value fell about $236 billion in one day. Who has the $236 billion now?
A: Almost nobody. It was never a pile of cash. Short sellers and buyers of put options gained, but only what the other side of their contracts lost, a small slice of $236 billion. About $73 billion did change hands, from buyers to sellers, and all of it still exists. What fell was the price tag on every share, and with it borrowing power, spending and fund values.

### Part 2 of 4. Created, moved, re-priced: who really moves money

Every money headline uses one of three verbs, often without saying which. Get the verb right and the headline usually makes sense.

| Verb | What happens | Example from 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Created | New money appears: a bank lends, or a bank or the central bank buys from someone who is not a bank | US bank loans grew by about $1 trillion in the year to September 2026, to $14.10 trillion, and that net growth became new deposits |
| Moved | Existing money changes hands: a trade, a wage, a tax, a buyback, a factory order | Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta guided to about $730 billion of capital spending in 2026 (press tallies of company guidance) |
| Re-priced | No money moves. The last trade re-marks everything like it | NVIDIA's $236 billion on 10 November. More than $2 trillion of tech market value wiped out on 5 November (NPR) |

Notice the scale. The technology firms' building plans for one year are about ten times the cash that traded in NVIDIA on 10 November. Once spent, that is real money paid to chip makers, builders and power companies, for things that get made. That flow shapes the economy far more than any one day's price tag.

### The movers, each with a verb

Picture a kitchen full of taps. Banks and the central bank can make new water appear. The Treasury moves water between the floors. Funds, companies and traders pour from one glass into another. When someone says the money is moving, ask whose hand is on which tap, and how far it is turned. Here are the biggest hands as of autumn 2026.

| Who | What they do | How big |
|---|---|---|
| Federal Reserve | Sets the price of overnight money. Creates or retires bank reserves (the banks' own accounts at the Fed) by buying bonds or letting them mature | Policy rate 3.75% to 4.00% after a rise on 16 September 2026. Assets $6.75 trillion |
| US Treasury | Borrows to cover the deficit and keeps its cash at the Fed | Plans to borrow a net $618 billion from private buyers in October to December 2026 |
| Banks | Create deposits when they lend | Loans $14.10 trillion, growing 7.6% a year |
| Index funds | Buy and sell by rule as money flows in or out | Morningstar reports they have held more than active funds in US funds since the end of 2023 |
| The three largest fund managers | Gather savings into funds and vote the shares | BlackRock alone reported $15.49 trillion under management at the end of September 2026 |
| Companies | Buy back their own shares with cash | About $1.1 trillion by S&P 500 firms in the year to June 2026, a reported record |
| Market makers | Quote buy and sell prices and hold shares briefly | One firm, Citadel Securities, says it handles about 35% of US retail trading |
| Borrowers on margin | Borrow against shares to buy more | $1.48 trillion, September 2026 |
| Foreign holders of US debt | Lend to the US government | About $9.25 trillion in July 2026 data (press tally of Treasury figures), Japan the largest at about $1.10 trillion |

> **Industry blocs are many firms, not one mind.** People talk about the financial industry, the technology industry and the defence industry as if each were one mind. Each is a group of named firms chasing their own profit. Finance: the three largest fund managers run roughly $33.5 trillion or more between them (a press tally using figures from different dates). Technology: four firms are spending about $730 billion on data centres this year. Defence: the US request for 2027 is $1.5 trillion, $350 billion of it hoped for from a separate bill (not passed as far as this project found). When they push the same way, much of it is shared incentive, the focal point game from Level 2 chapter 4. Some of it is open and organised: trade groups, lobbying that the law makes them record, and contracts with government. Ask of any claim: who, doing what, how much.

### The levers: how policy moves money between buckets

| Lever | How it moves money | A documented episode |
|---|---|---|
| Raising interest rates | Cash and bills pay more. Distant profits and long bonds are worth less today | 2022: US shares -18.0%, 10-year government bonds -17.8%, Treasury bills +2.1% (Damodaran, NYU) |
| Central bank buying (QE) | The central bank takes bonds out of private hands. Sellers who are not banks get new deposits and look for something else to hold | 2020: the Fed bought bonds on a vast scale from March while the government sent stimulus checks. US shares ended the year up 18.0%, counting dividends (the cash firms pay out). One year cannot separate the causes |
| Central bank shrinking (QT) | Bonds mature without replacement, so private buyers must absorb more | Ran from 1 June 2022 until 1 December 2025 |
| Government deficits | Spending puts deposits in private hands, borrowing adds bonds others must buy | $618 billion of planned net borrowing in October to December 2026, which also refills the Treasury's cash account, so it is not the same as the deficit |
| Proposed laws | Expected profits change before any law passes | 5 November 2026: chip shares fell 11.2% two days after the midterm elections. A bill to pause new data centres had been put forward in March 2026. Any link is reported, not proven |

**Check yourself.** Q: A company spends $10 billion buying back its own shares. Is money created, moved or re-priced?
A: Moved. The company's cash goes to the shareholders who sold. The price of the remaining shares may then rise, which is re-pricing, but no new money is created.

### Part 3 of 4. Seasons, read from six dials

Level 1 chapter 8 said money behaves like ice. It melts in a warm season and keeps its shape in a cold one. No single gauge tells you which season you are in. Six dials, read together, come close. As a rough rule, rising inflation and fast credit growth point to a warm season, and falling prices and shrinking credit point to a cold one.

| Dial | Reading | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Real interest rate: what a saver earns above inflation (read from a 10-year US government bond whose payments rise with inflation) | 2.78%, 10 November 2026 | High. Cash and bonds pay well above inflation. A hard season for gold and for shares priced on distant profits |
| Inflation trend | 3.7% in the year to September 2026, up from 3.4% | Rising, with gasoline over half of September's monthly rise (press reports of the US Bureau of Labor Statistics release) |
| Extra pay for risky borrowers (high-yield spread): the extra interest weaker firms pay above the government | 3.14 points, 28 October 2026, up from 2.63 in late August | Still small, but growing, so lenders are a little more nervous |
| Long rates minus short rates (yield curve) | +0.25 points (10-year 4.98%, 2-year 4.73%) | Positive. Lenders are still paid more for lending longer, which is normal. When short rates rise above long rates, many investors treat it as a warning sign |
| The dollar against other currencies | About 101.7, 21 October 2026 | Middling. One reading, not a trend |
| Money and credit growth | Money supply +5.7%, bank loans +7.6% in a year | Banks are lending faster than prices are rising |

> **One reading.** This project reads the dials as a warm season with high real rates: inflation rising, but rates well above it. One past period with high real rates is 1980 to 1982. The match is loose. Inflation then was 8.4% and falling, while today it is 3.7% and rising. Today's mix points both ways, so this is a reading to argue with, not a forecast.

| Season | Inflation a year | Shares | Cash (bills) | Government bonds | Gold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1971 to 1979, the great inflation | 7.6% | -1.3% | -1.2% | -3.1% | +24.3% |
| 1980 to 1982, high real rates | 8.4% | +5.9% | +3.5% | +3.1% | -11.0% |
| 1983 to 2007, the long calm | 3.1% | +9.2% | +2.0% | +5.3% | -0.7% |
| 2008, the crash year | 0.1% | -36.6% | +1.3% | +20.0% | +4.2% |
| 2022, the rate shock | 6.5% | -23.0% | -4.1% | -22.8% | -5.5% |
| Whole period, 1970 to 2024 | 3.9% | +6.7% | +0.5% | +2.1% | +3.7% |

Returns are a year, after inflation, compounded, from Aswath Damodaran's published US data (NYU Stern) and US consumer prices. No bucket wins every season. In 2008 cash beat shares, but long government bonds (+20.0% after inflation) and gold (+4.2%) beat cash. Over the 55 years from 1970 to 2024 cash beat inflation by just 0.5% a year. Gold won the 1970s and lost the early 1980s. Shares won the long run and lost more than a third of their value in a single year.

### Who buys after the crash

Every share sold in a crash has a buyer. On 9 and 10 November, buyers paid about $155 billion for NVIDIA shares (an estimate from volume times typical price). So the useful question is not whether someone buys, but who still has cash and cheap credit when others are forced to sell.

> **Musical chairs, played in a market.** This is the musical chairs of Level 1 chapter 7, played in a market. In quiet years debts are paid slowly from a flow of income. In a panic everyone wants cash on the same day, and the chairs run out. An investor who borrows against shares can drift the way Minsky described: calm years tempt bigger loans, and a fall forces a sale. In March 2020, as the pandemic hit, the Fed bought US government bonds on a vast scale. Its holdings rose from about $2.52 trillion on 11 March to about $3.63 trillion on 8 April (Fed weekly balance sheet). That is a central bank adding chairs so the music can keep playing.

After 2008 large investors bought foreclosed homes to rent out. By June 2022, 32 investors with more than 1,000 homes each owned nearly 450,000 US single-family homes, by one estimate cited by the US Government Accountability Office. That was about 3% of all single-family rentals.

- **Myth:** Crashes are planned so that the rich can buy everything cheaply.
- **Reality:** Those with spare cash and credit do buy cheaply after a crash, and some episodes are documented, such as large investors buying foreclosed homes after 2008. Planning the crash is a different claim, and it needs its own evidence: who acted, how, and what records that would leave behind. One part is documented. Rescues after a crash, such as rate cuts and central bank buying, lift asset prices first. That helps people who already own assets (the Cantillon effect from Level 1). It is not proof of a plan.

### Part 4 of 4. The arithmetic of a growing pot

Thomas Cover, an information theorist, asked a simple question in a recorded lecture on growth-optimal and universal portfolios. If you reinvest everything each day, what makes your money grow fastest over time? The answer fits on one page, and once you see it you will read every return figure differently.

Start with a game. You can hold cash, which stays at $1. Or you can hold a stock that, each period, either doubles or halves, with equal odds. On average the stock looks great: half the time +100%, half the time -50%, an average of +25%. But a double and a halving cancel out. Over many periods the stock typically goes nowhere. Typical here means the result in the middle of many plays, not the plain average.

| What you hold | Each period your pot becomes | Typical growth a period |
|---|---|---|
| Cash only | 1 times | 0% |
| The stock only | 2 times or 0.5 times | 0%, even though the average looks like +25% |
| Half in each, rebalanced back to half every period | 1.5 times or 0.75 times | +6.07% |

Where the 1.5 and 0.75 come from: start with $1, half in cash and half in the stock. If the stock doubles, the cash half stays $0.50 and the stock half becomes $1, so the pot is $1.50. If it halves, $0.50 plus $0.25 makes $0.75. One of each multiplies to 1.5 times 0.75, which is 1.125 over two periods, so the typical period grows by the square root of 1.125, about 1.06, or 6%. Two things that each go nowhere make about 6% a period together, in this made-up game. Real markets can pay far less for rebalancing, or nothing, as the box below shows. Rebalancing sells some of the stock after it doubles and buys more after it halves, so it earns from the swings themselves. In a computer run of 100,000 players over 20 periods, the rebalanced pot beat the stock alone three times in four. Yet one player in four still ended below where they started. A typical rate is not a promise to any one person.

Now go back to the drawer. Say the ten shares are worth RM1,000 and fall by half, to RM500. A 50% rise takes them back to just RM750. To get home they need 100%. Falls hurt more than rises help, because growth compounds from wherever you now stand.

| If your pot falls by | It must then rise by this to get back |
|---|---|
| 10% | 11% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 30% | 43% |
| 50% | 100% |
| 80% | 400% |

| Share of your pot in the coin-flip stock | Typical growth a period |
|---|---|
| 50% | +6.1% |
| 100% | 0% |
| 150%, borrowing the extra at no interest | -20.9% |
| 200%, borrowing the extra | Ruin on the first halving |

At 150%, a double turns each $1 into $2.50 and a halving into $0.25. One of each multiplies to 2.5 times 0.25, which is 0.625 over two periods. The square root of 0.625 is about 0.79, a loss of about 21% a period. Borrowing to hold more of a good bet can turn it into a losing one. Past the best mix, every extra borrowed dollar lowers the typical growth, and enough of them guarantee ruin.

> **The catches.** The 6% needs a stock whose ups and downs cancel out. Real shares are not coin flips. Suppose the stock rises 60% or falls 20% with equal odds. Then holding it alone grows 13.1% a period, and half-and-half rebalancing only 8.2%. Rebalancing loses when one asset keeps climbing. Costs bite too: trading 1% of each dollar moved cuts the 6.07% to 5.89%, and taxes can fall due every year. Cover's best mix is only known in hindsight. His method that knows nothing in advance comes close to it only over very long periods.

Cover added one more idea that is worth carrying everywhere. In Cover's theory, extra information, a tip or a forecast, can raise your growth rate only by as much as it actually tells you about what happens next. A tip that is right no more often than chance tells you nothing, so it adds nothing to your growth, however confident it sounds. Paying for it lowers your growth.

### Five questions before you believe a money headline

1. Created, moved or re-priced? Which verb is the headline really using?
2. How much cash actually changed hands, against the number in the headline?
3. Who is wired to this price: lenders, funds, pensions, the company itself?
4. Which season are we in, and does this move fit it?
5. Who acted, doing what, how much? Was a cause named only because it came first?

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: If the watch market had a listed index fund that people borrowed against, would a 30% fall become news?
  A: Very likely. The fall would force margin calls and fund sales, and its price would be live on every screen. The value lost would be the same. The wiring would be new. (builds on [When markets fall: buckets, collateral and rotation](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-buckets.md))
- Q: On 9 November NVIDIA fell 4% while chip shares rose 5%. Where did the money go?
  A: Possibly in part to other chip makers. AMD rose 13.3% and Broadcom 8.8%, and the whole market rose that day. This project did not measure the flows, so rotation inside the bucket is a likely reading, not a measured fact. (builds on [When markets fall: buckets, collateral and rotation](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-buckets.md))
- Q: Why might a central bank raise rates when shares are already falling?
  A: Because its job is prices and jobs, not share prices. In 2022 the Fed kept raising rates while shares fell 18%, because inflation was far above its target. (builds on [The seasons of money](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-08.md))
- Q: Level 1 chapter 7 showed that interest can be paid from a circulating flow, as long as the lender spends it back. What happens to that circulation when a crash makes everyone hoard cash?
  A: It slows, just as the musical chairs chapter warned. Debts fall due while money sits still, which is why central banks lend freely in a panic. (builds on [The musical chairs question](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-07.md))
- Q: If rebalancing earns from swings, why does nobody get rich from it easily?
  A: Because real assets trend, costs and taxes eat the gain, and the best mix is only known afterwards. The edge is real but small, and it comes from discipline, not prediction. (builds on [The seasons of money](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-08.md))

### Go deeper: Cover's mathematics, for the curious

The growth rate of a pot that is reinvested each period is the average of the logarithm of each period's gain. That is why the typical rate, the geometric mean, governs compounding, while the plain average does not. Maximizing it is the rule John Kelly set out in 1956 for repeated bets with an information edge and Cover generalized to portfolios.

For the coin-flip stock, the best fixed mix is half stock and half cash, with growth equal to the square root of 1.5 times 0.75, about 1.0607 a period. Cover's universal portfolio, published in Mathematical Finance in 1991, spreads money across every fixed mix and lets the winners grow. For two assets, a refined version of the method has a proven worst-case bound of about 1 divided by twice the square root of the number of periods of what the best fixed mix chosen in hindsight would have made (this project has not checked the exact form against the papers): about 11% of it after 20 periods and under 1% after 20 years of trading days. Its growth rate catches up, but slowly.

Sources: J. L. Kelly, A New Interpretation of Information Rate, Bell System Technical Journal, 1956. T. M. Cover, Universal Portfolios, Mathematical Finance, 1991.
