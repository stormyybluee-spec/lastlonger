# The musical chairs question

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 07. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-07.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-07 . Text built 2026-11-11. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*If all money is borrowed and interest is owed on top, can everyone ever pay? The honest answer is yes in a quiet world, and not always in a real one.*

Chapter 6 showed that money is born as debt. That raises a question almost everyone hits sooner or later, and the owner of this project put it in a sharp form.

> "Imagine I give a person my own currency YYY$10, but I tell them they must pay me back 5% interest, but no matter how much they work they can't repay if there is only YYY$10 in circulation." (The owner of this project, in their own words)

It feels like musical chairs. Ten chairs, ten and a half people. Someone must always be left standing, or someone must always borrow more to keep the music playing. Let us test the intuition carefully, because it is half right, and the half that is right matters a great deal.

*[Picture on the web page: The worry: YYY$10 in circulation, YYY$10.50 owed. If the stock of money is all there is, someone must default or borrow more.]*

### Why it feels impossible

The worry treats money as a fixed pile, a stock. You hand out 10. You ask for 10.50 back. There are only 10 in existence. Arithmetic seems to say no.

The missing piece is time. Money is not used once. It moves. The same coin can pay many debts in a year, because each person who receives it spends it again. Economists call how often money changes hands its velocity. Debts are paid from flows over time, not from the stock all at once.

### The real mechanism: the lender spends

> **Picture this.** You are the lender on a small island. You lend YYY$10 to Bea, a fisher, at 5% a year. Each year Bea owes you YYY$0.50 of interest. Where does she get it? From you. You need to eat, so you buy YYY$0.50 of fish from Bea each year. She takes that YYY$0.50 and pays you the interest. Next year, the same thing. The money supply never changes. It stays at YYY$10. Bea pays her interest forever. Nobody is left standing.

| Year | Money in circulation | Bea's loan | Interest Bea pays you | You spend back on fish | Anyone short? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | YYY$10 | YYY$10 | YYY$0.50 | YYY$0.50 | No |
| 2 | YYY$10 | YYY$10 | YYY$0.50 | YYY$0.50 | No |
| 10 | YYY$10 | YYY$10 | YYY$0.50 | YYY$0.50 | No |
| Total over 10 years | Still YYY$10 | Still YYY$10 | YYY$5.00 paid | YYY$5.00 spent | No |

Interest is a payment for a service, the use of money over time. As long as the person who receives interest spends it back into the economy, on goods, wages, rent or taxes, the money circulates and can be earned again. Even repaying the principal is possible. Bea can sell you more fish over time and pay down the YYY$10. When she finishes, the money she borrowed is gone, because the loan that created it is gone. That is not a failure. It is the loop from Chapter 6 running in reverse.

Economists who modeled this carefully, including critics of mainstream economics such as Steve Keen, have shown that a pure credit economy can pay interest in a steady state without needing endless new loans. So the strict version of the musical chairs claim, that interest can never be paid without more debt, is not correct.

- **Myth:** Because banks only create the principal and never the interest, the system needs ever more debt just to pay interest, or it collapses.
- **Reality:** Interest is paid out of the circulating flow of money, and banks spend their interest income on salaries, rent, dividends and taxes, which puts it back into circulation. In a steady state, interest can be paid indefinitely. The real dangers come from somewhere else, and they are listed below.

### Where the intuition is right

Now the important part. The owner's instinct points at real weaknesses. They are just different from the arithmetic one.

- Savers who do not spend. If the people receiving interest save it instead of spending it, money piles up with them and drains from everyone else. If savers as a group want their savings to keep growing, someone else must keep going deeper into debt. This is an accounting fact, made famous by the economist Wynne Godley: for every saver accumulating financial assets, some sector, households, businesses, government or foreigners, must be adding debt.
- Debt can outgrow income. Paying interest is possible. Paying more and more interest on debts that grow faster than incomes is not sustainable. Global debt is about $365 trillion, roughly 310% of world output, according to the Institute of International Finance in 2026.
- Repayment destroys money. If many borrowers try to pay down debt at once, deposits vanish, spending falls, prices and incomes drop, and the remaining debts get harder to pay.
- Panics need cash now, not over time. In a crisis everyone wants to settle at once. Then the stock of money really is too small for the pile of claims, unless a central bank steps in.

> "The more the debtors pay, the more they owe." (Irving Fisher, The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions, 1933)

Fisher saw this in the Great Depression. When everyone sells assets and cuts spending to repay debt, prices fall. Falling prices make each remaining dollar of debt heavier. Paying down debt can make debt burdens worse.

### Minsky: stability breeds instability

The economist Hyman Minsky described three kinds of borrowers. Hedge borrowers can pay both interest and principal from income. Speculative borrowers can pay interest from income but must keep rolling over the principal. Ponzi borrowers cannot even pay the interest from income, and must borrow more or sell assets that they hope keep rising.

Minsky's key idea is that long calm periods push people from the first kind toward the third. Good times make lenders and borrowers confident, so they take on more debt against rising asset prices. Then a small shock, like a dip in house prices, reveals that many borrowers were Ponzi borrowers all along. The 2008 crisis is often called a Minsky moment, a phrase coined by economist Paul McCulley.

*[Picture on the web page: The debt treadmill: when debt grows faster than income, the interest bill rises, so more borrowing is needed just to keep pace. Calm years tempt more people onto the faster belt.]*

### Why credit growth became structural after 1971

Under gold, a bank boom eventually ran into a gold shortage and stopped. After 1971 that brake was gone. Each time a slump threatened, central banks could cut rates and governments could borrow, so recessions were shorter but debt rarely got cleared out. Over decades, debt grew faster than incomes. The system became like a bicycle that is stable only while it keeps moving forward.

The US government is the clearest current case. According to Treasury data gathered by this project, the government spent about $7.40 trillion in fiscal year 2026 and collected about $5.40 trillion, a deficit of about $1.99 trillion. Even before interest, it ran a primary deficit of about $941 billion, close to 2.9% of the economy. Interest itself cost about $1.05 trillion. The average rate on its marketable debt is about 3.5%, but new borrowing costs closer to 4.9%, and about 36% of the debt reprices within a year. Unless deficits shrink or rates fall, the interest bill climbs on its own.

> The owner's notes say there is only about $15,000 of liquid money per person on earth against about $346 trillion of global debt, and conclude that we live in a world of scarce settlement capacity, not scarce money. The figures need updating: world broad money is now about $19,500 per person, and global debt is about $365 trillion. But the insight is sharp. Normally debts are paid slowly from flows, so a small stock of money services a huge stock of debt. In a panic, everyone wants settlement at once, and the stock is far too small. That is exactly why central banks act as lenders of last resort in a crisis.

**Check yourself.** Q: In the island story, what single behavior would turn the YYY$10 economy into a real musical chairs game?
A: If you, the lender, stopped spending your interest and hoarded it. Then money would drain out of circulation into your savings, and Bea would eventually be unable to pay without borrowing more or defaulting.

**Check yourself.** Q: Is it true that interest can never be paid without new loans?
A: No. In a steady state, interest can be paid indefinitely as long as interest income is spent back into circulation. The real risks are debt growing faster than income, savers hoarding, mass repayment shrinking the money supply, and panics.
