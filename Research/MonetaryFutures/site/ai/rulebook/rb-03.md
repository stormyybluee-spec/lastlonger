# War, Bretton Woods and the dollar's crown

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 03. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-03.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-03 . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Two world wars moved the world's gold to America, and in 1944 the dollar became the hub of every other currency.*

The gold standard of Chapter 2 worked best in peacetime. War is the most expensive thing a nation ever does, and governments at war will always choose victory over a fixed gold price. This chapter follows how the wars broke the old system and how a new one was designed in a New Hampshire hotel.

### The wars break the rules

When the First World War began in 1914, the warring countries suspended gold convertibility. They printed money and borrowed heavily to pay for the fighting. Between 1914 and 1920 prices roughly doubled in the United States, rose even more in Britain, and rose far more again in Germany.

After the war, countries tried to rebuild the gold standard. Britain returned to gold in 1925 at its old prewar rate of about $4.86, a decision made by Winston Churchill as Chancellor. The pound was now overvalued, so British exports were too expensive, and the country suffered high unemployment. John Maynard Keynes attacked the decision in a pamphlet called The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill.

> "In truth, the gold standard is already a barbarous relic." (John Maynard Keynes, A Tract on Monetary Reform, 1923)

When the Great Depression struck after 1929, the gold standard spread the damage. Countries losing gold had to raise interest rates and shrink their money supply in the middle of a collapse. Britain left gold in 1931. The United States effectively left domestic gold in 1933, when President Roosevelt ordered citizens to hand in most gold coins and bullion, then raised the official price from $20.67 to $35 an ounce in 1934. Research by economists Barry Eichengreen and Jeffrey Sachs found that countries that left gold earlier generally recovered sooner.

### Why America ended up with the gold

Through the 1930s and the Second World War, gold poured into the United States. Europeans sent gold to safety. Allied countries paid for American weapons, food and fuel. By the late 1940s the US government held roughly two thirds of all the gold owned by the world's governments. America also had the only large industrial economy that had not been bombed.

> **Picture this.** Imagine a long poker night where every player keeps losing chips to one player who also owns the casino's only working kitchen. By dawn that player holds most of the chips. When the others want to start a new game, they have little choice but to use that player's chips as the house standard. That is roughly the position of the United States in 1944.

### Bretton Woods, July 1944

In July 1944, delegates from 44 Allied nations met at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. Two men shaped the talks. Keynes, for Britain, proposed a new international clearing unit called bancor, which would push both deficit and surplus countries to adjust. Harry Dexter White, for the US Treasury, proposed a system built around the dollar. The country with the gold won the argument.

- The US dollar was fixed to gold at $35 an ounce. Foreign central banks, not private citizens, could swap dollars for gold at that price.
- Other currencies were pegged to the dollar at fixed rates, allowed to move only about 1% either way. Pegs could be changed only for a serious imbalance.
- The International Monetary Fund (IMF) was created to lend to countries with short-term payment problems, so they would not need to devalue or crash their economies.
- The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, now part of the World Bank, was created to finance rebuilding and development.

*[Picture on the web page: Bretton Woods, 1944: other currencies pegged to the dollar, and the dollar pegged to gold at $35 an ounce. The dollar became the hub, gold the anchor.]*

> This made the dollar the hub of a wheel. To defend its peg, a country needed a stock of dollars to buy its own currency when it fell. So central banks around the world began to hold dollars, and soon US Treasury bills, as their main reserves. The reserve role of the dollar, which still shapes your mortgage rate today, was born in this design.

### A system that worked, then strained

For about two decades the system delivered. Europe and Japan rebuilt quickly. The United States gave about $13 billion in Marshall Plan aid from 1948 to 1952, which put dollars into European hands. Trade grew fast, and inflation was mostly moderate. Many economists call the 1950s and 1960s a golden age of growth.

But the system had a crack in its foundation, spotted early by the Belgian economist Robert Triffin. He laid it out in testimony to Congress in 1959 and in a book in 1960.

### The Triffin dilemma

The world needed more and more dollars as trade grew. The only way foreigners could get more dollars was for the United States to send more dollars abroad than it took back, by buying imports, investing overseas, or spending on military bases and aid. Year after year, that piled up dollars in foreign hands.

But each of those dollars was a claim on American gold at $35. As foreign dollar holdings grew, they eventually exceeded the gold the US held. By the early 1960s that line had been crossed. If everyone asked for gold at once, the US could not pay.

> **Picture this.** A popular bakery issues tickets, each good for one loaf. Tickets are so trusted that the whole town uses them as money. The town grows and wants more tickets. The bakery keeps printing them, but it cannot bake much more bread. Every new ticket makes the town happier today and the bakery's promise weaker tomorrow. If the bakery stops printing, the town's trade seizes up. If it keeps printing, one day a crowd shows up wanting bread for every ticket. That is the Triffin dilemma.

The dilemma is this. If the US stops supplying dollars, the world economy runs short of money. If it keeps supplying them, confidence in the gold link erodes. Either way, the system is on a timer.

Governments tried patches. In 1961 the US and European central banks formed the London Gold Pool to hold the market price of gold at $35. France, under President Charles de Gaulle, began swapping its dollars for gold and publicly called for a return to a pure gold standard in 1965. The French finance minister Valery Giscard d'Estaing complained of America's exorbitant privilege: the ability to pay for things with its own currency that others must earn. The Gold Pool collapsed in 1968. The countdown to 1971, the subject of Chapter 5, had begun.

- **Myth:** The US took the world's gold by force or trickery.
- **Reality:** Most of it arrived through ordinary channels: European capital fleeing war and instability in the 1930s, and Allied payments for American goods during the war. The result gave the US enormous leverage at Bretton Woods, but it was mostly the product of the US being the safe, unbombed workshop of the world, not a heist.

> The United States still holds about 261.5 million ounces of gold, about 8,134 tonnes, according to the Treasury's own records for September 2026. It books that gold at $42.22 an ounce, a price set by law in 1973, so the official value is about $11 billion. At market prices it is worth about $1.08 trillion. The gap is a fossil of the Bretton Woods era still sitting in today's government accounts.

**Check yourself.** Q: Under Bretton Woods, could an American citizen walk into a bank and swap dollars for gold?
A: No. Only foreign central banks and governments could convert dollars to gold at $35. American citizens were not even allowed to own most gold bullion from 1933 until 1975.

**Check yourself.** Q: State the Triffin dilemma in two sentences.
A: The world needed a growing supply of dollars, which the US could only provide by running deficits with the rest of the world. But the more dollars piled up abroad, the less believable the promise to convert them into gold became.
