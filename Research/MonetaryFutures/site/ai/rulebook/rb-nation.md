### Is tax a scam?

A popular argument says tax is theft, or a scam that takes from workers and hands to elites. Go back to the board game from chapter 14. The game has been running for thousands of years, and it only works because someone keeps the board, issues the money, records who owns which square and stops players from simply seizing each other's property. That someone is the state, and the subscription fee is tax. Without it there is no common money, no court to defend your title deed, no land registry, no police and no army. You would be left trading by barter, and anyone stronger could take your land.

Tax also gives the money its value. Adam Smith noticed in 1776 that a ruler who demands taxes in a certain paper money gives that paper value. The German economist Georg Friedrich Knapp built a whole theory on the idea in 1905. Every American must pay federal tax in dollars, so every American needs dollars. That steady demand is one reason the dollar is accepted everywhere, long before anyone thinks about gold or oil.

The critics are not wrong about everything. Who pays matters. In the US, taxes on income and payroll carry about 86% of federal receipts, while gains on assets are taxed only when sold and can escape tax when passed on at death. Inflation is a tax nobody votes on. Spending can be wasteful or captured by insiders. So the honest verdict is: tax is not a scam. It is a fee. The real fights are over how big the fee is, who pays it, and whether the company spends it well.

- **Myth:** Tax is a scam. Without the state I would keep everything I earn.
- **Reality:** Without the state there is no money to earn in, no court to enforce your pay and no title to your home. The fair question is not whether to pay the fee, but whether it is set and spent fairly.

### Why the holy books forbade interest

The Torah forbids lending at interest to a fellow member of the community (Exodus 22:25, Leviticus 25:36 to 37), while Deuteronomy 23:19 to 20 allows it with a foreigner. The Quran forbids riba and warns those who take it (Al-Baqarah 2:275 to 279). Medieval Christianity banned usury for centuries as well. Three traditions, far apart, reached the same rule. Why?

Because in an ancient farming world, interest on debt could grow faster than any harvest. A bad year forced a family to borrow, the interest compounded, and soon the family handed over its land, then its labor, sometimes its children. Debt turned into permanent ownership by the lender. The same book of Leviticus prescribed a Jubilee every fiftieth year, when land returned to its original families. Mesopotamian kings regularly declared clean slates that cancelled farmers' debts. These were resets built into the rules, so the board could never become permanently titled to a few. It is the same pressure chapter 19 describes today.

> **Picture this.** Imagine Monopoly with one extra rule: every fifty laps, every street returns to the family that first held it, and all loans are torn up. Nobody can win forever, and nobody is a tenant forever. That was the Jubilee.

Islamic finance today, estimated at about $5 trillion to $6.5 trillion of assets in 2025 depending on who counts, replaces interest with sharing and trading. Instead of lending at interest, a bank can share the profits and losses of a venture, buy a house and sell it to you at a fixed markup paid in installments, or lease it to you. Islamic bonds, called sukuk, pay returns tied to real assets.

> Two catches. First, many products end up paying something very close to interest in a different wrapper. In November 2007 the leading scholar Muhammad Taqi Usmani said that about 85% of sukuk did not comply with Islamic law because of buyback promises that guaranteed the money back, and the standards body AAOIFI tightened its rules in 2008. Second, the Gulf currencies are pegged to the dollar, so their interest rates follow the Federal Reserve. Interest-free finance still lives inside a dollar system that runs on interest.

### The UAE: no income tax. Cheat, or strategy?

The UAE charges no personal income tax. It added a 5% VAT in 2018, a 9% corporate tax from June 2023 on profits above AED 375,000, and a 15% minimum tax on large multinationals from 2025. Much of the state's income comes from oil and gas, state companies and fees, and its sovereign funds are among the largest in the world. Its dirham is pegged to the dollar.

The result is a magnet. Henley and Partners estimated that about 9,800 millionaires would move to the UAE in 2025, more than to any other country. In company terms, the UAE hires experienced staff whose schooling, health care and early careers were paid for by other countries' taxpayers, at the moment those people earn the most. The home country trained them. The hub collects their best years and their capital.

> **The decode verdict.** Cheating: no. It is legal and openly announced. A deliberate strategy to attract wealth built elsewhere: D1, decoded-strong, about 85%. Lucrative: yes, as long as oil income funds the state, the dollar peg holds and the region stays safe. Singapore, Switzerland, Monaco, Ireland for companies, and others play versions of the same game. The OECD's 15% global minimum tax, agreed by more than 130 countries in 2021, was the big countries' attempt to limit it.

> The leaderboard leader plays by different rules. The United States is the only major country that taxes its citizens' full income wherever they live. Eritrea, the other usual example, charges its citizens abroad a flat 2%. An American who moves to Dubai still files a US tax return, and leaving for good means giving up citizenship and possibly paying an exit tax. Since 2010 a law called FATCA has made banks around the world report Americans' accounts. Only the company that owns the world's main currency and its payment pipes can keep its staff on the payroll after they leave.

### Why this usually takes years to see

Taxes, interest, pegs, hubs, ratings and reserves are usually taught in different courses, each with its own jargon and messy charts, and rarely with the history and geopolitics that explain why they exist. That is why understanding money usually takes years of relooping. Read the countries as companies on one leaderboard, and the pieces snap into one picture: who issues the money, who pays the fee, who trains the talent, who collects it, and who sets the rules.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why does a country that charges no income tax still need a state, a currency and courts?
A: Because the board still needs a keeper. The UAE funds its state mainly from oil, state companies and fees instead of income tax, but it still issues the dirham, runs courts and registries and provides security. The fee is collected another way.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why did ancient rules ban interest or cancel debts?
A: Because compounding interest in a farming world turned bad harvests into lost land and lost freedom. Bans and jubilees stopped debt from turning into permanent ownership by lenders.
