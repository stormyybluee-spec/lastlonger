# The country leaderboard: deep markets and the game nations play

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 11. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-leader.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-leader . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Why surplus nations park their savings in America, why climbing the leaderboard is so hard, and what it really took for each number one to get there and to fall.*

Imagine every country on a leaderboard ranked by how much the world trusts its money. Near the top is a strange fact: some of the hardest working, highest saving nations on earth send a large share of their savings to the United States, a country that saves little and borrows a lot. To understand why, you need three ideas: deep markets, governance incentives and game theory.

### What makes a market deep

A deep market is one where you can put in or take out enormous sums, quickly, at a fair price, and trust that the rules will not change on you. Few markets qualify. The US Treasury market is the deepest. Its qualities read like a checklist.

*[Picture on the web page: The qualities that make savers worldwide choose one market over their own.]*

- Size: tens of trillions of dollars of bonds, so even a central bank can buy or sell billions without moving the price much.
- Liquidity: buyers and sellers are always there, every hour, through dealers, futures and repo.
- Rule of law: independent courts, contracts that hold, and property that is not taken on a whim.
- Open doors: money can leave whenever the owner wants. No exit queue, no permission slip.
- A trusted central bank and a currency others already use for trade, debt and oil.
- Hedging tools: futures, swaps and options let holders protect themselves.
- Security: the military and alliances that protect trade routes and the system itself.

> Notice the trade-off hidden in that list. The US gives up some control (it lets money in and out freely and lets courts bind the government) and in return earns the world's trust. Countries that want to keep tight control of their money cannot offer the same deal. That single trade-off explains much of the leaderboard.

### Why surplus countries send their savings to America

When an exporter in Japan, China, Korea, Taiwan or the Gulf sells to the world, it earns dollars. If all those dollars were converted into local currency, the local currency would rise, exports would get dearer and factories would lose orders. So, often through the central bank, the dollars are kept as dollars and parked in the safest dollar asset available: Treasuries.

1. Stay competitive: holding the dollars instead of selling them keeps the home currency cheap and exports flowing.
2. Buy insurance: after the 1997 Asian crisis, countries that ran short of dollars were forced into painful IMF programs. Many vowed never again and built large dollar reserves.
3. No deep home alternative: many local bond markets are too small, too controlled or too easy to freeze to hold trillions.
4. Protect the wealthy: elites in less secure systems prefer assets where courts protect property, so private money follows the official money abroad.
5. Oil and commodities: these are priced in dollars, so exporters earn dollars and often recycle them through sovereign wealth funds.

### How that trickles down into the home economy

Parking savings abroad is a governance choice, and ordinary citizens pay part of the bill. A deliberately cheap currency makes imports, fuel and foreign goods dearer for households. Savings that could have financed homes, wages or local businesses instead finance American borrowing. Exporters and the state gain, households consume less than they produce. On the other side, Americans enjoy cheap imports and cheap credit while factory towns hollow out, as chapter 4 showed. Each side's elite chose the arrangement, and each side's workers live with its costs.

> **Picture this.** Two neighbors. One works long hours, sells most of what it makes to the other, and lends the payment back so the other can keep buying. The lender's family lives modestly. The borrower's family lives well but its workshop goes quiet. Neither can easily stop: if the lender stops lending, its buyer disappears. If the borrower stops buying, the lender's factory stalls. That is the quiet bargain behind the dollar.

Economist Larry Summers called this mutual dependence a 'balance of financial terror' in 2004. Each side holds the other hostage, which is why sudden moves are rare and why the game is played with signals, slow shifts and patience.

### The game theory of the leaderboard

*[Picture on the web page: A currency war as a prisoner's dilemma. Each country is tempted to cheat, and if both do, both lose.]*

- A coordination game: everyone uses the dollar because everyone else does. Switching only pays if many switch together, and the first to switch pays the most. That is why the leader keeps its place long after its advantages fade.
- A prisoner's dilemma: each country gains by weakening its currency or adding tariffs, but if all do it, trade shrinks and all lose. The 1930s round of competitive devaluations and tariffs is the classic case.
- A repeated game: nations meet again and again, so reputation matters. A country that keeps its promises can borrow cheaply for centuries. One that breaks them pays for decades.
- A war of exhaustion: rivals drain each other through arms races, sanctions and tariff wars. History suggests leaders more often fall from overstretch and debt than from being overtaken in a single contest. Historian Paul Kennedy called this imperial overstretch.
- The elite trap: a country rises on cheap labor and heavy investment, but climbing higher requires giving up control: floating the currency, opening the capital account, independent courts. The elites who profit from control often block those steps. Economists Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson describe this as extractive institutions resisting inclusive ones. It is one reason so many countries stall in the middle of the leaderboard.

- **Myth:** The country with the biggest economy automatically gets the top currency.
- **Reality:** Currency leadership lags economic leadership by decades. The US economy passed Britain's in the 1870s, yet sterling remained a leading reserve currency into the 1940s and beyond. Trust, habit and deep markets take generations to move.

### How the number ones rose, and how they fell

| Leader | Its edge | How it slipped | Debt record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rome | Roads, legions, control of the Mediterranean trade lake | Endless wars, coin debasement, inflation, splits | Silver content of the denarius collapsed over the third century |
| Portugal | Atlantic coast, navigation skill, the sea route to Asian spices | Small population stretched across oceans | Absorbed into the Spanish crown in 1580 |
| Spain | American silver and a vast Habsburg empire | Wars on many fronts, silver-fuelled inflation | Philip II defaulted on royal debts in 1557, 1560, 1575 and 1596 |
| Netherlands | Trade hub, merchant culture, the Bank of Amsterdam (1609), the first great joint-stock company (VOC, 1602) | Wars with England and France, rising rivals | Defeat in the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780 to 1784), French conquest in 1795 |
| Britain | Island safety, coal and industry, the Royal Navy, credible public debt after 1688 and the Bank of England (1694) | Two world wars, empire costs, rising America | Stopped paying its WWI debts to the US after 1934, devalued sterling in 1949 and 1967, IMF loan in 1976 |
| United States | Two oceans, a resource-rich continent, immigration, rule of law, deep capital markets, inventive culture | Today's test: debt, polarization, rivals in hardware | Changed gold clauses in 1933 and ended gold convertibility in 1971, but has always paid its bonds in dollars |

Notice the pattern. Defaults and devaluations rarely end an empire in one blow. They are symptoms of a deeper strain: spending on war and status that the productive economy can no longer carry. France shows how fast it can turn political: a partial default in 1770 and a debt crisis in the 1780s led to the calling of the Estates General in 1789 and the Revolution.

### What it really takes to be number one

- Produce: a real economy that makes things the world wants.
- Protect: security for trade routes and allies.
- Promise: institutions that make debt credible, so the state can borrow cheaply in a crisis. Britain after 1688 and Hamilton's America after 1790 both won wars partly because lenders trusted them.
- Plumbing: deep, open financial markets that others can use.
- People: a culture of work, saving, invention and trust in contracts.
- Place: geography that protects and connects, from Britain's channel to America's two oceans.

No single leader had all six forever. Each rose when its mix fit the technology and trade of its age, and each faded when debt, war and complacency ate the mix away. The open question for this century is whether the United States can keep its plumbing and promise strong enough to outlast the strain on the other pillars, and whether any rival is willing to give up enough control to offer a deeper market.

### Geography deals the cards, and the deck keeps changing

Place is one of the six pillars, but what makes a place valuable changes with technology. Every era has its own rare and needed thing, and the countries sitting on it get a turn near the top. Then the technology moves, and yesterday's treasure can lose its value almost overnight.

*[Picture on the web page: What the world found rare and needed, era by era. The places that held it rose. Some lost it when technology changed.]*

Chile's nitrate mines made it rich in the late 1800s, because nitrate was needed for fertilizer and explosives. In 1913 German chemists began making nitrogen in factories, and the boom ended. Botswana grew faster than almost any country from 1965 to 1995 on diamonds, and is now strained because laboratory-grown stones have pushed natural diamond prices down. Today the rare and needed things are battery metals, rare earths, chips, cheap power and compute.

### Rich ground, poor people: the resource curse

Having the cards is not the same as winning the hand. South Africa mines about 70% of the world's platinum, 46% of its chromium and 40% of its manganese, according to the US Geological Survey, and holds most of the world's platinum-group reserves. Yet its official unemployment rate was 33.6% in the second quarter of 2026, and 47.4% for people aged 15 to 34. The Democratic Republic of the Congo mines about 72% of the world's cobalt, the metal in most rechargeable batteries, yet remains one of the poorest countries on earth and has suffered decades of war.

*[Picture on the web page: Who captures the value of a mineral. The miner keeps the smallest share. China refines about 79% of the world's cobalt.]*

Economists call this the resource curse. Its parts fit together. Easy money from the ground lets rulers govern without taxing or serving citizens. Armed groups fight to control the mines. A flood of export dollars lifts the currency and makes every other industry uncompetitive, which is called Dutch disease after the Netherlands' gas boom of the 1960s. And the money is made at the next steps, refining, manufacturing and finance, which happen elsewhere.

- **Myth:** Resource-rich countries stay poor because of their culture or because people are out of touch with modern money habits.
- **Reality:** Habits such as saving in banks, paying tax and trusting contracts do matter, but research points mainly to institutions, and habits follow institutions. People save and invest where titles are safe and money holds its value. Colonial economies were built to dig and ship raw materials out. In South Africa, apartheid barred most people from owning land, skilled jobs and good schools until 1994, and since then power cuts, failing rail and ports, and corruption exposed by the state capture inquiry have held growth back. Change the institutions and the habits change with them.

The exceptions prove the rule. Norway built strong institutions before it found oil, then saved the oil money abroad in a fund that now owns about 1.5% of every listed company in the world. Botswana negotiated a partnership with the diamond company De Beers and spent the money on schools, health and roads. Geography deals the cards. Institutions decide whether a country can play them.

> Watch who refines, not just who digs. Indonesia banned exports of raw nickel ore in 2020 to force refining at home, and China's grip on refining battery metals and rare earths gives it more leverage than any mine does. On the country leaderboard, the processing step and the financial step are where rare things turn into power.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why might a country with huge savings choose to lend them to the US instead of spending them at home?
A: To keep its own currency cheap for exporters, to hold insurance against a dollar shortage, because its home markets are too small or controlled to hold the savings, and because its elites trust US courts with their wealth. Its households pay part of the cost through dearer imports and lower consumption.

**Check yourself.** Q: South Africa mines about 70% of the world's platinum. Why has that not made most South Africans rich?
A: Because the value is captured mostly at later steps and by owners, while the institutions that turn resource money into broad jobs, skills and ownership were weakened by history and poor governance. Rare ground pays only when institutions let it.
