# The money clubs: who writes the rules, who can really print, and the game the world plays

Rulebook Level 2 (The machinery), chapter 04. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-clubs.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-clubs . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*The G7, G10 and G20 are clubs, not governments. This chapter shows what they can and cannot do, which countries can really print money and which cannot, why Malaysia sits outside every club but inside the game, and five questions that turn the world into a game board.*

**In short**
- The G7, G10 and G20 are clubs, not governments: no treaty, no money of their own, no power to force anyone.
- The G20 moved fast in the 2008 to 2009 crisis and slowly in calm years. That is the usual pattern, with exceptions.
- Printing power is a ladder with three locks: its own currency, debt owed in that currency, and markets that let it print without a crash.
- Euro members, Ecuador and Panama cannot print on their own. Turkey, Argentina and Sri Lanka owed much of their debt in dollars and paid with inflation or default.
- Malaysia is in no club but lives by club rules. It sits on rung 3 of the printing ladder, where rung 1 is the top: 98.8% of its federal debt is borrowed in ringgit at home, yet foreigners hold about a fifth of its government bonds. Its brakes are the ringgit and its own fiscal laws.
- Five questions open the game board, and a four-step routine turns any headline into a game.

Malaysia's share of world exports is about two and a half times its share of world output. It lives by trade, so it lives by rules made in rooms it is not in. For each club's share of world wealth and debt, see the Level 2 chapter "The world's balance sheet".

None of these clubs has a treaty (a binding agreement between states), a binding vote, its own money or a large staff. They cannot pass a law, print a dollar or force a private lender. ### 2. Is the G20 just playing around?

Many people suspect the G20 is just playing around. Here is its record on ten big promises, chosen by one rule: every headline pledge with a target you can check, every crisis programme, and the latest summits. The grades are our judgment of the evidence.

| Promise | What happened | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 2009 crisis package ($1.1 trillion, plus $5 trillion of national spending plans) | Made, including a new SDR allocation in August 2009 (SDRs are reserve money the IMF creates and hands to its members). Most of the $5 trillion was plans already announced | Delivered |
| A Financial Stability Board | Created in April 2009, still running | Delivered |
| 2021 SDR allocation for all IMF members | Made in 2021, with G20 backing | Delivered |
| Basel III bank rules | Phased in from 2013. The US re-proposed the final part in March 2026, no final rule yet | Partly |
| 2020 debt pause for poor countries | $12.9 billion paused for 48 of 73 eligible countries. One private lender joined | Partly |
| The Common Framework for debt deals | Each case took about 2 to more than 5 years (Chad, Zambia, Ghana, Ethiopia) | Partly, slow |
| A 15% global minimum tax | In force in the EU, UK, Japan, Korea and Malaysia. The US never passed it and won a carve-out in January 2026 | Partly, then diluted |
| No new protectionism (2009) | Members kept adding barriers. The 2025 US tariffs broke the spirit outright | Not delivered |
| Phase out fossil fuel subsidies (2009) | Global subsidies were still very large in 2022 | Not delivered |
| The 2025 and 2026 summits | The US boycotted Johannesburg in 2025, then as host shut South Africa out of Miami | Stalled |

Score: 3 delivered, 4 partly, 2 not delivered, 1 stalled. So the suspicion is half right, and the answer depends on which promises you count, which is why the rule for choosing them is stated above. The quick actions all came in crises: the 2009 package, the 2020 debt pause and the 2021 SDRs. The pause itself was fast, but the debt deals that should have followed took years. In calm years the G20 has moved slowly, and in 2025 and 2026 it split in the open. So the suspicion fits October 2026. It would not have fitted April 2009.

> **The game behind the scorecard.** In a crisis the G20 plays a stag hunt (part 4 explains this game and the next): acting together pays most, but each member acts only if it expects the others to, and a shared shock makes everyone expect it. Even in 2009 some did less than others. In calm years it plays free-rider games. A club with no treaty can coordinate the willing, not force the unwilling.

### Part 2 of 4. Who can really print money

### 3. Three locks on the printing press

Here printing means a state and its central bank creating the money that the state's debts are owed in. Banks are the other printer, which the Level 2 chapter "Who really creates money" covers. To print freely, a country must open three locks.

1. Lock 1, the law. Does it issue its own currency? Italy, Ecuador and Panama do not. They use the euro or the US dollar, made by someone else.
2. Lock 2, the debt. Does the state owe its debt in money it can create? Sri Lanka could print rupees, but owed dollars, and stopped paying on 12 April 2022.
3. Lock 3, the market. Can it print a lot in a crisis without a currency crash or runaway prices? That depends on whether the world wants its money, how open its markets are, and its inflation record. Turkey and Argentina had their own money but owed a large part of their debt in dollars, and saw inflation reach 72% (2022) and 220% (2024). They failed lock 3 and partly lock 2.

Each lock is needed and none is enough alone. Passing locks 1 and 2 lets a country pay in its own money, if its law allows and its parliament does not block payment, as the US debt-limit fights of 2011 and 2023 threatened. It cannot choose what that money will buy.

### 4. The ladder

*[Picture on the web page: Six rungs of printing power, with rung 1 at the top (rung 5 has two halves, 5a and 5b). Malaysia sits on rung 3, highlighted: the middle of the ladder, at the strong end of its rung.]*

| Rung | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Reserve issuer | All three locks open with room. In a crisis the world buys its money | US by far, then the euro area as a whole, Japan, the UK |
| 2. Sovereign printer | All three open, but the world holds little of its money | Canada, Australia, Switzerland, Korea |
| 3. Constrained printer | Locks 1 and 2 open, lock 3 partly. Printing shows fast in the currency, in foreign selling or behind capital controls | China (controls), India, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, South Africa, Russia, Malaysia |
| 4. Fragile printer | Own money, but recently paid for printing with a crash, high inflation or default | Turkey, Argentina, Sri Lanka, Zimbabwe |
| 5a. Tied by choice | Could print, but tied its money to the dollar. A peg fixes the exchange rate. A currency board backs every note with dollars | Saudi Arabia (peg), Hong Kong (currency board) |
| 5b. No printer of its own | Must earn, tax or borrow the money it owes | Euro members one by one, Ecuador, Panama |

The rungs are our grading, not an official list. Two edges: Indonesia's central bank holds about 29% of its government's debt (June 2026), after buying from 2020 to 2022 and again in 2026, yet inflation was 1.9% in 2025. A rung 3 printer can print for a while if markets trust its brakes. Germany sits on rung 5b with the cheapest borrowing in the euro area, because markets trust its budget. And the euro area as a whole can print through the ECB, on conditions.

### Part 3 of 4. Malaysia, outside every club

### 5. Malaysia: outside every club, inside the game

Malaysia sits in no money club. Indonesia is the only Southeast Asian G20 member. Malaysia's seats are ASEAN (a founder in 1967), APEC, the IMF, the Chiang Mai reserve pool with China, Japan and Korea, and a BRICS partner seat, announced in October 2024 and in effect from 1 January 2025. Yet it applies rules written elsewhere, such as the 15% global minimum tax from 1 January 2025, a deal the US helped write and later won a carve-out from.

| Who holds Malaysia's federal government debt, Ministry of Finance measure (end June 2026) | Share |
|---|---|
| EPF | 30.5% |
| Banks | 28.5% |
| Insurers | 5.2% |
| Bank Negara | 3.8% |
| KWAP (civil service pensions) | 2.2% |
| Development finance institutions | 2.1% |
| Other domestic investors | 6.2% |
| Mostly foreigners (the rest, implied) | about 21.5% |

Bank Negara counts only government securities traded in the market, and on that count it holds near 1%. The Ministry counts all federal debt, which gives 3.8%. Both are right for what they count. The same difference explains why government bonds alone (MGS and GII) were RM1.309 trillion in March 2026 while federal debt, which also counts Treasury bills and loans, was RM1.38 trillion in June.

Federal debt was RM1.38 trillion at the end of June 2026, 63.1% of output. 98.8% of it is domestic debt: borrowed in ringgit in Malaysia's own market (Ministry of Finance). That does not mean held by Malaysians: foreigners hold about a fifth of the government bonds. Nearly half the debt (47.9%) is Islamic paper. Bank Negara, the central bank, holds only 3.8%, against about 29% at Indonesia's. Malaysia has not used its printer to fund the budget.

| Malaysia's own brakes | The rule | Where Malaysia is |
|---|---|---|
| Debt target (2023 fiscal law, Act 850) | Bring federal debt down to 60% of output over the medium term | 63.1% (end June 2026) |
| Debt-service limit (Act 850) | Debt service no more than 15% of revenue | 15.8% (2026 projection) |
| Statutory ceiling | MGS, GII and Islamic Treasury bills no more than 65% of output | Expected to stay under 65% in 2026 (Ministry of Finance, as reported) |
| Central bank law | The Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009 governs any lending by Bank Negara to the government | Bank Negara holds 3.8% of federal debt |

So Malaysia has two kinds of brake. The laws are the legal brakes, and today it is above two of its own lines. The ringgit is the market brake.

Malaysia did not take an IMF programme in 1998, while Thailand, Indonesia and Korea did. Today Bank Negara calls its regime a managed float, and the IMF calls it floating. Both are true: the IMF's floating allows smoothing, which is what Bank Negara means by managing. Offshore ringgit trading is still not allowed.

Against Indonesia, Thailand and Singapore, Malaysia is typical in most ways: its own currency, a float with smoothing, debt at home, low inflation, a partly managed capital account (some limits on moving money in and out). It is an outlier in five: the 1998 controls and peg, the highest foreign share of the three ASEAN peers checked (about 21.5% against 12.8% for Indonesia and 9.3% for Thailand), a compulsory pension fund as its biggest lender, nearly half its debt in Islamic form, and a central bank that holds very little government debt.

### Part 4 of 4. The game the world plays

### 6. Game theory in five minutes

Game theory asks: what is each player's best move, given what the others might do? The table lists six common games. Each one is then drawn as a box, seven boxes in all because the run game gets two, with two players, two moves each, and what each gets in every cell, from best to worst. Stag hunt and focal point are both coordination games: in the first, everyone must act at once, in the second, everyone just follows the habit.

| Box | Game | The shape | Where it settles |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stag hunt | Everyone gains most if all act. Acting alone is costly | All act or none. Each acts only if it expects the others to |
| 2 | Free rider | A shared good is paid for by some and enjoyed by all | Each player's best move is to let others pay |
| 3 | Prisoner's dilemma | Each gains by cheating whatever the other does | Both cheat and both end up worse off |
| 4 | Focal point | Many choices would work, so players pick the one everyone expects | The habit wins, even if others are as good |
| 5 and 6 | Run | Lenders who expect others to stay, stay. Those who expect others to flee, flee first | The same borrower can be safe or bust depending on belief. Big reserves or a backstop make staying the best move |
| 7 | Chicken | Two players drive at each other | Each tries to look more committed so the other swerves |

> **What game theory leaves out.** Domestic politics (a leader facing an election may choose the worse national outcome), ideology, accidents and plain error. Use the games as a lens on incentives, not as proof that every move was planned.

### 7. Five questions that open the game board

Guess each answer before you open it. Each one opens a game, and each game is a real case.

**Curiosity questions**
- Q: Why did the G20 move within months in 2008 to 2009, but take years on poor countries' debts?
  A: Two games. 2009 was a stag hunt (box 1): the shared fear of collapse made each member expect the others to act, and out came a $1.1 trillion package. Debt relief is a free-rider game (box 2): relief from one lender frees cash to pay the others. The 2020 pause covered $12.9 billion, but only one private lender joined, and Ethiopia's deal took more than five years to reach agreement in principle. (builds on [The country leaderboard: deep markets and the game nations play](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-leader.md))
- Q: If every country wants a tariff edge or a cheaper currency, what happens when they all try at once?
  A: Tariffs are the clean prisoner's dilemma (box 3): all lose when all raise them, as in the tariff war of the early 1930s. Currencies are murkier: in the 1930s devaluing helped the countries that did it, and historians find that devaluing together would have sped recovery (Eichengreen and Sachs, 1985). Exits have been deals like the 1985 Plaza Accord, or truces like the October 2025 US and China pause, which can lapse. Two giants trading threats are playing chicken (box 7). (builds on [The country leaderboard: deep markets and the game nations play](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-leader.md))
- Q: Why does the world keep saving in US dollars even while complaining about the US?
  A: A focal point (box 4). Money is useful because others use it. The dollar was 56.7% of world reserves in mid 2026, nearly three times the euro. The yuan was 2.1%, largely because China keeps capital controls. (builds on [How the world gets its dollars](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-04.md))
- Q: Why did Malaysia and its neighbours pile up dollars after 1998 instead of spending them?
  A: To change the payoffs of a run on their currency (box 5). In 1997 to 1998 the ringgit fell from about 2.50 to about 4.88, and Thailand, Indonesia and Korea had to accept IMF conditions. Malaysia's reserves rose from $21.5 billion in 1997 to $132.6 billion in June 2026. Big reserves make attacking a losing bet, and mean the country need not accept the IMF's terms. (builds on [The view from a smaller currency: Malaysia, savings and where money lands first](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-em.md))
- Q: Why can Japan owe about twice its economy and borrow cheaply, while Sri Lanka defaulted?
  A: A run on the debt (box 6): who holds it, and in which money, largely decides it. Japan owes yen mostly to itself: its central bank holds about 43% and foreigners about 13% (end 2025). Sri Lanka owed dollars to foreigners and stopped paying on 12 April 2022. Malaysia borrows in ringgit, but foreigners hold about a fifth of its bonds, so its risk is a weaker ringgit, not default. (builds on [The world's balance sheet: what it owns, what it owes, and how countries are built](https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/ai/rulebook/rb-worldbs.md))

| Box 1. Stag hunt: London 2009 | Others stimulate | Others hold back |
|---|---|---|
| You stimulate | Best: your spending leaks abroad, but theirs comes back as exports | Worst: your spending leaks abroad and you carry the debt |
| You hold back | Middle: safe, but you add little to the recovery | Middle: safe, but the slump deepens |

Settles: all act or none. In April 2009 shared fear made each expect the others to act. Misses: leaders were also rescuing their own banks, and some did less than others.

| Box 2. Free rider: debt relief | Other lenders give relief | Other lenders hold out |
|---|---|---|
| You give relief | Good: the borrower recovers and pays more later | Worst: your relief pays the others in full |
| You hold out | Best: you are paid in full from cash others freed | Bad: the borrower stalls or defaults |

Settles: holding out pays each lender whatever the others do, so relief needs a rule binding all. The 2020 pause had none for private lenders. Misses: China's lenders had their own rules on losses.

| Box 3. Prisoner's dilemma: tariffs | Other keeps tariffs low | Other raises tariffs |
|---|---|---|
| You keep tariffs low | Good: trade grows for both | Worst: you lose sales and gain nothing |
| You raise tariffs | Best: you gain at the other's cost | Bad: trade shrinks for both |

Settles: both raise and both lose, unless a deal or fear of next year's reply holds them back. Misses: tariffs also serve home aims such as revenue, jobs and security.

| Box 4. Focal point: the dollar | Others keep the dollar | Others switch |
|---|---|---|
| You keep the dollar | Good: deep markets, cheap to trade | Bad: you are left in the old money |
| You switch | Bad: thin markets, costly hedging | Good, after a costly move |

Settles: all keep or all switch, and the habit is the dollar. Misses: sanctions risk. Freezing Russia's reserves in 2022 raised the cost of holding dollars for some.

| Box 5. Run on a currency: 1997 to 1998 | Other funds attack | Other funds stay |
|---|---|---|
| You attack | Small reserves: best, the currency breaks. Big reserves: bad, the defence holds and the bet costs you | Worst: you bet alone and lose |
| You stay | Middle: you miss the gain, or the loss | Middle: nothing happens |

Settles: with small reserves, all attack or none. Big reserves make staying each fund's best move, and the IMF becomes an outside option, not a must. Misses: in 1998 Malaysia used controls, not only reserves.

| Box 6. Run on a government's debt | Other holders stay | Other holders sell |
|---|---|---|
| You stay | Good: paid in full | Worst: left holding as the price or the currency falls |
| You sell | Bad: you give up the yield | Bad, but better than staying: you got out first |

Settles: all stay or all sell. A pension fund at home mostly cannot leave the currency, a foreign fund can, so a state that owes its own money at home risks a weaker currency, not default. Misses: who pays the loss.

| Box 7. Chicken: the US and China, 2025 to 2026 | Other backs down | Other escalates |
|---|---|---|
| You back down | Middle: calm trade, nobody wins face | Bad: you lose face and the other gains |
| You escalate | Best: you win the standoff | Worst: a full trade war hurts both most |

Settles: each tries to look committed so the other swerves first. Real case: in 2025 the US and China raised tariffs on each other step by step, then agreed a pause after their October 2025 leaders' meeting. That truce is due around 10 November 2026. Misses: both sides also act for audiences at home.

### 8. Read any headline as a game

1. Players: who can act? Include the quiet ones.
2. Moves: what can each do? Two moves each if you can.
3. Payoffs: what does each get from each pair of moves? Best, good, bad or worst.
4. Order and information: who moves first, and who sees what?

Then name the game, and ask what it leaves out: elections, ideology, accidents and plain error.

| Step | Worked example: "Why Foreign Investors Disposed RM5.6 Billion In Malaysian Govt Bonds In July" (Business Today, 10 August 2026) |
|---|---|
| Players | Foreign funds, local holders such as EPF and the banks, Bank Negara |
| Moves | Funds sell or stay. Locals buy or wait. Bank Negara can sell dollars |
| Payoffs | Selling early dodges a ringgit fall but gives up the yield. Locals mostly cannot leave the ringgit |
| Order | Funds watch US yields. Flow data come out a month later |
| Game | A run on the debt (box 6) that did not start: 0.43% of the market, about RM11 billion back into government bonds in August, the ringgit 4.09 to 4.08 in July |
| Left out | The research house blamed outside events: US and Iran tension, oil and US yields |

### Go deeper: A sixth game: the race to the bottom on tax

Countries gain by undercutting each other's tax rates to attract company profits, and all lose revenue if all undercut. That is a prisoner's dilemma, and the 2021 deal set a 15% floor. Malaysia applied it from 1 January 2025. The US did not enact it, and in January 2026 the OECD package shielded US-based groups from the main top-up rules. The largest player took the defector's payoff, and the others accepted it to keep the rest of the deal. The US argues its own minimum taxes do a similar job, which is a real debate, not only defection.

### Go deeper: Three balance-sheet walks: the dollar privilege, defending the ringgit, a euro member borrowing

The dollar privilege. A US household buys Malaysian-made chips. The exporter sells the dollars to its bank, which sells them to Bank Negara, which buys US Treasury bills. No dollars were created abroad. Dollars moved from a US household to a foreign central bank, which lent them back to the US government. The US got goods for IOUs in its own money. Bank Negara created ringgit to buy the dollars, and usually soaks them back up later.

Defending the ringgit. A foreign fund sells Malaysian bonds and wants dollars. Bank Negara sells dollars from its reserves and receives ringgit, so banks' ringgit reserves fall. It then usually tops them back up to keep its interest rate at 2.75%. The cost is dollar reserves, which is why the 0.9 times ratio matters. A defence that lasts too long runs reserves down, as in 1997 to 1998.

A euro member borrowing. Italy spends more than it taxes. It must sell bonds to banks, funds, households and foreigners. The ECB can create euros and buy Italian bonds on the resale market, but only by a decision taken for the whole euro area. In a panic, deposits can move to banks in another euro country, as in Greece, where about EUR 41 billion left before the June 2015 controls.
