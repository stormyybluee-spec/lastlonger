# The deflation debate: would anyone spend a money that grows?

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 09. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-defl.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-defl . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*If a dollar buys more tomorrow, why spend it today? The oldest argument against hard money, why it never ends, and what both sides miss.*

Chapter 8 showed that money has seasons. This chapter takes one question from the cold season and follows it all the way down, because it sits under almost every fight about gold, Bitcoin and central banks. The question is simple: if I know my $1 will buy more tomorrow, why would I spend it today?

### The case against a growing money

1. Prices are expected to fall, so people wait to buy cars, homes and machines.
2. Shops and factories sell less, so they cut prices further and cut jobs.
3. Workers who lose income spend even less. Borrowers find their debts heavier, because the money they owe is worth more while their income shrinks.
4. Defaults rise, banks pull back, and the loop feeds itself. Economists call this a deflationary spiral. Irving Fisher described its debt side in 1933 and called it debt deflation.

This is why almost every major central bank aims for a little inflation, usually 2%, rather than zero or falling prices. The cases they point to are the Great Depression and Japan's lost decades, both in chapter 8.

### The case for a growing money

- You still need things today. Food, rent, medicine and school fees do not wait. People keep buying phones and televisions even though they know next year's model will be cheaper and better.
- Waiting has a cost. A sofa you buy next year is a year without a sofa. Economists call this time preference: people value having things sooner.
- History has good deflations. From the 1870s to the 1890s US prices fell for years while output, railways and industry grew fast, because new technology made things cheaper to produce.
- The data are weaker than the fear. A 2015 Bank for International Settlements study covering about 140 years and up to 38 economies found only a weak link between falling consumer prices and slower growth, mostly driven by the Great Depression. The damage came mainly from falling property prices combined with private debt.
- Discipline. When money cannot be printed, governments and companies must fund projects from real savings, so fewer bad projects get started.

### Why the fight never ends: two sides, two different deflations

*[Picture on the web page: The same falling prices with opposite causes. Good deflation starts with cheaper production. Bad deflation starts with collapsing demand and heavy debt.]*

The argument goes in circles because each side pictures a different deflation. The hard-money side pictures good deflation: things get cheaper because we got better at making them, incomes hold steady, and every paycheck buys more. The central-bank side pictures bad deflation: prices fall because nobody is buying, while debts stay fixed. Both have happened. Which one you get depends mostly on one thing: how much debt is in the system when prices start to fall.

> Today's world carries record debt. The US government alone owes $40.44T. In a deflation every one of those dollars gets heavier to repay. That is the real reason no heavily indebted government will choose a deflationary money. The debate sounds like it is about shoppers delaying purchases. Underneath, it is about who carries the debt.

### Spend the worse money, keep the better one

An older rule explains what people actually do when they can choose between two monies. It is Gresham's law: bad money drives out good. People spend the money that is losing value and hoard the one that is gaining. In Tudor England people spent the clipped and debased coins and kept the full-weight ones at home.

> **Picture this.** On 22 May 2010 a programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas, the first well-known purchase of real goods with Bitcoin. At about $86,000 a coin in early October 2026, those coins would be worth roughly $860 million. After stories like that, almost nobody spends Bitcoin on pizza. They spend dollars and keep the Bitcoin. That is Gresham's law in a single slice.

So a money expected to grow tends to become a store of value, while a money that slowly melts becomes the one people spend. That is why the medium of exchange argument against deflationary money is partly right and partly beside the point. People do not stop spending. They spend the other money. Today that other money is the dollar, or a dollar stablecoin, sitting next to gold or Bitcoin in the same wallet.

| Question | Mild-inflation camp | Hard-money camp |
|---|---|---|
| Will people stop spending? | Enough of them to start a spiral | No, needs and time preference keep them buying |
| What about debt? | Inflation shrinks debts in real terms | Debt should be smaller to begin with |
| Who gains? | Borrowers, including governments | Savers and lenders |
| Who loses? | Savers and people on fixed incomes | Borrowers and the heavily leveraged |
| Wages | Mild inflation lets real wages adjust without cutting paychecks | Falling prices raise real wages without raises |
| Best evidence | 1929 to 1933, Japan after 1990 | 1870s to 1890s, technology prices today |

- **Myth:** Falling prices always mean a weak economy.
- **Reality:** Prices of televisions, computers and phones have fallen for decades while the industries making them boomed. What hurts is falling prices plus heavy debt plus collapsing demand, not falling prices alone.

> This matters for the future this desk studies. Robots, AI and cheap solar push the cost of making things down, a good-deflation force. A debt-heavy system cannot let that show up as falling prices, so it creates enough new money to keep prices rising gently. The productivity gain does not vanish. It flows to whoever receives the new money first and to owners of scarce things such as land, energy and platforms. That is the link to the Cantillon effect and the platform toll booths in chapter 16.

**Check yourself.** Q: If prices are expected to fall 2% a year, why might a family still buy a fridge today?
A: Because it needs a fridge now. Waiting a year to save 2% means a year without a fridge. Real needs and time preference keep spending going. A spiral starts when incomes fall and debts bite, not from cheaper prices alone.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why do most people spend dollars and keep gold or Bitcoin?
A: Gresham's law. When two monies circulate side by side, people spend the one expected to lose value and hoard the one expected to gain.
