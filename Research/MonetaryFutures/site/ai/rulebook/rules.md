# The rules, the mistakes and the questions (Fundamentals)


## The rules

1. **Money is a promise kept in a ledger. Ask who made the promise and who keeps the ledger.** The form of money keeps changing, from clay to coins to code. The promise behind it is the thing that holds or fails.
2. **Check all three jobs: medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account.** Few things do all three well. Use each money for the job it is good at.
3. **Every deposit is somebody's loan, and every bank loan creates a deposit.** Modern money is mostly bank credit, so the health of money depends on the health of lending.
4. **Interest can be paid from money that circulates. Debt that grows faster than income cannot be paid forever.** The musical chairs problem appears when savers hoard and debts outgrow incomes, not because interest is unpayable.
5. **Borrow only what you can service through a bad season without selling.** Forced sellers sell at the bottom. Leverage turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss.
6. **Know your season. Ask whether money is melting or hardening.** The same choice, like holding cash or long bonds, wins in one season and loses in the next.
7. **Nominal is not real. Divide by inflation before you celebrate.** Money is a rubber ruler. A raise below inflation is a pay cut.
8. **Measure what matters in hours of work.** Hours sidestep the moving ruler and show what is really getting cheaper or dearer.
9. **Watch asset prices, not only consumer prices.** The price of entering ownership can run far ahead of pay while headline inflation looks calm.
10. **Own something productive from your first paycheck.** Ownership compounds while you sleep. Wages stop when you stop.
11. **Keep idle cash where it earns the safe rate.** Banks and token issuers keep the gap between what safe bills pay and what they pay you.
12. **No asset is safe in every season. Spread your bets across seasons, not just across names.** Cash failed in the 1970s, long bonds failed from 2020 to 2023, stocks failed from 1929 to 1932.
13. **Gold is insurance against the system, not income.** It pays nothing and can fall for twenty years, but it shines when trust in money breaks.
14. **When rulers need money they cannot raise honestly, they water down the currency.** From Rome's denarius to modern inflation, debasement is the oldest hidden tax.
15. **A temporary suspension of a money promise often becomes permanent.** The 1971 gold suspension was announced as temporary and never ended.
16. **Reserves held in someone else's currency come with someone else's conditions.** Russia's frozen reserves in 2022 taught every government this rule.
17. **Every trade deficit is also a capital inflow. Follow the dollars both ways.** Cheap imports and cheap loans are two halves of the same loop, and someone pays for each.
18. **Calm years breed risky borrowing. Be most careful when lending feels safest.** Minsky showed that stability itself pushes people toward fragile debt.
19. **When everyone repays at once, money shrinks. Keep a cash buffer for that day.** Fisher showed that mass repayment can make debts heavier, so liquidity matters most when it is scarcest.
20. **Watch interest against defense, and debt against output.** These ratios have signaled decline in past empires, and interest now exceeds US defense spending.
21. **New rails, old anchors. Ask what sits under any new money and who earns the interest.** Stablecoins look new but are backed by Treasury bills, and the issuer keeps the yield.
22. **Find the toll booths, and where you can, own one.** Owners of crossing points, platforms, land and payment rails earn whether players win or lose.
23. **Expect any fixed money promise to bend in a great war or crisis.** Gold promises were suspended in 1914, 1933 and 1971.
24. **Stop inflation early. Inflation people expect is far harder to stop than inflation that surprises.** The 1970s show what happens when expectations take root, and Volcker shows the price of uprooting them.
25. **Judge any plan to open the board by whether it gives real assets or credentials at scale.** The Homestead Act and the GI Bill worked because they gave land, schooling and loan guarantees large enough to matter.
26. **Separate the story from the mechanism. A ratio that only works today is a coincidence.** The barter myth and the gold times ten idea both sound right and fail the test of history.
27. **Check big decisions with more than one ruler: dollars, real dollars, hours and a hard asset.** Every ruler moves. Agreement across several rulers is the closest thing to truth.
28. **Pass on the mistakes, not just the successes.** Each generation forgets its parents' crisis. Written lessons are the cheapest inheritance.
29. **A crash headline is a repricing, not a robbery. Ask who was forced to sell, not who stole the value.** Most lost market value was never cash, and forced sellers are what turn a dip into a crash.
30. **Before you buy into a bucket, ask what is moving money into it and what would move it out.** Rotation, not circulation, decides prices in the medium run.
31. **Learn the bond before you trust any story about money.** Rates, mortgages, stablecoins and currency power all rest on government bonds.
32. **Ask who pays for a country's surplus, not just who earns it.** Cheap currencies and exported savings move costs onto households.
33. **Respect the bond market. It votes every second.** Governments that lose its trust pay for it in higher interest bills.
34. **Spend the melting money, keep the growing one, and know which is which.** Gresham's law decides which money people spend and which they save.
35. **Know which ruler your future spending will use.** Savings that look safe in the home currency can shrink in the currency your future bills are priced in.
36. **Follow where new money lands first.** Hubs receive it early and at full value. Talent and capital follow.
37. **Wealth on paper is a price, not a pile.** Most wealth is the last trade's price on existing assets at today's interest rates. It cannot all be sold at that price.
38. **Look for the next base layer before it is titled.** Newcomers get ownership early only at new frontiers: new industries, energy, compute and whatever comes next.
39. **The longer the bond, the bigger the swing.** Long bonds lock in rent for more years, so their prices move most when rates change.
40. **Money is debt. Know who owes the other side of every dollar you hold.** Deposits, notes and bonds are all someone's liability. Only real things and gold or Bitcoin sit outside the mirror.
41. **Tax is the subscription fee for the board. Argue about the size and who pays, not whether.** Without the state there is no common money, no title and no court.
42. **A yield is nominal. Subtract tax and inflation before you call it a gain.** Bonds can pay every coupon on time and still lose buying power.
43. **Geography deals the cards. Institutions decide whether a country can play them.** Resource-rich countries with weak institutions often stay poor while refiners and financiers capture the value.
44. **Crypto is three families, not one. Know which one you hold.** Bitcoin, stablecoins and other tokens answer every money question differently.
45. **If you cannot say where a yield comes from, the yield is the product being sold.** Terra's 20% and many schemes since were paid from reserves or newer money, not earnings.
46. **The ruler belongs to whatever debts and taxes are written in.** Until wages, rents, mortgages and taxes are written in a token, every token gets converted to dollars somewhere.
47. **Launching a token is printing money. Ask who pays for it.** Unless a network earns real fees, the founders' seigniorage is paid by later buyers.
48. **Money is not only made by bank loans. Ask which floor it lands on.** Fed purchases and Treasury moves change reserves upstairs. Only some of them reach the deposits people can spend.
49. **When the Fed buys bonds, look at who sold them.** From a bank, only reserves rise. From a pension fund or other nonbank, a new public deposit appears too.
50. **Banks are the everyday printer. The government plus the Fed is the emergency printer.** Bank-loan money disappears when loans are repaid. Deficit money held by the Fed and banks tends to stay, because the debt is rolled over.

## Mistakes each generation made

| When | Mistake | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Rome, the 200s AD | Paid for armies by debasing the silver coin, then tried to stop the resulting price surge with a price edict in 301 AD. | Price controls cannot hold back a watered-down money. Goods simply disappear from markets. |
| The 1890s, gold standard era | Kept money fixed to gold while the economy grew, so prices fell for two decades and debtors, mostly farmers, were crushed. | A money that cannot grow with the economy punishes borrowers, and its supply should not depend on mining luck. |
| The 1920s | Bought stocks with borrowed money, often putting down a small fraction of the price, in the belief that prosperity was permanent. | Leverage plus euphoria produces forced selling. When prices turn, borrowed money must still be repaid. |
| The early 1930s policymakers | Let thousands of banks fail, allowed the money supply to shrink by about a third, clung to the gold standard and raised tariffs. | In a panic, the central bank must lend freely against good collateral. Countries that left gold earlier recovered sooner. |
| The 1960s | Paid for war and new programs at once while promising gold at $35, ignoring the Triffin warning. | A promise that must keep growing will eventually outgrow what backs it. Plan the exit before the run. |
| The 1970s | Treated inflation as a small price for jobs, tried wage and price controls, and tightened only briefly before easing again. | Once people expect inflation, it feeds itself. Stopping it late costs far more than stopping it early. |
| Latin American borrowers and their lenders, around 1980 | Recycled oil dollars into loans at floating interest rates in a currency the borrowers could not print. | Borrowing in someone else's currency at floating rates hands your fate to their central bank. |
| Japan in the 1990s | Let banks hide bad loans and kept failing firms alive, so the economy repaired its balance sheets slowly for decades. | Recognize losses quickly and recapitalize banks. Slow cleanup turns a bust into a lost generation. |
| The 2000s, up to 2008 | Believed house prices could not fall nationwide, lent to borrowers who could not repay and stacked leverage on top. | Housing is not risk-free. Stability bred the fragile debt that Minsky warned about. |
| The 2010s | Assumed near-zero interest rates would last and loaded up on long-term bonds and cheap fixed debt for assets. | Safe assets are safe only in a season. When rates rose, banks like Silicon Valley Bank failed holding them. |
| The early 2020s | Called the inflation surge transitory for too long, and chased high crypto yields from lenders that re-lent customer assets. | Every yield has a source. If you cannot see where the return comes from, you may be the return. |

## Questions readers ask

**If the market drops 20%, did someone else make that money?**
Mostly no. Market cap is the last price times all shares, so a fall rewrites the value of every share even though only a few traded. Sellers received cash from buyers. Only short sellers and option buyers profit from the fall, and only by what their direct counterparties lose. See chapter 19.

**If the government can create money, why does it borrow and tax at all?**
Taxes give money its demand and keep spending from overheating the economy. Borrowing lets the government spend more than it taxes without the central bank directly creating new money for it, which the law restricts. If a government simply created money for all its spending, inflation would follow, as many countries have shown.

**Why not just print enough money to pay off the national debt?**
The money would flood into the economy and push prices up, eroding everyone's savings. Also, much of the debt is held as savings by pension funds, banks and families, so paying it off with new money would turn their safe assets into cash during an inflation.

**Is the dollar going to collapse?**
Nobody can promise anything, but this project's October 2026 view gives the dollar system about a 62% chance of continuing to extend its role through 2028, with the chance of a disorderly reset rising over longer horizons. Currencies at the top usually decline slowly, over decades, not overnight.

**Is my money safe in the bank?**
In the United States, deposits are insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Below that limit, your deposit is very safe. Above it, you are trusting the bank's own finances.

**Should I buy gold?**
This guide is not financial advice. Gold is best understood as insurance against a loss of trust in money and its managers. It pays no income and can fall for long stretches, as it did from 1980 to 2001. Many people who hold it keep it as a modest slice, not their whole savings.

**Is Bitcoin money?**
It works as a medium of exchange in some places and as a store of value for some holders, but it is too volatile to be a good unit of account. Today it acts mostly as an asset held outside the banking system, not as everyday money.

**Why does inflation exist at all? Could we have zero?**
Most central banks aim for about 2% because mild inflation makes wage adjustments easier, leaves room to cut rates and keeps the economy away from deflation traps. Zero is possible but risky, because a small shock can tip it into deflation.

**Who decides interest rates?**
The Fed sets a short-term rate for overnight lending between banks. Longer-term rates, such as mortgage rates, are set by markets based on inflation expectations, government borrowing and demand for safe assets.

**Why does the US have so much debt and still borrow cheaply?**
Because the world needs dollars and safe dollar assets, US Treasuries have a large built-in demand. That demand is not unlimited, which is why yields rose in 2026 as debt grew and some foreign buyers sold.

**What happens if China sells all its Treasuries?**
Yields would rise and the dollar might weaken, but China would also lose money on its sales and push up its own currency, hurting its exports. This project rates a fire sale as very unlikely. China has instead reduced its holdings gradually, from about $694 billion in February 2026 to about $591 billion in August.

**Will stablecoins replace banks?**
They are replacing some ways money moves, not the lending that banks do. Stablecoins cannot lend or pay interest by law, and they are backed by Treasury bills. Loans against your future income still come from regulated lenders.

**Can the US just revalue its gold to pay the debt?**
Revaluing its gold to market price would book about $1.07 trillion, roughly one year of net interest, once. The deficit recurs every year, so it would not solve the problem.

**If interest is never created, how can all loans be repaid?**
Interest is paid out of money that circulates. When lenders spend their interest income, it returns to borrowers through wages and sales. The real risk is debt growing faster than incomes, or everyone trying to repay at the same moment.

**Why do house prices rise faster than inflation?**
House prices are mostly land prices in expensive cities, and land cannot be made. Low interest rates, limited building and zoning rules push prices up, while the consumer price index counts rents, not purchase prices.

**Will AI and robots make everything free?**
They are already making many goods cheaper in hours of work. But housing, land, health care and attention are not getting cheaper, and this project's research found that robots can meet only part of any extra demand while housing supply grows about 1% a year.

**What is the single most useful habit from this guide?**
Own something productive from your first paycheck, and borrow only what you can carry through a bad season. Those two rules protect you in almost every season of money.

**Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?**
A bond's payments are fixed. When new bonds pay more, an old bond only sells if its price drops until its fixed payments give the same yield. See the bonds chapter.

**Why does China or Japan lend so much to the US instead of spending it at home?**
To keep their currencies cheap for exporters, to hold insurance against a dollar shortage, and because US markets are deeper and safer than their own. Their households pay part of the cost. See the leaderboard chapter.

**If a money gains value over time, why would anyone spend it?**
People still need things now, and waiting has a cost. In practice they spend the money that is losing value and keep the one that is gaining, which is Gresham's law. See the deflation debate chapter.

**My local inflation is low. Why do I feel poorer when I travel or pay overseas fees?**
Because your home currency may have weakened against the currencies those bills are priced in. Low home inflation measures only the home ruler. See the chapter on the view from a smaller currency.

**If the world is so rich, why do so many people feel locked out?**
Because most of that wealth is the price of assets that were owned long ago, revalued upward by falling interest rates, while newcomers must buy in at today's prices with credit. See the chapter on the titled world.

**Is tax a scam?**
No. Tax is the fee for the board itself: the money, the courts that protect your title and the security around them. But who pays the fee and how well it is spent are fair questions. See the Nation Inc. chapter.

**Why do bond prices fall when rates rise? I still find it confusing.**
Picture an apartment with a locked rent. If rents in town rise, a buyer will not pay full price for your low locked rent. A bond's coupon is that locked rent. Try the slider in the bonds chapter.

**If my bond pays 5%, am I really 5% richer each year?**
No. 5% is the nominal yield. Take off tax and inflation and the real gain may be closer to 1% or 2%, or even below zero. Try the second slider in the bonds chapter.

**Why are countries full of minerals often poor?**
Because the value is mostly captured at the refining, manufacturing and financial steps, and because easy resource money weakens institutions. See the geography section of the leaderboard chapter.

**Is crypto money?**
It depends on the family. Stablecoins work as money but are really dollars. Bitcoin is a candidate store of value but is rarely spent or used to price things. Most other tokens are not money at all. See the crypto chapter and its six questions.

**If tokens get good enough, will we still need the dollar to measure prices?**
Only if debts, wages and taxes start being written in a token. Until then every token is converted to dollars somewhere along the chain, and markets keep rebuilding one hub to price everything through. See the owner's question in the crypto chapter.

**Is money only created when banks make loans?**
No. Banks also create deposits when they buy securities, the Fed creates them when it buys from nonbanks, and Treasury spending adds them while taxes and auctions take them away. See the chapter on who really creates money.
