# Why empires and their currencies fall

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 10. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-09.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-09 . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*No money has ruled forever. The ones that fell usually fell the same way: war, debt and a slow watering down of the promise.*

The dollar has been the world's main money for about a century. Before it, other currencies held that role and lost it. This chapter walks through the most famous cases, looks for the common pattern, and then asks honestly what is different today. It is the chapter where humility matters most, because history rhymes but never repeats exactly.

### Rome: the long watering down

Chapter 2 told the story of the denarius. Rome's army and borders were enormously expensive. As conquest slowed, new silver stopped arriving, but the bills kept coming. Emperors filled the gap by debasing the coin. By the late 200s AD the coinage had collapsed, prices had soared and the state increasingly collected taxes in goods rather than in its own worthless coins.

> The story has a twist that few people know. The emperor Constantine introduced a gold coin, the solidus, in the early 300s. In the eastern half of the empire, which became Byzantium, the solidus kept its gold content for roughly 700 years, until it was debased in the 1000s. It was the dollar of the medieval Mediterranean. A money can stay honest for centuries when the state that issues it chooses to keep it so.

### Spain: rich in silver, poor in the end

In 1545 Spanish colonizers found a mountain of silver at Potosi, in today's Bolivia. Silver from Potosi and Mexico flooded into Spain for over a century, and the Spanish silver coin, the piece of eight, became a world money used from China to the American colonies.

Yet the Spanish crown went bankrupt again and again. Philip II stopped payments to his bankers in 1557, 1560, 1575 and 1596. The silver was spent on wars across Europe and on imports, and it flowed out as fast as it came in. Prices across Europe rose several-fold over the 1500s, an episode called the price revolution. Historians still debate how much of that inflation came from American silver and how much from population growth. What is clear is that easy money without a productive base did not make Spain lastingly strong.

### The Dutch: the first modern financial center

In the 1600s the small Dutch Republic became the trading and financial center of Europe. Amsterdam created the Dutch East India Company in 1602, one of the first companies with tradable shares, and the Bank of Amsterdam in 1609, whose bank money was trusted across Europe. Dutch guilders and bills of exchange financed world trade.

Decline came through a mix of costly wars with England and France, rising debt, and loss of trading edge as Britain grew. A disastrous war with Britain from 1780 to 1784 hit hard. It later emerged that the Bank of Amsterdam had secretly lent heavily to the struggling East India Company and the city, which undermined trust in its money. The company was dissolved in 1799 and the bank was wound down in 1819.

### Britain: the pound sterling

Britain built the most successful money of the 1800s. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, and the gold standard of Chapter 2 made sterling the currency of world trade and London the world's banker. Britain carried enormous debt after the Napoleonic Wars, more than twice its economy, but the Industrial Revolution grew the economy fast enough to carry it. High debt alone did not sink Britain.

Two world wars did. Britain sold overseas investments and borrowed heavily from the United States to fight. After 1945 it was a large debtor with a weakened industry. The decline of sterling then took decades: a convertibility crisis in 1947, American financial pressure during the Suez crisis of 1956, a devaluation in 1967, and a loan from the IMF in 1976. The economist Barry Eichengreen showed that the dollar actually overtook sterling as a reserve currency as early as the mid-1920s, fell back in the 1930s, and only pulled clearly ahead after the Second World War. Changes at the top are slow and messy, not sudden.

### The common pattern

*[Picture on the web page: A common pattern in past declines: rising power and trust, a peak with a trusted currency and financial center, then war costs, rising debt, debasement or inflation, and a rival taking the lead. A framework, not a law.]*

1. A rising power wins trust through productive strength, trade and sound money.
2. Its currency becomes the world's preferred money, and its capital becomes the financial center. Borrowing becomes cheap.
3. Cheap borrowing funds wars, overseas commitments and consumption. Debt rises faster than the productive base.
4. To pay the bills, the state debases its coin or inflates its paper, and trust starts to fade.
5. A rival with stronger production offers an alternative. The center of money moves, usually after a great war.

A popular chart, often shown by investment firms, lists the leading currencies of the last 600 years with lifespans of roughly 80 to 110 years each: Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, France, Britain and the United States. Treat it with care. Historians argue about whether reserve currency even means much before the 1800s, the dates are rough, and as the sterling story shows, top currencies often share the stage for decades. The chart is a conversation starter, not a countdown clock.

### Dalio's big cycle: one framework

The investor Ray Dalio, in his 2021 book Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order, describes a big cycle of empires. He tracks measures such as education, innovation, competitiveness, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, financial center strength and reserve currency status. In his account, empires rise as education and innovation improve, peak when their currency and financial center dominate, and decline as debts mount, money is printed, wealth gaps widen, internal conflict grows and a rival rises.

The framework is useful because it makes you look at many measures at once. It is not a law of nature. Critics point out that the sample of empires is small, that the stages are easier to see in hindsight than in real time, and that very different histories get squeezed into one shape. Use it as a checklist for questions, not a calendar for predictions.

> "If something cannot go on forever, it will stop." (Herbert Stein, economist and adviser to Presidents Nixon and Ford, 1980s)

### What looks familiar now

- Interest now costs more than defense. In fiscal year 2026 the US paid about $1,052 billion in net interest against about $965 billion for national defense, the third year in a row. The historian Niall Ferguson has argued that a great power spending more on debt service than on defense is a warning sign.
- Debt rising faster than the economy. US debt held by the public is about 100% of GDP, and the deficit was about $1.99 trillion in 2026.
- Allies hedging. Foreign official holdings of Treasuries at the New York Fed fell about $254 billion from February to October 2026, and central banks bought about 863 tonnes of gold in 2025.
- A tax base leaning on wages. About 86% of federal receipts in 2026 came from individual income and payroll taxes, while machines and capital are taking a larger share of income.

### What is genuinely different

- No ready successor. The euro has no single government bond market and no single treasury behind it. China keeps controls on money leaving the country, which limits the yuan as a reserve. This project's research found that rivals' strongest cards today are in hardware, like rare earth magnets, batteries and solar technology, not in currency.
- US debt is in its own currency. Spain owed its bankers in silver it could not create. The US owes dollars it can create. It is unlikely to default outright. The risk is inflation and higher interest rates, which is a slower, quieter kind of decline.
- Digital rails may extend the dollar's reach. Most of the growth in dollar stablecoins went to holders outside the US, spreading dollar use rather than shrinking it.
- Network effects are global and instant. Payments, oil, loans and contracts all run on dollar plumbing, and switching costs are enormous.

**Check yourself.** Q: What do Rome, Spain and Britain have in common in the decline of their money?
A: Each faced war costs and debts larger than their productive base could carry, leaned on debasement, borrowing or inflation to pay, and saw trust and leadership shift to a stronger rival.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why is Dalio's big cycle a framework rather than a law?
A: It is based on a small number of historical cases, its stages are clearer in hindsight than in real time, and very different histories are fitted to one shape. It is useful for asking questions, not for predicting dates.
