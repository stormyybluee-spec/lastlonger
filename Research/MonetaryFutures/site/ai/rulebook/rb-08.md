# The seasons of money

Rulebook Level 1 (Foundations), chapter 08. Stack: Fundamentals. Id: rb-08.

Web page: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/#rb-08 . Text built 2026-11-12. Guide for AI readers: https://neetmonetary.netlify.app/llms.txt

*Money has weather. In some decades it melts, in others it hardens, and the same choice can make you rich in one season and poor in the next.*

So far we have looked at how money is made. Now we look at how it behaves over time. Prices do not move in a straight line. They move in long seasons, some inflationary, some deflationary. Most people live through only two or three seasons, so they assume the one they grew up in is normal. The rulebook exists so you do not make that mistake.

### Two kinds of weather

- Inflation: prices in general rise, so each unit of money buys less. Debts get lighter in real terms. Savings in cash shrink in real terms.
- Deflation: prices in general fall, so each unit of money buys more. Debts get heavier in real terms. Cash gains real value, but jobs and incomes usually suffer.

> **Picture this.** Think of money as ice. In a warm season, inflation, ice held in your hand melts, so you want to turn it into something that does not melt, like a house or a tool, and owing ice to someone is cheap because it shrinks while you owe it. In a cold season, deflation, ice keeps its shape and even grows, so holding ice is smart and owing ice is dangerous because your debt grows heavier.

### A timeline of seasons, 1870 to 2026

*[Picture on the web page: Seasons of money from 1870 to 2026: inflationary eras (wars, the 1970s, 2021 onward) and deflationary or disinflationary eras (1873 to 1896, 1929 to 1933, 1982 to 2020).]*

| Years | Season | Main cause | Who tended to win | Who tended to lose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1873 to 1896 | Long deflation | Gold standard, money growth slower than output | Lenders, savers | Farmers and other debtors |
| 1897 to 1914 | Mild inflation | New gold discoveries | Debtors, farmers | Fixed-income holders, slightly |
| 1914 to 1920 | War inflation | War spending, gold suspended | Debtors, governments | Savers, bondholders |
| 1929 to 1933 | Deflationary collapse | Bank failures, money supply fell about a third | Holders of safe cash | Debtors, workers, banks |
| 1941 to 1951 | War and postwar inflation with capped rates | War debt, Fed held rates down | The government as debtor | Savers in bonds and deposits |
| 1966 to 1981 | The Great Inflation | Deficits, oil shocks, loose policy, no gold anchor | Fixed-rate borrowers, owners of gold and real assets | Savers, bondholders, fixed incomes |
| 1982 to 2007 | Disinflation, long bond summer | Volcker's credibility, globalization | Bondholders, stock owners | Late borrowers at high fixed rates |
| 2008 to 2020 | Low inflation, zero rates | Debt overhang after 2008, cautious banks | Asset owners | Savers earning nearly nothing |
| 2021 to 2023 | Inflation surge | Pandemic spending, supply shocks, energy | Borrowers with low fixed mortgages | Renters, savers, long bond holders |
| 2024 to 2026 | Sticky, energy-led inflation | Oil shock, deficits, AI investment boom | Owners of energy and real assets | Wage earners whose pay lags prices |

The last row comes from this project's 2026 data. US consumer energy prices rose about 20.4% over a year, oil traded near $98 a barrel in October, and a key inflation measure ran at 5.0% annualized in the second quarter. Real hourly pay fell about 0.7% in the year to September 2026. The Fed under Kevin Warsh raised rates in September 2026, against the President's wishes.

> Financial repression is the polite name for a quiet trick governments use to shrink debt. They keep interest rates below inflation, so lenders to the government lose real value every year. Economists Carmen Reinhart and Belen Sbrancia showed in 2011 that the US and UK used this after the Second World War to cut their debt burdens substantially. A mild version is happening now: the average interest rate on US government debt is about 3.2 to 3.5%, while nominal income has grown about 5.7% a year over the past decade, so inflation is quietly shrinking the debt relative to the economy.

### Why governments prefer mild inflation

- It shrinks debt. A government that owes trillions benefits when each future dollar is worth a little less.
- Wages are sticky downward. People resist pay cuts. With mild inflation, a business can let real wages adjust by giving smaller raises instead of cutting pay, which keeps more people employed.
- It leaves room to cut rates. If inflation is 2% and rates are 4%, a central bank can cut rates enough to make borrowing cheap in real terms during a slump. With zero inflation, there is less room.
- Deflation is scarier. Once people expect falling prices, they delay buying, firms delay hiring, and debts grow heavier. That spiral is hard to stop.

New Zealand adopted the first formal inflation target in 1990. The Federal Reserve formally adopted a 2% target in 2012. The owner calls inflation a circulation incentive, a leaking bucket that pushes people to spend or invest rather than hoard cash. That is a fair description of how a mild target works, and it is also why holding idle cash is a slow loss in most modern seasons.

> "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." (Milton Friedman, 1963)

Friedman's line is famous and partly true. Over long periods, sustained high inflation does come with fast money growth. But most economists today add that supply shocks, like the oil embargoes of the 1970s or the 2026 energy shock, can drive prices up for a while even without fast money growth, and that expectations matter a great deal. Where scholars disagree is how much weight to give each.

### Deflation traps

The United States from 1929 to 1933 is the classic trap. About 9,000 banks failed. The money supply fell by about a third, as Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz documented. Prices fell by about a quarter. Unemployment reached about 25%. Fisher's debt deflation, from Chapter 7, ran at full speed.

Japan is the modern case. After its stock and property bubble burst around 1990, banks were slow to admit losses, households and firms paid down debt for years, and prices drifted slightly downward for much of the period from the late 1990s to the early 2010s. The Nikkei stock index did not regain its 1989 peak until 2024. The economist Richard Koo called this a balance sheet recession: everyone repaying debt at once, so nobody borrows to spend.

### A store of value changes jobs with the season

Here is a subtle point that most people never notice. An asset is not simply safe or risky. It is a tool that works in some seasons and fails in others.

| Asset | Works as a store of value in | Fails in | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and savings accounts | Deflation | Inflation | Cash gained real value in 1930 to 1933, lost it in the 1970s |
| Long-term government bonds | Disinflation and deflation | Inflation and rising rates | Great from 1982 to 2020, lost close to half their market value from 2020 to 2023 |
| Gold | Inflation, crisis and loss of trust | Disinflation with high real rates | Rose from $35 to $850 by January 1980, then fell to around $255 by 1999 to 2001 |
| Fixed-rate debt (as a borrower) | Inflation | Deflation | A 3% mortgage from 2021 became a gift as prices rose |
| Stocks of strong companies | Moderate inflation and growth | Deep deflation and very high real rates | Fell sharply in 1929 to 1932 and in 1973 to 1974 |

> Gold is usually called an inflation hedge, but from 1980 to 2001, a period that still had inflation, gold lost most of its value in real terms. What gold really hedges is a loss of trust in the money or the people managing it. When trust is high and real interest rates are high, gold tends to struggle. When trust falls, gold shines. In 2025 central banks bought about 863 tonnes of gold, a sign that some governments trust the system less.

- **Myth:** There is one safe asset you can buy and forget.
- **Reality:** Safety depends on the season. Long-term US government bonds are free of default risk, but holders lost close to half their market value from 2020 to 2023 as rates rose. Cash is safe from crashes but loses value to inflation. The most reliable protection is to understand which season you are in and to avoid betting everything on one season continuing.

**Check yourself.** Q: You have a fixed-rate mortgage and inflation jumps to 8%. Are you better or worse off in real terms?
A: Better off as a borrower, as long as your income keeps up. Your fixed payments and remaining debt shrink in real terms because each dollar you repay buys less.

**Check yourself.** Q: Why did Japan's long stagnation happen even though interest rates were near zero?
A: Households, firms and banks were focused on repairing their balance sheets after the 1990 bust, so they paid down debt instead of borrowing to spend. Cheap money did not help when nobody wanted to borrow.
