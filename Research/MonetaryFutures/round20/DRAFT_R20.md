## A hawkish Fed, cheaper oil and a first scorecard that beat the market
Between 23 October and 26 November the Fed held its rate at 3.75% to 4.00% on 28 October, but two of the twelve voters wanted a hike. The minutes of 18 November say almost all participants expect more tightening, and prediction markets put a 25bp hike on 9 December at 92% to 94%, as reported on 25 November. Growth was strong (Q3 real GDP 4.3% at an annual rate on the second estimate) and core PCE inflation rose to 3.3% on a year in October. The 10-year Treasury yield fell from 5.13% to 4.91% (24 November), but the fall was in expected inflation. The real 10-year yield stayed near 2.8%, still above real growth of 2.3%.
The energy shock eased. Brent oil fell about 20% in November to about $92 a barrel, as reported, and the International Energy Agency now sees a supply surplus this quarter. Gold fell about 14% to $3,557 an ounce on 24 November, yet central banks bought 321 tonnes in the third quarter, up 42% on a year. The Treasury kept its auction sizes, leaned on bills and kept buying back old bonds. Foreign official holders kept selling Treasuries, down $335B since February, while private buyers in financial centres took up the supply. Japan let the yen touch 160 and relied on warnings, with November's intervention figure still to come. China and the US rolled their export-control truce to 10 January 2027.
For the core question, 'Will energy replace money?', the answer stands. Energy is not becoming money you spend, but it is becoming the collateral behind new dollar credit. The month made both halves clearer. Oil lost a fifth of its price in weeks, a reminder that energy is a poor store of value. At the same time, borrowing for AI and data centres kept growing. By one estimate, big cloud firms have sold about $296B of bonds in 2026, and the Fed's own minutes now name opaque AI financing as a risk. The first strain showed on 5 to 6 November, when the Nasdaq 100 fell about 5.9%, as reported.
The desk's first scorecard is in. On the 37 predictions that fell due, the desk's Brier score is 0.117 (0 is perfect, and always saying 50% scores 0.25). On the 12 with a market price, the desk scored 0.095 against the market's 0.115. The biggest misses were on political timetables and on the strength of gold buying. The scenario odds stay at 62/15/1/22 for 2028 until the court reviews them. This page is general education, not financial advice.

### [R20-2] (confirmed, 85) The Fed held in October. Is a December hike now the base case?
Yes, by the Fed's own account. On 28 October the FOMC held the federal funds target range at 3.75% to 4.00% by 10 votes to 2. Governor Lisa D. Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth M. Hammack dissented, both preferring a 25bp hike. The minutes, released on 18 November, say almost all participants judged further hikes likely to be appropriate if the economy moves as expected. A couple said a move larger than 25bp could be considered in December if inflation data disappoint.

PCE prices rose 3.8% on a year in October, and 3.3% without food and energy (core). Unemployment fell to 4.1% in October, with payrolls up 165k. Prediction markets priced a 25bp hike on 9 December at 94% (Polymarket) and 92% (Kalshi), as reported on 25 November. November CPI comes out on 10 December, the day after the decision.
One change to the Fed's calendar was reported. Chair Warsh said on 28 October that one of the eight 2027 meetings and two in 2028 will be strategy meetings with no rate vote, as reported by wire services. No Fed document with the new calendar has been found yet.

### [R20-3] (confirmed, 80) Did real interest rates fall when the 10-year yield fell?
No. The 10-year Treasury yield fell from 5.13% on 21 October to 4.91% on 24 November, with a low of 4.84% on 5 November and a high of 5.15% on 13 November (Fed H.15 data). But the 10-year breakeven, the market's measure of expected inflation, fell from 2.37% to 2.12%. The real 10-year yield, on inflation-protected bonds, rose a little, from 2.76% to 2.79%, with a high of 2.92% on 16 November.
So the cost of money after inflation stayed high. Real GDP grew 2.3% over the year to Q3, so the real 10-year yield is about 0.5 points above real growth. The Desk's tripwire on this gap stays breached. Markets seem to expect the Fed to win against inflation, which lowers expected inflation but keeps real rates up.
The push from Japan eased. Japan's 10-year government bond yield fell from 2.914% on 22 October to 2.713% on 25 November (Ministry of Finance data), while its 30-year yield held near 4%. The US 2-year yield fell from 4.81% to 4.63%.

Not in this window. The October minutes say reserve management purchases stay at zero, with the pace reviewed each month. ## [r20-treasury] The Treasury kept its plan, and private buyers kept replacing official ones
Coupon sizes held, bills stay the flex, and buybacks continue. The debt passed $40.79T and interest takes 19.8% of receipts. Foreign official holders sold another $81B in September, while custody centres bought.

### [R20-5] (confirmed, 85) Did the Treasury change its borrowing plan at the November refunding?
Only slightly. On 4 November the Treasury announced a $125B refunding: $58B of 3-year notes, $42B of 10-year notes and $25B of 30-year bonds, raising about $28B of new cash. Coupon auction sizes stayed the same. The guidance now says Treasury expects to keep them 'for the next several quarters'. The words 'at least' were dropped, which leaves room for increases later.
Bills stay the flexible part. Treasury cited strong demand for bills and plans larger bill sizes across the curve in January. Buybacks continue: up to $70B of liquidity-support buybacks and up to $25B of cash-management buybacks from November to January. From March 2027 the 7-year note moves from a new issue each month to a new issue each quarter with two reopenings. No new tenor, century bond or fee on foreign holders was announced.
Privately held net borrowing for October to December is estimated at $618B, about $10B below the August estimate, and at $800B for January to March 2027, as reported. The cash balance is assumed at $850B at the end of December.

### [R20-6] (confirmed, 85) How fast are the debt and the interest bill growing?
Total public debt reached $40.79T on 24 November, up $349B from $40.44T on 22 October (Treasury, Debt to the Penny). The October deficit was $404.1B against $284.3B a year earlier, which press reports call the largest October on record. Receipts fell 8.8% on a year. Customs duties fell from $31.4B to $19.2B, which the press links to tariff refunds after the Supreme Court's February 2026 tariff decision and to a high base a year earlier.
Net interest was $1,064.0B over the 12 months to October, against $994.1B for defense (computed from the Monthly Treasury Statement). Interest now takes 19.8% of federal receipts, up from 19.5%. The Desk's tripwire for fiscal dominance is 25%.
The average interest rate on marketable debt rose to 3.570% at the end of October, from 3.518% at the end of September. Nominal GDP grew 6.0% over the year to Q3, so growth still beats the average cost of debt. That is why the debt ratio drifts up only slowly despite large deficits.

## [r20-reserves] Gold fell but central banks bought more, Japan held back, and China rolled its pause
Gold fell about 14%, yet central banks bought 321 tonnes in Q3. Japan let the yen touch 160 and relied on warnings. China and the US moved their cliff date to 10 January 2027. Emergency dollar lines sat almost unused.

### [R20-8] (confirmed, 80) Gold fell sharply. Did central banks stop buying?
No. Spot gold was $3,556.82 an ounce on 24 November (CNBC, based on Reuters), about 14% below the roughly $4,130 implied by the Desk's figures on 22 October. The fall came as markets priced a December Fed hike and real yields stayed near 2.8%.
Yet the World Gold Council's Q3 report (3 November) shows central banks bought 321.2 tonnes, up 42% on a year, the strongest official quarter in a year. Only about 120 tonnes were reported by the banks themselves. The named buyers were the People's Bank of China (63t), Poland (40t), Kazakhstan (14t), Uzbekistan (14t) and the Czech Republic (5t). Russia was the only sizeable seller (19t). The UAE made a small reduction, so Round 5's list of current buyers should drop it. The PBoC added about 26 tonnes in October, its 24th straight monthly rise, as reported from official reserve data.
The buyers are still central banks without a standing Fed swap line. US gold, still booked at $42.22 an ounce (about $11B), is worth about $930B at the 24 November price, down from about $1.08T.

## [r20-energy] Oil fell by a fifth, AI borrowing grew, and the core answer got clearer
Brent fell from about $110 to about $92 as supply returned. AI investment kept growing on borrowed money, and the first market strain appeared. Stablecoins shrank while banks built their own rails.

### [R20-12] (plausible, 70) Is the oil shock over?
It eased, but it is not over. Brent crude was about $110 a barrel on 2 November, as reported. It fell about 16% in the week to 6 November, the steepest weekly fall since 2020, and settled at $92.57 on 9 November and $92.44 on 16 November (Reuters). Around 19 to 20 November it was about $92, down about 20% in November, as reported. The International Energy Agency now sees a supply surplus of 1.7 million barrels a day in the fourth quarter, as reported from its 13 November report.
Supply came back faster than expected. Seven core OPEC+ members kept their December targets unchanged on 1 November, and the full group meets on 29 November to set 2027 quotas. Flows through the Strait of Hormuz are recovering but fragile. Claims that tankers hit mines on 15 to 16 November could not be verified by Reuters, and US and Iran talks through Qatar are unresolved. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is at its lowest since October 1982 (Reuters).
So the Desk's line 'Brent near $110, Hormuz still mostly closed' is out of date. The oil route to a disorderly reset has weakened for now. But euro area inflation was still 3.8% in September, as reported, and the ECB is still expected to hike.

### [R20-13] (plausible, 70) Is AI investment still growing, and who is paying for it?
It is still growing, and more of it is paid for with debt. NVIDIA reported revenue of $115.8B for the quarter to 25 October, up 103% on a year, with $107.6B from data centres. ### [R20-14] (plausible, 65) Does this month change the answer to 'Will energy replace money?'
No. It makes the answer clearer. The Desk's answer has two parts. Energy will not become money you spend, because it is perishable, tied to a place, and its price swings. Energy will matter as the real limit on output and as collateral for new dollar credit. November tested the first part: Brent lost about a fifth of its price in a month. A coin pegged to a barrel of oil would have lost value just as fast.
The second part grew. Borrowing behind data centres and chips is now in the hundreds of billions of dollars a year, by one estimate $296B of cloud-firm bonds in 2026. Most of it is in dollars, though Alphabet's EUR 6B bond shows the euro can also price watts. Power is still the scarce input. In its December 2025 auction the PJM grid bought 134,479 MW for 2027/28, short of its reliability requirement, with data centres a large part of the rise in demand, as reported by PJM. Texas grid operator ERCOT received about 198 GW of large-load requests in Q1 2026, by one report.
The Desk's confidence in this answer stays at 65. One new risk is worth naming. If AI debt runs into trouble, the collateral layer the answer depends on would shrink, at least for a time.

### [R20-15] (plausible, 70) Are stablecoins and new payment rails still growing?
The coins shrank, but the rails kept being built. Total stablecoin supply was $288.87B on 16 November, from $305B on 21 October (DefiLlama data). Tether's USDT was $175.33B and USDC $69.24B. ## [r20-europe] France's budget stalls, the ECB leans toward a hike, and Malaysia holds steady
The French Assembly rejected the budget's revenue part 458 to 0, but the spread fell to about 148bp. The ECB held and markets price a December hike. Bank Negara held at 2.75% as the ringgit weakened.

### [R20-16] (plausible, 70) France still has no 2027 budget. How close is a crisis?
Closer to a slow grind than a crisis. On 4 November the National Assembly rejected the revenue part of the 2027 budget by 458 votes to 0. The amended text would have raised the public deficit from 5.0% to 5.9% of GDP, according to Budget Minister David Amiel, a figure other parties dispute. The government's text went to the Senate, which studies it from 20 November to 9 December. After 17 December the government can bring the budget into force by ordinance. It can also use Article 49.3.
Rating agencies waited. Moody's kept France at Aa3 with a negative outlook on 23 October. Fitch moved its outlook to negative on 30 October, as reported. S&P, which rates France A+, reviews it on 27 November.
Bond markets calmed. The French 10-year yield was 4.74% on 26 November and the German 3.26%, a spread of about 148bp, down from a peak of about 174bp on 22 October by one report. The French yield fell below 4.8% on 23 November after President Macron signalled he could write fiscal discipline into the constitution. The Desk's 175bp tripwire came close in October but was not crossed.

### [R20-18] (plausible, 70) What does a hawkish Fed mean for Malaysia?
So far, a weaker ringgit but steady policy at home. Bank Negara Malaysia kept the Overnight Policy Rate at 2.75% on 5 November, its eighth meeting without change. It expects growth above 5% in 2026. Headline inflation averaged 1.8% and core 1.9% so far this year, and the bank expects both to rise in 2027. It said it will judge any move in 2027 on the data.

October inflation rose to 1.9% from 1.8%, as reported, with the electricity, gas and fuel group up 4.7%.
