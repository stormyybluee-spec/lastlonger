## L3.4 Why the peasants stayed poor: money, output and the missing chain (id rb-peasant)

Kicker: For most of history most people were poor, and stayed poor for centuries. A reader says the cause was a missing chain: money, credit, a strong state, schools and knowledge, each needing promises about the future to get started. This chapter tests that chain link by link.

[0 tldr]
- More money did not by itself make more output. Spain got the most silver and fell behind. China had paper money eight hundred years before Europe's factories and did not build them first.
- The reader is right that a whole chain was missing. Old states collected only a few percent of what their people made, most people could not read, and books were rare.
- Promises about the future did pay for states, wars, canals and railways. They did not pay for the first factories, which were small and built from profits and family savings.
- The loop was real. Places that pulled ahead, such as Italy, Holland and China, stalled or fell back. What finally broke the loop in England is still argued, and no leading account says it was money.

[1 bridge] Built on earlier chapters: recall these before you start
- rb-credit: Chapter 02 found that old poverty came more from scarce output than from scarce coins. This chapter asks why output was scarce.
- rb-1971: Chapter 03 found that gold was a loose limit on lending, and that elastic money is not unlimited.
- rb-02: Rulers watered down coins long before paper money.
- rb-07: Debt is a promise. It is light when what it paid for grows, and heavy when it does not.

[2 callout] (How to read this chapter) A scene, four tests and a verdict, about 40 minutes in all. Each test ends with a short line on what it does to the claim. This is the second of seven deeper chapters that follow the overview in chapter 02.


### The claim on trial

[4 p] Chapter 02 said the peasants of old kingdoms were poor mainly because output was scarce, not because coins were scarce. The reader who wrote in accepts that, and pushes back. Output was scarce for a reason, the reader says. Here is the reply in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. Output was scarce because the cycle of money and credit that creates output was scarce.
- Part 2. The other links of the chain were missing too: a central state, schools, and shared knowledge.
- Part 3. Every link needs large promises about the future before it can start. Armies, schools, roads and factories are paid for long before they pay back.
- Part 4. A country that started and was stopped by war, famine or old habit fell back into a loop, and did not progress as a whole.

[6 p] This is a bigger idea than the one in chapter 02, and a better one. It deserves a careful test.


### The kingdom, counted

[8 p] In 1688 an English official named Gregory King sat down to count his whole country, family by family. He was a careful man, and his table is one of the first of its kind. Near the bottom he listed 400,000 families of cottagers and paupers, about 1.3 million people. Each family lived on about six and a half pounds a year.

[9 p] Then King did something cold. He drew a line across his table. Above it were the families who added to the wealth of the kingdom. Below it were 849,000 families who, in his words, were "decreasing the wealth of the kingdom". They spent more than they earned. More than half the families in one of the richest countries on earth could not cover a year of their own living.

[10 p] And England was lucky. Across the Channel, French famines between 1693 and 1710 killed over two million people. In the world as a whole, about 43 of every 100 children born in 1800 died before they were five. In Britain it was about 33.

[11 p] That is the world the claim is about. Why did it last so long?


### Test 1. Did places with more money make more?

[13 p] If scarce money was what held output back, then places that got a flood of money, or invented new kinds of it, should have pulled ahead and stayed ahead. History ran that experiment several times.

[14 table]
| Place | The money it had | What happened to output per person |
|---|---|---|
| Spain, 1500s | First call on the silver of the Americas | About 1,118 dollars in 1500, 1,295 in 1600 and 1,009 in 1650, in today's money. It rose a little and then fell below where it began |
| England, 1600 to 1650 | Coins per person rose 2.7 times | Fell 14% |
| China, from 1023 | State paper money, centuries before Europe | In 1090 China was richer per person than England. It later fell far behind. Under the Mongol rulers paper money lost value at about 11% a year from 1260 to 1290, and by 1368 people were back to barter |
| Holland, 1600s | The cheapest credit in Europe. Its government borrowed at 4% when the English king paid 6% to 30% | In 1650 it was about three times as rich per person as England. Then it stopped growing for 150 years |

[15 p] So four places had more money or cheaper credit than their neighbours. One fell back, one shrank, one printed its way to ruin, and one stalled at the top.

[16 p] There is evidence on the other side too. The historian Nuno Palma finds that American silver did help England in a quieter way. It let more people buy and sell with coins. In the 1520s about 40% of English households lived on wages. By the 1780s few households were outside the cash economy. Coins did not make the cloth, but they made it easier to sell. And when Europe ran short of silver in the 1400s, several historians find that trade suffered.

[17 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds only weakly. Money and credit help trade to move, and a shortage hurts. But a flood of money, or very cheap credit, did not make a country productive. Spain, China and Holland each had what the claim says was missing.


### Test 2. Were the other links missing?

[19 p] Here the reader is on firm ground. Take the links one at a time.

[20 p] The state. A modern government collects a large share of what its people make, and spends it on roads, courts, schools and soldiers. Old kings could not.

[21 table]
| Where and when | Tax collected by the central state |
|---|---|
| England, 1500 | About 2.2% of what the country made |
| England, 1600 | About 1.7% |
| Most European states, 1500s | Under 5% |
| Britain and the Netherlands, 1780s | Over 10% |
| The Ottoman Empire | Per person, less than three days of a labourer's pay each year |

[22 p] A state that small can barely keep the roads safe, let alone build a school in every village.

[23 p] Schooling. In 1820 about 12% of the world's people could read. In 1870 about 17% of the world's children were in primary school. By 1950 it was 57%, and by 1970 it was 72%. The reader's own figure, that a child has had about a 70% chance of going to school since the 1970s, is close to the record. Malaysia is reported to have gone from about half its children in primary school in 1955 to nearly all by 1960.

[24 p] Knowledge. Before printing, every book was copied by hand. In Britain about 208,000 books were printed in the fifty years to 1499. In the next fifty years it was about 2.8 million, thirteen times as many.

[25 p] So the links were missing, as the claim says. But the order in which they arrived is a surprise.

[26 table]
| Link | When it arrived in England | Before or after incomes took off? |
|---|---|---|
| Printing and cheap books | From about 1500 | Long before |
| A state that could tax | From the mid 1600s. By the 1700s England raised twice as much tax per person as France | Before |
| Reading | About 53% could read in 1650 and 54% in 1750. No change in a century when incomes were rising | Flat during the rise |
| Schools for every child | 39% of children in primary school in 1870, 98% in 1900 | After. A century after the first factories |

[27 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 holds. A whole chain was missing, and the reader names the right links. One correction: schools for everyone came after the take-off in the first country, not before it. The first factories were built by a people of whom nearly half could not read.


### Test 3. Does every link need promises from the future?

[29 p] This is the heart of the claim. Did the links get built on borrowed money, on promises to pay later? Look at who actually borrowed.

[30 p] The state borrowed, on a scale never seen before. In 1688 the English government owed about 1 million pounds. Nine years of war later it owed nearly 17 million, about 40% of everything the country made in a year. By the end of the wars with Napoleon the debt was about 180% of a year's output. That is the reader's picture exactly: enormous promises about the future. But almost all of it paid for war.

[31 p] The factories did not borrow much. This is the fact that surprises most people. The first cotton mills were small. One cost between 1,000 and 3,000 pounds to start. A famous Manchester firm, M'Connel and Kennedy, began with about 1,700 pounds. Mill owners mostly paid their way from profits, family savings and credit from the merchants they dealt with. The law even held private lending down. From 1714 no lender could charge more than 5% interest, and some historians find that war borrowing by the state left less for everyone else.

[32 p] The big networks did borrow, and here the record is mixed. Canals and railways cost too much for any family.

[33 table]
| A promise that was kept | Promises that were broken |
|---|---|
| New York built the Erie Canal from 1817 with borrowed money. Before it borrowed, it set aside taxes on salt and on auctions to pay the interest. The canal earned well and the debt was repaid easily | Other American states copied the idea without the pledged taxes. In 1841 and 1842 eight states and the territory of Florida stopped paying their debts |
| Japan is reported to have borrowed 1 million pounds in London in 1870, at 9% interest, for its first railway. The line from Tokyo to Yokohama opened in 1872 | Indiana sold over 10 million dollars of bonds for canals and roads. Only about 5.5 million reached the building sites, and work stopped in 1839 |

[34 p] So a promise worked when something real was pledged behind it. A tax, a canal that earns tolls, a railway that sells tickets. A promise with nothing behind it did not.

[35 p] The reader's idea has serious company among economists. The "big push" theory says that one factory alone may fail in a poor country, because nobody nearby earns enough to buy what it makes, while many factories started together can succeed. Starting many things at once takes money up front. The economic historian Alexander Gerschenkron argued that countries that came late, such as Germany and Russia, leaned on big banks or on the state to do exactly this. Neither idea fits Britain, the first case. Both fit late developers better.

[36 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds in part. States and big networks were built on promises about the future. The first factories were not. And a promise worked only when something was pledged behind it. For countries that came late, the reader's picture fits better than it fits the first one.

[37 check] Q: A friend says the Industrial Revolution was built on bank loans. What would you tell them?
A: The first cotton mills cost about 1,000 to 3,000 pounds each and were mostly paid for from profits and family savings. The huge borrowing of that age was by the government, and it paid for war. Borrowing mattered more later, for canals and railways, and for countries that industrialised after Britain.


### Test 4. Was there a loop?

[39 p] The reader says that a country stopped halfway fell back, and went round again. The record agrees. The clearest case is England after the Black Death.

[40 table]
| England | People | What a worker's pay could buy (an index) |
|---|---|---|
| 1347, before the plague |  | 26 |
| 1350, after it |  | 52 |
| 1450 | 1.9 million | 70 |
| 1650 | 5.3 million | 29 |

[41 p] The plague killed so many that workers became scarce and their pay doubled. For a century the survivors' families lived better than any peasants before them. Then the people came back. By 1650 there were almost three times as many mouths, and a worker's pay bought no more than before the plague. Every gain had been eaten. This is the trap that Thomas Malthus described in 1798.

[42 p] Whole countries went round the same way.

[43 table]
| An early leader | Its peak | What followed |
|---|---|---|
| Northern Italy | The richest part of Europe in 1450 | Output per person fell by about a quarter by 1600 |
| Holland | Three times as rich as England in 1650 | No growth per person from 1600 to 1750 |
| China | Richer than England in 1090 | Output per person fell 43% from 1700 to 1820 |

[44 p] And yet the loop was not perfectly closed, even in England. While a day's pay was falling, the country's total output per person was slowly rising: about 1,159 dollars in 1300 and 1,446 in 1650, in today's money. People worked more days and more of them worked in trades. Then something changed.

[45 table]
| England and Britain | Output per person, in today's dollars |
|---|---|
| 1650 | 1,446 |
| 1700 | 2,412 |
| 1800 | 3,343 |

[46 p] What broke the loop? Scholars have argued for two hundred years, and each has part of the evidence.

[47 table]
| The explanation | Who | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| People | Thomas Malthus, Gregory Clark | More food meant more people until the land could feed no more. Something had to break that link |
| Rules | Douglass North and Barry Weingast | After 1688 the English king could no longer seize property or cancel his debts, so people dared to invest. Critics answer that interest rates stayed high for forty years after |
| Prices | Robert Allen | English wages were high and coal was cheap, so it paid to invent machines that saved workers and burned coal |
| Knowledge | Joel Mokyr | A culture grew up that treated useful knowledge as something to be shared, tested and improved |
| The state | John Brewer, Mark Dincecco | A state that could tax and borrow could keep order and protect trade |
| Finance | Peter Rousseau and Richard Sylla | Banks and markets helped to start growth in several countries. Critics answer that English law held private credit down |

[48 p] Read down that list. Money appears once, in the last row, and its own critics are strong. But read it again and the reader's larger point survives. Nearly every scholar names a different link, and each link leaned on the others. A state needs taxes, taxes need trade, trade needs safe roads and coins, machines need knowledge, knowledge needs books. The reader's chain is a fair picture of how the pieces fit.

[49 p] The break was not kind to everyone who lived through it. In 1832 a woman of 23 named Elizabeth Bentley told a committee of Parliament about her childhood. She had started work in a flax mill in Leeds at the age of six. When the mill was busy she worked from five in the morning until nine at night, and children who came late were strapped.

[50 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 holds as a description. Loops were real, in wages and in whole countries. What broke the loop in England is still argued. The leading accounts point to people, rules, prices, knowledge and the state. None of them says the missing piece was money.


### The verdict on the chain

[52 table]
| The claim | Finding | The fact that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Output was scarce because money and credit were scarce | Holds only weakly | Spain, China and Holland had the most money or the cheapest credit, and each stalled or fell back |
| 2. The other links were missing too | Holds | English kings collected about 2% of output in 1500. About 12% of the world could read in 1820 |
| 3. Every link needs promises from the future | Holds in part | The state's debt went from 1 million to 17 million pounds in nine years. A cotton mill cost 1,000 to 3,000 pounds, from savings |
| 4. A country stopped halfway fell into a loop | Holds as a description. The cause of the escape is argued | A worker's pay doubled after the plague and was back where it began by 1650 |

[53 p] Here is the claim again, rebuilt so the record can carry it.

[54 steps]
- The peasants stayed poor because a whole chain was missing: a state that could tax and keep order, knowledge that could spread, and ways to make more with the same hands. Money and credit were one link, and not the one that was most often missing.
- Where money or cheap credit arrived without the other links, as in Spain, it raised prices or paid for wars. Where the other links were in place, money helped trade to move.
- Promises about the future built the strong state and the great networks, the canals and railways. They worked when something real was pledged behind them. The first factories were built from savings.
- Countries that came later, after the first had shown the way, leaned much more on banks and on the state. For them the reader's picture is closer to the truth.

[55 p] That last line matters for the chapters to come. The reader's claim is weakest as a story about England in 1700 and strongest as a story about a country trying to catch up in the 1900s. Malaysia is one of those. The next chapters test the claim where it is strongest.

[56 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that the early leaders stalled because money or credit ran short, and not for other reasons. Second, that Britain's first factories depended on bank credit after all. Third, that countries with deeper credit markets before 1800 grew faster than their neighbours, which China and Holland suggest they did not.

[58 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Two views this chapter could not test
[58.0 p] Some scholars explain why parts of the Middle East fell behind Europe by pointing to law and politics. Timur Kuran argues that rules on partnerships and inheritance kept firms small. Jared Rubin argues that rulers who drew their authority from religious leaders were slow to allow printing and lending at interest. Both are careful to say the cause was a set of rules and political bargains, not a faith. This chapter did not test either view.

[58.1 p] Deirdre McCloskey argues that secure property existed in China, India and much of Europe for centuries before 1800, so rules alone cannot explain the take-off. She points instead to a change in how people talked about trade and invention. That too is untested here.
