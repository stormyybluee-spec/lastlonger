# R15B: arguments and human detail for H1 to H4

Lane: R15B, round 15, chapter rb-peasant. Date of work: 2026-11-16.
General education, not financial advice. Each claim carries a status: "verified" (source opened and seen), "secondary" (a reliable summary seen, not the full text), or "memory" (not checked, must not be printed).
The owner's point 3 is treated as four hypotheses (H1 to H4 in FRAME_R15.md). This file looks for evidence against them as hard as for them.

## Part 1. The main explanations of the long poverty and the take-off

### 1.3 High wages and cheap energy (Robert C. Allen, 2009)

Claim. Britain before 1800 had high wages and cheap coal. So it paid British firms, and only British firms, to invent machines that swapped energy and capital for labour. Once the machines were improved they spread to low-wage places too.

Best evidence. Allen's "welfare ratio" divides a labourer's yearly earnings by the cost of a family's basic basket. London and Amsterdam stay high through the early modern period, while Florence and Vienna slip to about one or below in the 1700s, and Beijing and Delhi are similarly low (Allen 2013 restatement, Figure 1). In silver, an English spinner earned about 2.9 grams a day against 2.1 grams in France in the 1780s, with English prices only 4% higher (same paper, note 3).

Best objection. Jane Humphries argues the first cotton mills ran on cheap women's and children's labour, not on dear labour, and that Allen's baskets had too few calories. Allen accepts the calorie point (raising the basket to 2,100 calories a person) but says the ranking of countries does not change. Others note that high wages also existed in the Dutch Republic, which did not industrialise first.

Sources.
- Allen, "The High Wage Economy and the Industrial Revolution: A Restatement", Oxford Discussion Paper 115, June 2013. https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/Economics/History/Paper115/allen115.pdf (verified)
- Allen, "The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective", Cambridge University Press, 2009 (secondary, via reviews: https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/943/print)

### 1.2 Institutions and secure property (North and Weingast 1989, Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2001 and 2005) and the critics

Critics, first findings. Sussman and Yafeh (Journal of Economic History, 2006, vol. 66, pp. 906 to 935) find that British interest rates stayed high and volatile for four decades after 1688, moved with Dutch rates, that British debt per head stayed below Holland's until about 1780, and that Britain did not borrow more cheaply than European states with weaker property rights. Their conclusion is that in the short run markets did not reward the reforms. https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v66y2006i04p906-935_00.html (verified, abstract)

### 1.6 Finance and credit, first findings

Temin and Voth, "Prometheus Shackled: Goldsmith Banks and England's Financial Revolution after 1700" (Oxford University Press, 2013). Using the ledgers of Hoare's Bank and other goldsmith banks, they argue that usury laws limited lending and that wartime government borrowing crowded out private loans. In 1714 the legal ceiling was cut from 6% to 5% and Hoare's rationed its lending. A reviewer notes the loan-book analysis ends in 1724, before the take-off. https://academic.oup.com/book/7462 (secondary, publisher abstract and review summaries)

### 1.2 (continued) Institutions and secure property, full statement

Claim (North and Weingast 1989). After 1688 Parliament controlled the purse and the crown could no longer seize wealth or default at will. Because the state's promises became believable, lenders would lend it far more, at lower rates, and private property and markets grew in that shelter.

Best evidence. In the nine years from 1688 to 1697 English government debt grew from about 1 million pounds (2 to 3% of GNP) to nearly 17 million (about 40% of GNP). By 1720 it was over fifty times the 1688 level and "on the order of GNP". Before 1688 Holland borrowed long term at 4% while the English crown paid "between 6 and 30 percent" for short loans. Source: North and Weingast, Journal of Economic History 49(4), 1989, pp. 803 to 832, pages 822 to 823. https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-my/wp-content/uploads/sites/138/2016/12/14091657/NorthWeingast-1989-Constitutions-and-Commitment.pdf (verified)

Claim (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2005). The rise of Western Europe after 1500 was mainly the rise of Atlantic traders. Where the monarch was already somewhat checked before 1500, profits from Atlantic trade made merchants strong enough to win secure property rights, and those rights drove later growth. Source: "The Rise of Europe: Atlantic Trade, Institutional Change, and Economic Growth", American Economic Review 95(3), 2005, pp. 546 to 579. https://www.nber.org/papers/w9378.pdf (secondary, abstract)

Best objections.
- Sussman and Yafeh (2006): the markets did not price in the reform for about forty years (see above).
- Deirdre McCloskey ("Bourgeois Dignity", University of Chicago Press, 2010, chapters 33 to 37) argues that much of Europe, China and India had "credible commitments to secure property rights in the thirteenth century CE", and that China "has had secure property in land and in commercial goods for millennia". A cause that was present for centuries without a take-off cannot explain why the take-off came when it did. Her own explanation is a change in how society spoke about and honoured trade and innovation. Source: McCloskey, "Why Neo-Institutionalism Can't Explain the Modern World: A Pamphlet". https://www.deirdremccloskey.com/docs/AgainstNorth.pdf (verified). Critics of McCloskey: Jared Rubin's EH.net review says she does not explain where the change in attitudes came from (https://eh.net/book_reviews/bourgeois-dignity-why-economics-cant-explain-the-modern-world/, secondary).
- The great jump in debt was spent on war, not on roads or factories. Temin and Voth find it crowded private borrowers out (see 1.6).

### 1.5 State capacity and the fiscal-military state (Brewer 1989, Besley and Persson 2009 and 2011, Dincecco 2009 and 2011)

Claim. Before a market can grow, a state must be able to tax its whole territory, keep order and enforce contracts. John Brewer ("The Sinews of Power", 1989) showed that eighteenth-century Britain, often thought of as a light state, in fact taxed heavily through an efficient excise service and so could borrow and fight. Besley and Persson model "fiscal capacity" and "legal capacity" as investments that states make when they expect wars or common interests. Dincecco shows that states which first centralised taxes and then limited the ruler raised far more revenue.

Best evidence. In a panel of 11 European states with 1,773 yearly observations from 1650 to 1913, centralised and limited regimes raised significantly more revenue per head (Dincecco, Journal of Economic History 69(1), 2009, pp. 48 to 103). English revenue per head averaged 7.50 grams of gold from 1700 to 1788, more than double the French 3.71 grams. Dincecco and Katz estimate this gap alone was linked with English yearly growth per head 0.08 to 0.11 points higher, when French growth averaged only 0.19% a year. Sources: https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v69y2009i01p48-103_00.html (secondary, abstract) and Dincecco and Katz, "State Capacity and Long-Run Economic Performance", MPRA 50042, 2013, https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/50042/1/MPRA_paper_50042.pdf (verified)

Best objection. A strong taxing state is not enough: Qing China, Mughal India and the Ottoman empire kept order across huge areas for long periods, and France had a large state, yet none took off first. Much of the extra British revenue went to war and debt interest. In Dincecco's own account, centralisation on the continent came largely from French conquest between 1789 and 1815, well after Britain's take-off had begun, so for most of Europe it followed rather than led. The cross-country regressions show association, and reverse causation (richer economies can be taxed more) is hard to rule out.

### 1.1 The trap of population (Thomas Malthus 1798, Gregory Clark 2007)

Claim. Before 1800 new techniques came so slowly that any rise in income let more children survive, and the extra mouths pushed income back down. So income per head had no upward trend for thousands of years, whatever the rulers, the money or the religion. Only when productivity growth became faster than population could grow did the trap break, in England around 1800.

Best evidence. Clark writes that "the average inhabitant in the world of 1800 was no better off than the average person of 100,000 BC", that life expectancy at birth in 1800 was 30 to 35 years, about the same as for foragers, and that across pre-industrial societies the number of surviving children per woman stayed close to two (2.005 on average in his calculation). From English wills he finds the richest men left about twice as many surviving children as the poorest. Source: Clark, "A Farewell to Alms", Princeton University Press 2007, draft chapter 1 (2006), https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/FTA2006-1.pdf (verified)

Clark's account of the escape. Clark argues that because the better-off had more surviving children, many of their children moved down the social ladder, and with them spread habits such as saving, patience and long hours of work. This is Clark's claim. It is one of the most disputed parts of the book (see objection).

Best objections.
- E. A. Wrigley (Population and Development Review 34(4), 2008, pp. 784 to 788) notes that Malthus himself later gave up the rigid version. In Northwestern Europe, people married late or not at all when times were bad, so fertility fell and living standards could stay well above bare need. Wrigley finds the evidence for the spread of middle-class habits "slim", since it rests on wills from one Suffolk archdeaconry in the early 1600s. https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/Farewell%20to%20Alms/Wrigley%20Review.pdf (verified)
- New estimates of GDP per head (Broadberry and others, Maddison Project) show slow but real growth before 1800 in England and Holland, which a strict trap would not allow (see lane R15A for the series).
- For the owner's thesis this cuts both ways. The trap explains poverty without any reference to money or credit (against H1). It also gives a ready mechanism for H4: gains are eaten up by population growth.

### 1.4 Useful knowledge and a culture of improvement (Joel Mokyr, "The Gifts of Athena" 2002, "A Culture of Growth" 2016)

Claim. Growth before 1800 kept running out of steam because techniques rested on narrow know-how that was not written down, tested or shared. In the 1600s and 1700s a cross-border community of scholars (the "Republic of Letters") came to believe that knowledge of nature should be open, tested and put to use. Mokyr calls the link between science and the workshop the "Industrial Enlightenment". It made invention cumulative, so it no longer stalled.

Best evidence. Mokyr's own evidence is mostly qualitative (societies, journals, the careers of engineers and instrument makers). The best numerical test is by Squicciarini and Voigtlander (Quarterly Journal of Economics 130(4), 2015). From the list of more than 8,000 subscribers to the Encyclopedie, they measure "upper-tail knowledge" in almost 200 French cities. Subscriber density predicts city growth after French industry took off, while average literacy does not. In their VoxEU column they report that soldiers' height is linked to subscriptions in 1830 but not before 1750, which argues against reverse causation. Sources: https://www.nber.org/papers/w20219 (secondary, abstract) and https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/knowledge-elites-enlightenment-and-industrialisation (secondary)

Best objection. Enrico Spolaore (NBER Working Paper 26061, July 2019) accepts much of the account but asks what culture is and how it can be measured apart from the institutions and incomes it lives in. https://www.nber.org/papers/w26061 (verified, abstract). Others note the timing problem in reverse: the Enlightenment was European-wide, yet Britain industrialised first, and many key early inventions (the water frame, the spinning mule) came from craftsmen with little science. Geoffrey Hodgson (Journal of Institutional Economics, 2022) questions how Mokyr separates culture from institutions (secondary).

Relevance to H2. This is the closest scholarly support for the owner's "little knowledge or public information" link. It places the knowledge link before the take-off, but it is an elite story, not a schooling story. The Squicciarini and Voigtlander result is evidence against mass literacy as the trigger.

### 1.6 Finance and credit: did a financial revolution come first?

Claim, for (P. G. M. Dickson 1967, Rousseau and Sylla 2003). Dickson's "The Financial Revolution in England" (1967) showed how, after 1688, England built a market in long-term, Parliament-backed public debt, with the Bank of England (1694) and a stock market in London. Rousseau and Sylla generalise: in the Dutch Republic, England, the United States, France, Germany and Japan, a modern financial system (sound public debt, a central bank, banks, securities markets) appeared first and "helped jump-start growth". In a 17-country panel from 1850 to 1997 they find a strong link between finance and growth, strongest in the 80 years before the Great Depression, and they suggest growth "might indeed have been finance-led". Source: Rousseau and Sylla, NBER Working Paper 8323 (2001), published in Bordo, Taylor and Williamson (eds), "Globalization in Historical Perspective", 2003, pp. 373 to 416. https://www.nber.org/papers/w8323 (secondary, abstract). Dickson 1967 (memory, not opened).

Claim, against (Temin and Voth 2013). The financial revolution was a revolution in lending to the state, not to industry. Usury laws capped private rates (6% until 1714, then 5%), so when war pushed up the yield on government debt, banks rationed private loans. In their reading, credit was shackled, not released. Source: https://academic.oup.com/book/7462 (secondary)

How the first factories were in fact financed. Most scholars agree that British firms relied mainly on internal funds for long-term fixed investment. Francois Crouzet (1965) wrote that the pioneers did not meet "insurmountable problems in raising the long-term capital they needed". Start-up sums were small: about 1,000 to 2,000 pounds for a small cotton works and over 3,000 pounds for a large Arkwright-type mill (Honeyman 1982). M'Connel and Kennedy of Manchester began in 1791 with about 1,700 pounds, and its net worth grew almost 13-fold between 1796 and 1810 at an average 19% return on capital, mostly by reinvesting profits. Total fixed capital in cotton was about 1.9 million pounds in 1788 (Chapman and Butt 1988). Mokyr (1985) and Chapman argue the other side: that weak capital markets kept firms small and raised bankruptcy, so "the rate of profit set a ceiling on the rate of accumulation". Source: Harvey S. James Jr., "British Industrialization and the Profit Constraint Hypothesis", working paper, January 1996, https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/eh/papers/9612/9612003.pdf (verified, it quotes Crouzet, Mokyr and Chapman)

Short-term credit did matter. Merchants' trade credit, country banks and bills of exchange paid for raw cotton, wages and stock. This is a point for H3 in a weak form: factories needed working credit, but their buildings and machines were mostly not paid for by large long-term promises. (secondary, general agreement in the literature cited by James 1996)

### 1.7 Money supply: monetarist readings of pre-modern stagnation

Claim, the bullion famine (John Day 1978, Peter Spufford, John Munro). In the late Middle Ages, and worst in the mid-1400s, Europe ran short of silver and gold coin. Silver flowed east to pay for spices and silks faster than European mines could replace it, and people hoarded coin in uncertain times. Mints closed, prices fell and trade shrank. Munro wrote that his work "to a considerable extent" supports the monetarist views of Day and Spufford. Sources: Day, "The Great Bullion Famine of the Fifteenth Century", Past and Present 79, May 1978 (secondary, bibliographic). Munro's own summary, https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/munro5/Munro'sContributionsEcHist.pdf (secondary, search summary)

Claim, American silver and English growth (Nuno Palma 2019 and 2022). Slow but steady growth in early modern England was partly driven by more money. Silver and gold from the Americas raised the money stock, which lowered the cost of trade, drew people into the market and into towns, made taxes easier to collect and helped the state build fiscal capacity. In England about 40% of households lived mainly on wages in 1524 to 1525, a share that had been about constant since the 1200s, but by the early 1780s few people were outside the cash economy. Palma quotes David Hume: since the American mines, "industry has increased in all the nations of Europe, except in the possessors of those mines". In a related paper (Review of Economic Studies 89(3), 2022) Palma uses the American discoveries as a natural experiment and finds that the monetary injection had real effects on output. Sources: Palma, "Money and Modernization in Early Modern England", Manchester discussion paper EDP-1903, January 2019 (Financial History Review), https://hummedia.manchester.ac.uk/schools/soss/economics/discussionpapers/EDP-1903.pdf (verified). Palma 2022, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:restud:v:89:y:2022:i:3:p:1593-1627 (secondary)

Best objections.
- The receivers of the metal did worst. Spain for the whole period, and Portugal after about 1700, got the silver and gold first and fell behind. Palma himself names this problem and points to "Dutch disease" and the resource curse (Hamilton, Drelichman). This is the strongest evidence against a simple reading of H1: more money in hand did not make more output.
- Opponents of the bullion-famine view (Carlo Cipolla, Nathan Sussman) argue that coin shortages were local and short, and that credit, bills of exchange and changes in the speed at which coin changed hands could make up for missing metal. Munro names them as the main opponents. (secondary)
- Velocity and credit. Even in the bullion-famine story, the problem is the means of payment, not a missing "large promise" about the future. Money supply can explain swings in prices and trade but has trouble explaining a permanent rise in output per head.
- Most of Europe received the same silver, but only England and Holland grew strongly, so money looks at most like a helping condition.

### 1.8 The owner's own idea, stated fairly, and the nearest scholars

The owner's idea, fairly put. The links of a growing economy (money and credit, a taxing state, schooling, shared knowledge, armies, roads, factories) feed each other, so none pays off alone. Each one costs a great deal before it pays back, so each needs a large promise about the future (a loan, a bond, a tax pledge) to start. A society that cannot make or keep such promises, or is knocked off course halfway, falls back.

Nearest scholar 1: the "big push" (Paul Rosenstein-Rodan 1943, Murphy, Shleifer and Vishny 1989). Rosenstein-Rodan argued that in a poor country no single factory pays, because its workers cannot buy enough of its own goods, but many factories started together create each other's customers. Murphy, Shleifer and Vishny turned this into a formal model with two stable outcomes, a poor one and an industrial one. Their three mechanisms are: factory wages raise demand for other factories' goods, investment today shifts demand into the future, and shared infrastructure such as a railway spreads its fixed cost over many firms. Source: Journal of Political Economy 97(5), 1989, pp. 1003 to 1026, NBER Working Paper 2708. https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v97y1989i5p1003-26.html (secondary, abstract). This is very close to the owner's "each chain feeds the others" and "large threshold". The model does not say the push must be paid for by credit, but the railway mechanism (a large fixed cost paid before the users exist) is the owner's "promise about the future" in a precise form.

Nearest scholar 2: Alexander Gerschenkron ("Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective", 1962). The further behind a country was, the bigger the jump it needed, and the more it relied on special institutions to gather capital: large investment banks in Germany, and the state itself in Russia. This is close to the owner's idea that the start needs central backing. Sources: Fishlow's review of the book, https://lburlamaqui.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Fishlow-A-2003.-Review-of-Gerschenkron-1962.pdf (secondary, not opened)

Nearest scholar 3: the finance-led view of Rousseau and Sylla (see 1.6), and the state-capacity view in which the fiscal and legal capacity of a state are joint investments that reinforce each other (Besley and Persson, "Pillars of Prosperity", 2011, memory).

Best objections.
- Britain, the first case, had no big push. Its industrial revolution was slow, spread over many decades, led by many small firms that mostly grew from their own profits (see 1.6). National income grew by about 1 to 2% a year (lane R15A has the series). The big push is a theory of late developers, not of the first one.
- Tests of Gerschenkron are mixed. Later work finds German universal banks had limited effects on industrial investment and growth, and firms linked to banks did about as well as those without (Burhop 2006, cited in search summaries, secondary). For Italy, Federico and Toniolo found German-style banks played little role. For Russia, Olga Crisp argued that newer evidence was "highly inconsistent" with Gerschenkron's account.
- Coordination failure needs no credit at all to be solved. A big trading market (Britain's Atlantic trade, the Allen story) can supply the demand that a big push would otherwise have to create.

## Part 2. H3. Promises about the future, concretely

### 2.1 Canals: the Erie Canal (worked) and the state canal defaults of 1841 to 1842 (failed)

Worked. New York began the Erie Canal in 1817 with uncertain prospects. It pledged two taxes to the debt from the start, auction duties and a salt tax. In 1824 these raised 290,000 dollars against canal bond interest of 350,000 dollars, and tolls on the half-built canal came in above forecast. The surplus went into a sinking fund (the Canal Fund) and the state "was easily able to service and redeem its debts". Cost at opening in October 1825 was about 7.1 million dollars (popular sources, secondary). Source: Wallis, Sylla and Grinath, "Sovereign Debt and Repudiation: The Emerging-Market Debt Crisis in the U.S. States, 1839-1843", NBER Working Paper 10753, September 2004, p. 9. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10753/w10753.pdf (verified)

Failed. Other states copied New York without its tax pledge. US states borrowed more than 200 million dollars in the quarter century after 1815, and state debt outstanding was 198 million dollars in 1841. In 1841 and 1842 eight states and the Territory of Florida defaulted. Indiana's "Mammoth" bill of January 1836 authorised 10 million dollars of borrowing for canals and a railway. By 1839 it had issued more than 10 million in bonds but received only about 5.55 million for the works, because about 3 million had been sold on credit to the Morris Canal and Banking Company, which failed to pay in August 1839. By the autumn of 1839 all construction in the state stopped. Pennsylvania borrowed to pay interest instead of raising taxes. The authors' key finding: in every case of repudiation the debt was linked to state borrowing for banks, and most states had not expected their canals to earn much by 1841. Same source (verified)

Lesson for H3. The promise worked where it was backed by a tax pledged in advance and failed where the plan was to borrow now and let future tolls pay. Half-built works earned nothing, which matches the owner's "stopped halfway" image well.

### 2.2 Railways: British railway mania (built, but many investors ruined)

In the 1840s British investors poured money into railway shares. Andrew Odlyzko calls the mania "by many measures the greatest technology mania in history" and finds private investors put in more than twice as much into construction as earlier estimates implied. A 2008 talk by Odlyzko estimates that about a third of the investment was lost to investors. Railway investment at the peak (about 1847) is put at around 7% of GDP in a secondary repost, which must be checked before printing. The network that was built carried Britain's trade for a century. Sources: Odlyzko, "Collective hallucinations and inefficient markets: The British Railway Mania of the 1840s", preliminary version, January 2010, https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/hallucinations.pdf (secondary, search summary only, the server failed when fetched). Lesson: a promise about the future can build something useful while ruining the people who made it.

### 2.3 Public debt and bubbles: England 1688 to 1720 (worked), France and the Mississippi scheme 1720 (failed)

Worked. English government debt rose from about 1 million pounds in 1688 to nearly 17 million in 1697 (about 40% of GNP) without inflationary finance, and to about GNP by 1720, backed by taxes voted by Parliament and earmarked for interest. North and Weingast 1989, pp. 822 to 823 (verified). But it was spent mainly on war against France, and on Temin and Voth's evidence it crowded out private credit (see 1.6).

Failed. In 1719 to 1720 John Law's Mississippi Company in France and the South Sea Company in Britain both offered to swap government debt for company shares. Both share prices rose very fast and then collapsed in 1720, the South Sea shares in September. Larry Neal describes the two as the first international stock-market boom and bust. Scholars note the French collapse had severe and long-lasting effects (Law's bank had printed notes to hold up the share price), while the British one was much smaller. Neal notes Law himself lost over 500,000 pounds by the end of August 1720. Sources: Neal, "The Rise of Financial Capitalism", Cambridge University Press 1990, chapter 4, https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/rise-of-financial-capitalism/banque-royale-and-the-south-sea-company-how-the-bubbles-began/440F42627398D9AF09BF714F27B1655B (secondary, abstract). Neal, "I am not master of events", UC Riverside seminar paper, 17 November 2008, https://webarchive.ucr.edu/economics.ucr.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Neal.pdf (verified, abstract). Many historians link the French failure to French distrust of banks and paper money for decades after (memory, needs a source).

### 2.4 States that borrowed abroad to modernise: Meiji Japan (worked) and Egypt under Ismail (failed)

Worked. Japan's new government raised a 9% loan of 1 million pounds in London in 1870, linked to building its first railway, Shimbashi (Tokyo) to Yokohama, which opened on 14 October 1872. In 1875 its public debt was about 5.1 million pounds at home (about half bearing no interest) and only about 1.1 million pounds abroad. In 1876 the state turned the samurai stipends into bonds worth about 174 million yen, a domestic promise about the future that ended a large yearly charge on the budget. Japan borrowed little abroad until the 1890s and did not default. Sources: LSE thesis U050350, https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/132230/1/U050350.pdf (secondary, search summary, file too large to open). Britannica, "The emergence of modern Japan", https://www.britannica.com/place/Empire-of-Japan/The-emergence-of-modern-Japan (secondary). "Borrowed little abroad until the 1890s" is memory and must be checked.

Failed. Khedive Ismail borrowed heavily in Europe in the 1860s and 1870s for the Suez Canal, railways, irrigation, palaces and war. Loans were sold far below face value. The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica says the cash that reached the treasury "was far less than the nominal amount" and that no loan cost less than 12% a year. Totals differ by source, from about 68 million pounds of funded debt to about 94 million with the floating debt. In 1875 Ismail sold Egypt's Suez Canal shares to the British government. In April 1876 payments were suspended and in May 1876 the Caisse de la Dette Publique was set up, under which foreign commissioners took charge of much of Egypt's revenue. British occupation followed in 1882. Sources: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Caisse-de-la-Dette-Publique (secondary). Debt totals: unverified and conflicting, not to be printed until checked in a modern history (for example David Landes, "Bankers and Pashas", 1958).

Lesson for H3. In both cases a state paid in advance for railways and other works. The difference was the cost of the promise and what backed it. Japan borrowed little, mostly at home, and built a tax base first (the land tax reform of 1873, memory). Egypt borrowed a great deal abroad at high real rates, and the promise ended in the loss of control over its own finances.

What the cases say about H4. Each case did stall, and war or plague is part of most of the stories, which fits H4. But in at least three of the four (Song China, the Dutch Republic, northern Italy) money and credit were plentiful by the standards of their time. The stall therefore cannot be put down to a lack of credit alone. The explanations most often given are population pressure, the cost of war, the loss of markets, the lack of cheap energy and, in the scholars' accounts of the Middle East, legal and political rules.

## Part 4. Evidence against the owner's hypotheses that must not be left out

1. Britain's first factories were financed mostly from the owners' own and their families' savings and reinvested profits, plus short-term trade credit, not from large long-term borrowing. Start-up sums were 1,000 to 3,000 pounds. James (1996), quoting Crouzet (1965) (verified). This weakens H3 for the factory link.
2. The financial revolution after 1688 financed war. On Temin and Voth's evidence government borrowing crowded out private lending, and the usury cap of 5% after 1714 forced banks to ration credit (secondary). On Sussman and Yafeh's evidence markets did not reward the new institutions with lower rates for about forty years (verified abstract). So large promises about the future existed in Britain, but they did not obviously go to the industrial link.
3. Growth took off under metal money. Britain was in effect on gold from 1717 and legally from 1821, with paper money not convertible from 1797 to 1821. The take-off happened while money was tied to a scarce metal. This is a direct problem for the owner's point 2 and for H1 (memory on the dates, to be checked in lane R15A).
4. China had paper money from 1023, deep commercial credit and large markets centuries before Europe, and did not industrialise first. By the early 1400s the Ming had in practice abandoned paper money and moved to silver (von Glahn 2025, verified).
5. Spain and Portugal received the most American silver and gold and fell behind. Philip II of Spain borrowed heavily against silver revenues and stopped payments to his bankers four times (Drelichman and Voth, Economic Journal 2011, https://www.jvoth.com/papers/lending-to-the-borrower-from-hell.html, secondary). Palma (2019, verified) and Hume (1752, quoted by Palma) both note that the owners of the mines did worst.
6. The Dutch Republic had the cheapest credit and deepest capital market in Europe around 1700 and stalled while Britain, with dearer credit, took off (Part 3).
7. The Malthusian account (Clark) explains the long poverty with no monetary cause at all, and the knowledge account (Mokyr, Squicciarini and Voigtlander) puts the trigger in elite knowledge, not in mass schooling or credit.
8. Squicciarini and Voigtlander find that average literacy did not predict French city growth after industrialisation, while elite knowledge did. This is evidence against the schooling link as a trigger in H2 (secondary).

## Evidence for and against H1 to H4

For.
- H1 and H2: English state debt rose from about 1 million to nearly 17 million pounds in nine years after 1688 (North and Weingast), England taxed twice as much per head as France (Dincecco and Katz), and American silver raised English monetisation and market use (Palma). Money, credit and the state's capacity all grew before and during the take-off.
- H2: Upper-tail knowledge predicts French industrial growth (Squicciarini and Voigtlander), and Mokyr places a culture of shared useful knowledge before 1800.
- H3: Canals, railways and modern states were in fact paid for in advance with long-term borrowing (Erie Canal, British railways, Meiji Japan). The big push theory (Rosenstein-Rodan, Murphy, Shleifer and Vishny) states the owner's "threshold" idea in formal terms.
- H4: Indiana's works stopped halfway in 1839 and earned nothing. Song China, northern Italy and the Dutch Republic all stalled after an early lead, often after war or plague. The Malthusian trap gives a mechanism for falling back.

Against.
- H1: Spain and Portugal had the most silver and gold and fell behind. China had paper money and credit centuries early. The Dutch had the cheapest credit around 1700 and stalled. Britain took off under metal money.
- H2: Strong states (France, Qing China) did not take off first. Mass literacy did not predict French growth. Much of the gain in British state capacity went to war.
- H3: The first factories were small and financed mainly from profits and family savings, not big promises. The public debt after 1688 went to war and, on one reading, crowded out private credit. Many promises failed (Mississippi 1720, eight US states in 1841 to 1842, Egypt 1876).
- H4: The stalled leaders were not short of money or credit, so the loop, where it existed, ran through population, war, energy and markets rather than through missing promises alone.
