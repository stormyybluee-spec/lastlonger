### RT-02. Test 1 does not admit the other causes, and Holland is told backwards (blocks 14, 15, 17, 52)

What is wrong. Each case has an obvious rival cause that the chapter never names in Test 1. Spain spent its silver on long wars. England 1600 to 1650 includes the Civil War of the 1640s. The Chinese paper money collapse came at the end of Mongol rule, in a time of rebellion. Holland stalled after wars with France and England from about 1670, with a heavy public debt. A fair test has to say this, because it cuts both ways: it means these cases do not prove money was useless either.

Holland is also told backwards. It had the cheapest credit and it rose to be about three times as rich as England by 1650 (the draft's own figure). That fits the reader's claim. The stall came later. The chapter counts Holland only as a failure.

There is a second fairness problem. The reader's Part 1 is about a "cycle of money and credit". A flood of silver coin into Spain is not the same thing as a working credit system. The test is closer to "more metal, more output", and it should say so.

Evidence. Dutch boom ended about 1670 with wars against France and England and a later debt overhang (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_Netherlands_(1500%E2%80%931815)). De Vries and van der Woude ask why growth stopped after 1650 or 1670, and Riley disputed that it stopped at all (same page, and the Utrecht introduction at https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/386202/The_Political_Economy_of_the_Dutch_Republic_Introduction_.pdf?sequence=1).

Fix 1, block 15.
OLD: So four places had more money or cheaper credit than their neighbours. One fell back, one shrank, one printed its way to ruin, and one stalled at the top.
NEW: So four places had more money or cheaper credit than their neighbours. Spain fell back. England's output fell. Chinese paper money collapsed at the end of Mongol rule. Holland rose to the top and then stalled. But be fair to the reader. Each case had another big cause as well. Spain fought long wars. England fought a civil war in the 1640s. The Mongol state was falling apart. Holland fought France and England and carried a heavy debt. So these cases show that money alone was not enough. They do not show that money did not matter.

Fix 2, block 17.
OLD: Spain, China and Holland each had what the claim says was missing.
NEW: Spain, China and Holland each had plenty of money or cheap credit, and each later stalled. Holland is the hardest case. It grew rich while its credit was cheap, which fits the claim, and stalled only later, in years of war and heavy debt.

Fix 3, block 52, table cell.
OLD: Spain, China and Holland had the most money or the cheapest credit, and each stalled or fell back
NEW: Spain, China and Holland had plenty of money or cheap credit. Holland grew with it, but each one later stalled, often in years of war

### RT-03. China: "printed its way to ruin" is unfair to its monetary history (blocks 14, 15, tldr)

What is wrong. The table jumps from 1023 to the Mongol collapse. It leaves out that paper money worked for long periods. A Hoover study finds that under the Yuan the price level was stable for over half a century, with hyperinflation only in the last two decades. That sits badly with the draft's "about 11% a year from 1260 to 1290", which needs a named source. The early Ming note of 1375 was the one with no backing and no limit, and it lost most of its value within decades. Then, from about 1550, China took in huge amounts of Japanese and American silver, and historians link this to a fast commercial boom in the late Ming. That is a "more money" case with a different result, and the chapter leaves it out.

Evidence. https://www.hoover.org/research/first-precious-metal-standard-monetary-policy-paper-money-under-mongols-china and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Ming_Treasure_Note. Silver and late Ming commercial growth: von Glahn, https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D3045EA55D654DCDCDCB3874E481A0B4/S0212610919000193a.pdf/changing_significance_of_latin_american_silver_in_the_chinese_economy_16th19th_centuries.pdf

Fix, block 14, table cell (China, third column).
OLD: In 1090 China was richer per person than England. It later fell far behind. Under the Mongol rulers paper money lost value at about 11% a year from 1260 to 1290, and by 1368 people were back to barter
NEW: In 1090 China was richer per person than England. It later fell far behind. Paper money worked for long stretches, but it collapsed twice, in the last years of Mongol rule and again under the early Ming, and people fell back on barter and silver. Later, from the 1500s, a flood of foreign silver came with a boom in trade

### RT-04. Gregory King is over-read (block 9)

What is wrong. "More than half the families" is right by King's own counts. 849,000 families were in the "decreasing" group and 511,586 in the "increasing" group, so the decreasing group was about 62% of families. But King's line is an account of who added to or drew from the nation's yearly surplus. It is not a finding that these families could not get through a year. Their gap was filled by poor relief, charity, and the work of wives and children. The group also held common seamen and soldiers, who were fed by the state. Historians also say King's treatment of the poorest families is the weakest part of his table. "Could not cover a year of their own living" and "something cold" make him sound like he judged them, which is not quite fair either.

Evidence. Family counts and incomes: https://www.york.ac.uk/depts/maths/histstat/king.htm. Arkell on the weak parts of the table: https://ehs.org.uk/article/illuminations-and-distortions-gregory-kings-scheme-calculated-for-the-year-1688-and-the-social-structure-of-later-stuart-england1

Fix, block 9.
OLD: They spent more than they earned. More than half the families in one of the richest countries on earth could not cover a year of their own living.
NEW: By his reckoning they used up a little more than they earned, and the gap was made up by poor relief, charity and odd jobs. They were not all starving. But more than half the families in one of the richest countries on earth had nothing left over at the end of the year. King's numbers for the poorest are guesses, and later historians have argued with them.

### RT-05. "Schools came after" is too simple, and the reading numbers need a check (blocks 26, 27)

What is wrong. Three problems. First, skill is not the same as schooling. Many machine makers and mill mechanics learned through apprenticeship, not school. Second, schools did exist before 1870: Sunday schools, charity schools, and in Scotland a system of parish schools, which is often linked to Scotland's high reading rates. Third, the reading figures, 53% in 1650 and 54% in 1750, do not match the best known study. Cressy's signature counts put men at about 30% and women at about 10% around the 1640s, and men near 50% and women near 25% by about 1714. Those numbers rise, they are not flat. Signatures also understate reading. So "no change in a century" is not safe.

Evidence. Summary of Cressy: https://manyheadedmonster.com/2014/10/13/the-rabble-that-cannot-read-ordinary-peoples-literacy-in-seventeenth-century-england/ and https://www.history.org.uk/files/download/13324/1387299394/Jackie_Eales.pdf. Literacy in England, Scotland and Wales: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-of-education-quarterly/article/literacy-in-england-scotland-and-wales-15001900/75B90CDDA614D4A0DC3C77604169BE2C

Fix 1, block 26, table cell.
OLD: About 53% could read in 1650 and 54% in 1750. No change in a century when incomes were rising
NEW: Rising slowly. Estimates differ a lot, and they count signatures, which miss people who could read but not write. Many men could sign by the 1700s, fewer women

Fix 2, block 27.
OLD: One correction: schools for everyone came after the take-off in the first country, not before it. The first factories were built by a people of whom nearly half could not read.
NEW: One correction: schools for everyone came after the take-off in the first country, not before it. But learning did not wait for them. Sunday schools, charity schools and Scotland's parish schools taught many to read. And the skills that built machines were mostly learned at work, as an apprentice to a skilled man. Many of the workers in the first factories could not read at all.

### RT-06. "Factories were built from savings" is an older consensus, and it hides credit (blocks 0, 31, 37, 54)

What is wrong. The Crouzet view (profits ploughed back) is still widely held for the building and machines. But it leaves out the credit that kept mills running: bills of exchange, trade credit from suppliers and buyers, and loans from country banks. These are promises about the future. Brunt argues that some country banks acted like venture capital firms, for example a Truro bank that paid for Watt steam engines in Cornish mines. On government debt, the draft gives only the crowding out side. Ventura and Voth argue the opposite: that safe, easy to sell government bonds pulled money out of farm improvements and helped new industries grow faster. A fair chapter names both.

Evidence. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/rediscovering-risk-country-banks-as-venture-capital-firms-in-the-first-industrial-revolution/81A01AB97854AD48365F513A72202803 and https://www.nber.org/papers/w21280 and https://focus.bse.eu/sovereign-debt-industrial-revolution/

Fix 1, block 31.
OLD: and some historians find that war borrowing by the state left less for everyone else.
NEW: and some historians find that war borrowing by the state left less for everyone else. Others disagree. Hans-Joachim Voth and Jaume Ventura argue that safe government bonds drew rich landowners away from weak farm investments, and so left more room for the new industries. And short loans still mattered. Mills bought cotton on credit and sold cloth on credit, often with paper bills that promised payment in a few months. Some country banks took real risks on new machines.

Fix 2, block 37.
OLD: The first cotton mills cost about 1,000 to 3,000 pounds each and were mostly paid for from profits and family savings.
NEW: The first cotton mills cost about 1,000 to 3,000 pounds each and were mostly built from profits and family savings. But they ran on short credit, from suppliers, buyers and local banks. So it was not built on long bank loans. It still used a lot of credit.

Fix 3, block 54.
OLD: They worked when something real was pledged behind them. The first factories were built from savings.
NEW: They worked best when something real was pledged behind them. The first factories were built mostly from savings, and kept running on short credit from merchants and local banks.

Fix 4, block 0.
OLD: They did not pay for the first factories, which were small and built from profits and family savings.
NEW: They paid for little of the first factories, which were small and built mostly from profits and family savings, though they ran on short credit.

### RT-08. The output jump from 1650 to 1700 needs a warning (blocks 44, 45)

What is wrong. Block 45 shows output per person going from 1,446 to 2,412 in fifty years, a rise of about two thirds, before any factory. Then only to 3,343 in the next hundred years. The text says "Then something changed", so a reader will think the loop broke around 1650 to 1700. A critic of the Broadberry series notes that England's growth per person before the mid 1700s came in two half centuries, 1350 to 1400 and 1650 to 1700, and that population fell in both. That is close to the Malthus story the chapter tells, not an escape from it. The series also switches from England to Britain at 1700, as Our World in Data notes for the Maddison series. Ask the verifier to confirm both numbers and the series break.

Evidence. https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/Economic-History/An-evaluation-of-British-Economic-Growth.pdf (summary in search results), and the Maddison page note: https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/gdp-per-capita-maddison-project-database

Fix, block 44.
OLD: People worked more days and more of them worked in trades. Then something changed.
NEW: People worked more days and more of them worked in trades. Then output per person jumped. Part of that jump came while the number of people stopped growing, which is what Malthus would expect. The real break, when output kept rising while the people also multiplied, came later, in the 1700s and 1800s.

### RT-09. "Pay doubled" right after the plague is misleading (blocks 40, 41)

What is wrong. The table puts 26 in 1347 and 52 in 1350. Money wages jumped, but prices jumped too. John Munro finds that real wages of builders in southern England did not regain their late 1330s level until the late 1370s, about thirty years after the plague, and then rose to a peak in the late 1400s. Other series show a steadier rise from the 1350s. Either way, the gain came over decades, not in three years. The table source and series name should be given.

Evidence. https://ideas.repec.org/p/tor/tecipa/munro-04-04.html and https://www.medievalists.net/2010/08/23/before-and-after-the-black-death-money-prices-and-wages-in-fourteenth-century-england/

Fix, block 41.
OLD: The plague killed so many that workers became scarce and their pay doubled.
NEW: The plague killed so many that workers became scarce. Their pay rose fast, but prices rose too, and in some studies the real gain took a generation to arrive. Over the next century, what a day's pay could buy grew far beyond what it had been.

### RT-10. The table of explanations leaves out empire, coal and colonies, and marriage (blocks 47, 48)

What is wrong. Three omissions matter for the verdict. (1) Kenneth Pomeranz argues that coal near the surface and land overseas in the colonies, his "ghost acres", let Britain escape the limits that held back the richest parts of China. (2) Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson find that Europe's rise after 1500 came mostly in countries trading across the Atlantic, and that this trade made merchants strong enough to limit kings. (3) Historians of slavery, from Eric Williams on, argue that the profits and markets of the slave trade and plantations fed British growth. Critics say those profits were too small to matter much, so it must be shown as a debate. Also missing, the European marriage pattern (late marriage, many never married), which demographers see as a brake on the Malthus loop. These matter for the verdict because Pomeranz and the Atlantic trade view both say the break needed things outside Britain, which the reader's chain does not include. Parthasarathi adds that Britain protected its cotton makers against Indian cloth, a state choice, not a credit one, and many leading historians reject his view.

Evidence. https://people.bu.edu/chamley/764-23/AR05.pdf (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson), Pomeranz summary https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/Farewell%20to%20Alms/Pomeranz%20-%20abad.pdf, Parthasarathi and his critics https://muse.jhu.edu/article/482079/pdf, marriage pattern https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/120527/1/JMEMS_revised_Oct_2023_journal_style.pdf

## 2. Eight voices: the single most important miss for each

1. A monetary historian who stresses money supply. You test silver floods, not credit. The reader's Part 1 is about a working cycle of money and credit. The best case for it is Holland before 1670 and England after 1688, where cheap, trusted credit came with growth. The chapter counts Holland as a failure and never tests England after 1688 as a credit success. It also leaves out the late Ming silver boom.

2. A historian of the British industrial revolution. The chapter treats fixed capital as the whole story. Mills lived on working capital: cotton bought on credit, cloth sold on bills, country banks that took risks (Brunt). The 1650 to 1700 jump in block 45 also needs a warning, since it came while population stalled.

3. A historian of China. China appears only as a failure with paper money. Song paper money worked for long periods, Yuan prices were stable for decades, and the silver economy of the late Ming and Qing was large and busy. Pomeranz's coal and colonies argument, the main China centred view of the divergence, is missing.

4. A historian of the Islamic world. The go deeper box names two scholars and no critics, and the Ottoman tax row has no date. In the 1500s and 1600s Ottoman and English tax per person were close. Schwartz's work on the "print ban" story is the obvious check that is missing.

5. A development economist who studies late industrialisers. The closing claim, that the reader's picture fits late developers, is a promise with no evidence in this chapter. Gerschenkron is named in one line. Japan's loan is marked unchecked. Either trim the claim or give one worked case.

6. A historian of slavery and empire. Slavery, the colonies and Atlantic trade are not named once. Eric Williams, Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, and Pomeranz all put things outside Europe into the break. Colonial rule as a reason India or Southeast Asia did not take off is not mentioned. This is the largest omission for a reader in Asia.

7. A Malaysian 18 year old reader. "Where are we in this story?" Melaka was a great port of the age of commerce. Malaya enters only through one uncertain school figure. The reader also meets words that need a plain gloss: "crowding out", "pledged", "index", "big push".

8. A demographer. The Malthus loop is told with no brake. England's late marriage and many people never marrying (the European marriage pattern, Hajnal, Wrigley and Schofield) slowed the loop and is part of why England's wages stayed higher than in many places. Child deaths in block 10 are given, but not how they fed the loop.

## 4. Depth and narration

Three places where one more layer would most help an 18 year old.

1. Block 31, how a mill actually paid its way. Walk through one season: the owner buys raw cotton on credit from a Liverpool merchant, pays wages in cash each week, sells cloth on a bill that promises payment in a few months, and uses that bill at a local bank. This shows that promises about the future were inside the factory even when the building came from savings. Sources: Brunt and Pressnell, as above.

2. Block 41, why the gain was eaten. Show the mechanism step by step: more food and better pay meant earlier marriage and more children who lived, more people meant more mouths per field, and the price of bread rose faster than pay. Then add the brake that England had, late marriage, which made the fall slower. Source for the brake: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/120527/1/JMEMS_revised_Oct_2023_journal_style.pdf

3. Block 30, what the war debt did. Show the human chain: a London merchant buys a government bond, the state pays sailors and buys ships, and every year taxes on beer, salt and other goods are collected to pay the interest. Then the open question: did this crowd out the mills (Temin and Voth) or move money from sleepy farms to busy industry (Ventura and Voth)? This turns a number into a story with a real argument at its heart.

Where the story sags into report voice.
- Blocks 17, 27, 36 and 50, the "What test N does to the claim" boxes, all start "Part N holds..." and read like a grading sheet. Let one of them speak in a voice, for example "So the reader wins this round, with one surprise."
- Block 52, the verdict table, repeats numbers already given. Fine as a summary, but it should not be the last strong beat. Let block 55 end on a person or a place.
- Block 56, "This view would change if any of three things were shown. First ... Second ... Third", is a report. It could be told as three questions a young historian could go and answer.
- Block 47, a six row table of names with one line each. A reader will not remember it. Pick two scholars and let them argue in a short paragraph.

Strongest scene. Block 8 to 9, Gregory King drawing a line across his table, is the best opening image in the chapter (fix the over reading in RT-04 but keep the scene). Block 49, Elizabeth Bentley at six years old in a Leeds flax mill, is the strongest human detail. It is now a single paragraph in Test 4. Give it one more sentence that links it back to the argument: the escape from the loop was paid for in part by the long hours of children like her.
