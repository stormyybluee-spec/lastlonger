## L3.4 Why the peasants stayed poor: money, output and the missing chain (id rb-peasant)

Kicker: For most of history most people were poor, and stayed poor for centuries. A reader says the cause was a missing chain: money, credit, a strong state, schools and knowledge, each needing promises about the future to get started. This chapter tests that chain link by link.

[0 tldr]
- More money did not by itself make more output. Spain got the most silver and fell behind. China had state paper money more than seven hundred years before Europe's first factories, and did not build them first. Holland grew rich on cheap credit, then stalled. War played a part in each case.
- The reader is right that a whole chain was missing. Old states collected only a few percent of what their people made, most people in the world could not read, and books were rare.
- Promises about the future did pay for states, wars, canals and railways. They paid for little of the first factories, which were small and built mostly from the owners' own funds and profits, though the mills ran on short credit.
- The loop was real. Places that pulled ahead, such as Italy, Holland and China, stalled or fell back. What finally broke the loop in England is still argued. Most leading accounts name other links first, though some give money and finance a leading part.

[1 bridge] Built on earlier chapters: recall these before you start
- rb-credit: Chapter 02 found that old poverty came more from scarce output than from scarce coins. This chapter asks why output was scarce.
- rb-1971: Chapter 03 found that gold was a loose limit on lending, and that elastic money is not unlimited.
- rb-02: Rulers watered down coins long before paper money.
- rb-07: Debt is a promise. It is light when what it paid for grows, and heavy when it does not.

[2 callout] (How to read this chapter) A scene, four tests and a verdict, about 40 minutes in all. Each test ends with a short line on what it does to the claim. This is the second of seven deeper chapters that follow the overview in chapter 02.


### The claim on trial

[4 p] Chapter 02 said the peasants of old kingdoms were poor mainly because output was scarce, not because coins were scarce. The reader who wrote in accepts that, and pushes back. Output was scarce for a reason, the reader says. Here is the reply in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. Output was scarce because the cycle of money and credit that creates output was scarce.
- Part 2. The other links of the chain were missing too: a central state, schools, and shared knowledge.
- Part 3. Every link needs large promises about the future before it can start. Armies, schools, roads and factories are paid for long before they pay back.
- Part 4. A country that started and was stopped by war, famine or old habit fell back into a loop, and did not progress as a whole.

[6 p] This is a bigger idea than the one in chapter 02. It deserves a careful test.


### The kingdom, counted

[8 p] In 1696 an Englishman named Gregory King set out to estimate how his country lived in 1688, group by group. His numbers are estimates. Near the bottom he listed 400,000 families of cottagers and paupers, about 1.3 million people. Each family lived on about six and a half pounds a year.

[9 p] Then King drew a line across his table. Above it were the families who added to the wealth of the kingdom. Below it were 849,000 families who, in his words, were "decreasing the wealth of the kingdom". In King's reckoning they used up more than they earned. Poor relief, charity and odd jobs filled the gap, and the group also held seamen and soldiers. Still, about three in five families had nothing left over each year.

[10 p] In France, two famines between 1693 and 1710 killed over two million people, by one careful study. In the world as a whole in 1800, an estimated 43 of every 100 children died before they were five. In Britain it was about 33.

[11 p] That is the world the claim is about. Why did it last so long?


### Test 1. Did places with more money make more?

[13 p] If scarce money was what held output back, then places that got a flood of money, or invented new kinds of it, should have pulled ahead and stayed ahead. History gives four cases to look at.

[14 table]
| Place | The money it had | What happened to output per person |
|---|---|---|
| Spain, 1500s | First call on the silver of the Americas | About 1,118 dollars in 1500, 1,295 in 1600 and 1,009 in 1650, in 2011 dollars adjusted for prices. It rose, fell below where it began, and recovered to about 1,280 by 1700 |
| England, 1600 to 1650 | Coins per person rose 2.7 times | Fell 14%, in the years of the Civil War |
| China, from 1023 | State paper money, centuries before Europe | In 1090 China was richer per person than England, by one estimate. It later fell far behind. Paper money worked for long stretches, and under the Mongols prices rose slowly for about fifty years. In the rebellion that ended their rule people stopped trusting the notes, and by 1368 they were back to barter |
| Holland, 1600s | Very cheap credit for its time. Before 1688 Holland borrowed long term at 4%, while the English king paid 6% to 30% on short loans | It grew by more than half from 1500 to 1600, and in 1650 was two to three times as rich per person as England, depending on the data set. Output per person stayed flat from 1600 to 1750 |

[15 p] So four places had plenty of money or cheap credit for their time. Spain fell back, England's output shrank, China's paper money lost trust, and Holland rose and then stalled. But each case had another big cause. England fought a civil war, the Mongol state was breaking up, and Holland fought costly wars from about 1670. Spain, some scholars say, suffered a resource curse, where easy wealth weakens the rest of the economy. So money alone was not enough. That does not mean it did not matter.

[16 p] There is evidence on the other side too. The historian Nuno Palma argues that American silver helped England a great deal, in a quieter way. It let more people buy and sell with coins. In the 1520s about 40% of English households lived on wages. By the 1780s few households were outside the cash economy. Coins did not make the cloth, but they made it easier to sell. Palma also argues that more money made taxes easier to collect and helped England toward modern growth. Some historians, such as Peter Spufford, argue that trade suffered when Europe ran short of silver in the 1400s. Others, such as Carlo Cipolla, say credit made up for missing coin.

[17 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds in a weaker form: money looks like a helper, not the missing piece. Money and credit help trade to move, and a shortage hurts. But a flood of money did not by itself make a country productive. Holland grew rich while its credit was cheap, which fits the claim, and stalled only later, in years of war.


### Test 2. Were the other links missing?

[19 p] Take the links one at a time.

[20 p] The state. A modern government collects a large share of what its people make, and spends it on roads, courts, schools and soldiers. Old kings could not, or did not.

[21 table]
| Where and when | Tax collected by the central state |
|---|---|
| England, 1500 | About 2% of what the country made |
| England, 1600 | About 2%, and about 4% by 1650 |
| Most European states, 1500s | Under 5% |
| Britain and the Netherlands, 1780s | Over 10%. All rough estimates |
| The Ottoman Empire | Per person, at most three days of an Istanbul labourer's pay each year, and usually under two, 1500 to 1914 |

[22 p] Schooling. In 1820 about 12% of the world's adults could read. In 1870 about 17% of the world's children were in primary school. By 1950 it was 57%, and by 1970 it was 72%. The reader's figure, a 70% chance that a child goes to school, matches the world in 1970. The Razak Report of 1956 planned a national school system for Malaysia, and one rough data set puts primary enrolment at about two in three children in 1950 and nearly all by 1960.

[23 p] Knowledge. In Europe, before printing, every book was copied by hand. In Britain about 208,000 books were printed in the fifty years to 1499. In the next fifty years it was about 2.8 million, thirteen times as many.

[24 p] So the links were missing, as the claim says. But the order in which they arrived is a surprise.

[25 table]
| Link | When it arrived in England | Before or after the first factories (from about the 1770s)? |
|---|---|---|
| Printed books | From the late 1400s | Long before |
| A state that could tax | Tax rose to about 4% of output by 1650. From 1700 to 1788 England raised about twice as much tax per person as France | Before |
| Reading | Estimates disagree. A model from book sales gives 53% in 1650 and 54% in 1750. Signature counts show men rising from about 30% in the 1640s to 50% by 1714, and women from 10% to 25% | Before, but how fast it rose is unclear |
| Schools for every child | About 39% of children in primary school in 1870 and 98% in 1900 (estimates) | After, about a century after the first factories |

[26 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 holds, with one surprise. A whole chain was missing, and the reader names the right links. The surprise: schools for everyone came after the take-off in the first country. But Sunday schools, charity schools and Scotland's parish schools taught many children before then. And Italy had books and rich cities early, and still stalled.


### Test 3. Does every link need promises from the future?

[28 p] This is the heart of the claim. Did the links get built on borrowed money, on promises to pay later? Look at who actually borrowed.

[29 p] The state borrowed, on a scale never seen before in England. In 1688 the English government owed about 1 million pounds. Nine years of war later it owed nearly 17 million, about 40% of everything the country made in a year. By the end of the wars with Napoleon the debt was close to 180% of a year's output on one measure, and about 150% on another. That looks like the reader's picture: enormous promises about the future. But most of it paid for war.

[30 p] How did such a debt work? A London merchant bought a bond, a paper that promised him interest every year. The state spent his money on ships and soldiers, and Parliament voted taxes to pay the interest. By 1815 the interest took close to two fifths of all the money the state raised.

[31 p] The factories borrowed little for their buildings and machines. The first cotton mills were small. A small one cost about 1,000 to 2,000 pounds to start, a large one over 3,000. A famous Manchester firm, M'Connel and Kennedy, began with about 1,700 pounds. Most scholars agree that owners paid for these mainly from their own funds and from profits put back into the firm. Others, such as Joel Mokyr, argue that weak loan markets kept firms small. The law also held private lending down. From 1714 no lender could charge more than 5%. Peter Temin and Hans-Joachim Voth find that war borrowing made banks lend less to others. Jaume Ventura and Voth disagree. Safe bonds, they argue, drew landowners away from weak farm investments and left room for new industry.

[32 p] Promises were still inside the factory, though. Picture one season at a mill. The owner buys raw cotton on credit and pays months later. He sells his cloth for a bill, a paper that promises payment in a few months. If he needs cash now, a local bank pays him a little less than the bill's value. Some country banks went further. The historian Liam Brunt describes a Truro bank that paid for Watt steam engines in Cornish mines. So the buildings came mostly from savings, but a mill's daily life ran on short promises.

[33 p] The big networks did borrow, and here the record is mixed. Canals and railways were far bigger, too big for most families alone.

[34 table]
| A promise that was kept | Promises that were broken |
|---|---|
| New York built the Erie Canal from 1817 with borrowed money. When it began to borrow, it set aside taxes on salt and on auctions to pay the interest. The canal earned well and the debt was repaid easily | Pennsylvania and some other states copied the idea without pledged taxes. In 1841 and 1842 eight states and the territory of Florida stopped paying their debts |
| Japan is reported to have borrowed 1 million pounds in London in 1870, at 9% interest, for its first railway. The line from Tokyo to Yokohama opened in 1872. Repayment is not checked here | Indiana issued over 10 million dollars of bonds for canals, a railway and roads, but got only about 5.5 million in cash. About 3 million went on credit to a bank that failed in 1839, and work stopped |

[35 p] So in these cases a promise worked when something real was pledged behind it. A tax, a canal that earns tolls, a railway that sells tickets. A promise with nothing behind it often failed. Every state that refused to pay had also borrowed to fund banks. A few cases show a pattern, not a law.

[36 p] The reader's idea has serious company among economists. The "big push" theory says that one factory alone may fail in a poor country, because nobody nearby earns enough to buy what it makes, while many factories started together can succeed. Starting many things at once takes money up front. The economic historian Alexander Gerschenkron argued that countries that came late, such as Germany and Russia, leaned on big banks or on the state to do this. Neither idea was built to explain Britain, the first case, and tests of Gerschenkron's idea are mixed.

[37 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds in part. States and big networks were built on promises about the future. The first factories mostly were not. In these cases a promise worked best with something pledged behind it. Whether the picture fits late developers better is for later chapters.

[38 check] Q: A friend says the Industrial Revolution was built on bank loans. What would you tell them?
A: A small mill cost about 1,000 to 2,000 pounds, a large one over 3,000, paid mostly from the owners' own funds and profits. But mills ran on short credit. The huge long-term borrowing of that age was by the government, and it paid mostly for war. Borrowing mattered more for canals and railways.


### Test 4. Was there a loop?

[40 p] The reader says that a country stopped halfway fell back, and went round again. The record agrees. The clearest case is England after the Black Death.

[41 table]
| England | People (millions) | What a day's pay could buy (an index, Bank of England data set) |
|---|---|---|
| 1347, before the plague | 4.8 | 26 |
| 1350, after it | 3.2 | 52 |
| 1450 | 1.9 | 70 |
| 1650 | 5.3 | 29 |

[42 p] The plague killed so many that workers became scarce. On this index, what a day's pay could buy doubled within a few years. John Munro finds a slower rise, over a generation, because prices rose too. Either way, survivors lived better for a century. Then the people came back. By 1650 there were almost three times as many mouths as in 1450, and a day's pay bought about the same as before the plague. The gain had been eaten. This is the trap that Thomas Malthus described in 1798.

[43 p] Why was the gain eaten? When pay was good, more children lived. Gregory Clark finds that the richest Englishmen left about twice as many surviving children as the poorest. A generation later there were more mouths on the same fields, so food grew dear and pay bought less. But north-west Europe had a brake. In hard times people married late, or not at all. E. A. Wrigley argues that this kept living standards there well above bare need.

[44 p] Whole countries went round the same way.

[45 table]
| An early leader | Its peak | What followed |
|---|---|---|
| Italy | Richer than England or Holland in 1450 | Output per person fell by about a quarter by 1600 |
| Holland | Two to three times as rich as England in 1650 | No growth per person from 1600 to 1750 |
| China | Richer than England in 1090, by one estimate | Output per person fell about 43% from 1700 to 1820, by one estimate |

[46 p] And yet the loop was not perfectly closed, even in England. Output per person tells a softer story. It stayed roughly flat from 1400 to 1600 while a day's pay fell, then dipped in the civil war. Then, from 1650 to 1700, output per person jumped.

[47 table]
| England, then Great Britain from 1700 | Output per person, Maddison data set (2011 dollars) | Output per person, Broadberry and others (1990 dollars) |
|---|---|---|
| 1500 | 1,697 | 1,114 |
| 1600 | 1,691 | 1,123 |
| 1650 | 1,446 | 1,110 |
| 1700 | 2,412 | 1,563 |
| 1800 | 3,343 | 2,080 |

[48 p] Read each column on its own, since the dollars differ. Both show output flat or falling up to 1650, then rising. But the jump to 1700 is about two thirds in one and two fifths in the other. Part of it is only because 1650 was a low year after civil war, and because the number of people had stopped growing. The real break came later. By 1850 there were three times as many people as in 1700, and a day's pay bought about twice what it had in 1650.

[49 p] What broke the loop? Scholars have argued for two hundred years, and each has part of the evidence.

[50 table]
| The explanation | Who | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| People | Thomas Malthus, Gregory Clark | More food meant more people until the land could feed no more. Clark adds that the habits of the better off, such as saving, spread through their many children. Wrigley calls his evidence slim |
| Marriage | John Hajnal, E. A. Wrigley | People in north-west Europe married late, or not at all, in hard times, so the loop turned more slowly |
| Rules | Douglass North and Barry Weingast | After 1688 the king could no longer seize property or cancel his debts at will, so lenders trusted the state. Critics answer that interest rates stayed high for about forty years |
| Prices | Robert Allen | English wages were high and coal was cheap, so it paid to invent machines that saved workers and burned coal. Jane Humphries answers that the first mills used cheap child and female labour |
| Knowledge | Joel Mokyr | A culture grew up that treated useful knowledge as something to be shared, tested and improved |
| The state | John Brewer, Mark Dincecco | A state that could tax and borrow could keep order and fight. Critics answer that China, India and the Ottoman lands kept order too |
| Money | Nuno Palma | More silver coin drew people into markets and made taxes easier to collect. Critics answer that Spain, which got the silver first, fell behind |
| Finance | Peter Rousseau and Richard Sylla | Banks and markets helped to start growth in several countries. Critics answer that English law held private credit down |
| Coal and colonies | Kenneth Pomeranz | Cheap coal and land overseas freed Britain from limits that held back the richest parts of China |
| Atlantic trade | Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, James Robinson | Profits from Atlantic trade made merchants strong enough to limit kings |

[51 p] Read down that list. Money and credit lead in two rows, and both have strong critics. Credit also sits inside the rules and state rows. But read it again and the reader's larger point survives. Nearly every scholar names a different link, and some look outside Britain. The reader argues that each link leaned on the others. A state needs taxes, taxes need trade, trade needs safe roads and coins, machines need knowledge, knowledge needs books. This chapter does not prove that each link needed all the others. That part is still to be tested.

[52 p] The break was not kind to everyone who lived through it. In 1832 a woman of 23 named Elizabeth Bentley told a committee of Parliament about her childhood. She had started work in a flax mill in Leeds at the age of six. When the mill was busy she worked from five in the morning until nine at night, and children who came late were strapped. The committee that heard her was set up to support a law, and its questions led the witnesses. Still, she shows who paid part of the price of the escape.

[53 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 holds as a description. Loops were real, in wages and in whole countries. What broke the loop in England is still argued. The leading accounts point to people, rules, prices, knowledge, the state, and coal and colonies. Two give money or credit a leading part, and both are disputed. Most treat money as a helper, not the missing piece.


### Beyond Europe

[55 p] This chapter's numbers come mostly from Europe, where the long data series are best. That limits the verdict. The historian Anthony Reid calls the years from about 1450 to 1680 Southeast Asia's age of commerce, when Melaka was one of the world's great ports. He links its end to a crisis in the 1600s and to Dutch power, though others question that story. China took in huge amounts of Japanese and American silver from about 1550, and the historian Richard von Glahn links this to a fast boom in late Ming trade. There, more money came with more trade.

[56 p] India was the world's great maker of cotton cloth. On one estimate its output per person fell by about a fifth from 1600 to 1850. The historian Prasannan Parthasarathi argues that its industry fell back after about 1780, as the East India Company took control and Britain protected its own mills against Indian cloth. Many leading historians doubt that this was the main cause. Whether colonial rule, or the profits of slavery, shaped who grew rich is a large open question that this chapter has not weighed.


### The verdict on the chain

[58 table]
| The claim | Finding | The fact that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Output was scarce because money and credit were scarce | Holds in a weaker form. Money helped, but was not enough alone | Spain, China and Holland had plenty of money or cheap credit, and each later stalled, often in war. Holland grew rich first |
| 2. The other links were missing too | Holds | English kings collected about 2% of output in 1500. About 12% of the world's adults could read in 1820 |
| 3. Every link needs promises from the future | Holds in part | The state's debt went from 1 million to 17 million pounds in nine years. A cotton mill cost about 1,000 to 3,000 pounds or more, mostly from the owners' own funds |
| 4. A country stopped halfway fell into a loop | Holds as a description. The cause of the escape is argued | What a day's pay could buy doubled after the plague, and was about back where it began by 1650 |

[59 p] Here is the claim again, rebuilt so the record can carry it.

[60 steps]
- The peasants stayed poor because a whole chain was missing: a state that could tax and keep order, knowledge that could spread, and ways to make more with the same hands. Money and credit were one link, helpful but not shown to be the decisive one.
- Where money arrived without the other links, as in Spain, it did not make the country more productive. Where the other links were in place, money and credit helped trade to move.
- Promises about the future built the strong state and the great networks, the canals and railways. In these cases they worked best when something real was pledged behind them. The first factories were built mostly from the owners' own funds, and ran on short credit.
- Countries that came later may have leaned more on banks and on the state, as Gerschenkron argued. This chapter did not test that.

[61 p] That last line matters for the chapters to come. The reader's claim is weakest as a story about England in 1700. It may be strongest for a country catching up in the 1900s, but that is an idea to test, not a finding. The next chapters test it.

[62 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that the early leaders stalled because money or credit ran short, and not for other reasons. Second, that Britain's first factories depended on bank credit after all. Third, that countries with deeper credit markets before 1800 grew faster than their neighbours. Holland's record so far is mixed.

[64 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Two views this chapter could not test
[64.0 p] Some scholars explain why parts of the Middle East fell behind Europe by pointing to law and politics. Timur Kuran argues that rules on partnerships and inheritance kept firms small. Jared Rubin argues that rulers who drew their authority from religious leaders were slow to allow printing and lending at interest. Kuran's claim is about rules of Islamic law, which he says were sound for their time. Rubin says plainly that the cause was not the faith itself but the bargain between rulers and religious leaders. Both views are argued over. Some reviewers say Kuran reads the law too narrowly, and that merchants found ways around it. Others say the law was a sign of deeper problems, not their cause. The historian Kathryn Schwartz found that the story of an Ottoman ban on printing rests on thin evidence. This chapter did not test any of these views.

[64.1 p] Deirdre McCloskey argues that secure property existed in China, India and much of Europe for centuries before 1800, so rules alone cannot explain the take-off. She points instead to a change in how people talked about trade and invention. Jared Rubin, reviewing her book, answers that she does not explain where that change came from. That too is untested here.
