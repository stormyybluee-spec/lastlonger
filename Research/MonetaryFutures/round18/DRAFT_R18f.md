## L3.7 Why do the biggest printers end up poor? Speed, trust and the price of money (id rb-printers)

Kicker: Some countries printed so much money that it became worthless. Others created even larger sums and, for years, saw little rise in shop prices. A reader says money obeys supply and demand, that the speed at which it changes hands matters, and that a small country can catch inflation from abroad. This chapter tests all three, and ends at a market in Kuala Lumpur.

[0 tldr]
- Over long periods, countries that create money much faster than others have much higher inflation. Among low-inflation countries the link is much weaker.
- How fast money changes hands matters a great deal, but it is partly a result of trust. In Germany in 1923 people spent money the moment they got it. In America after 2008 much of the new money sat still.
- In the four cases here, printing to pay the government's bills came on top of another big blow, such as war costs, a farm collapse or an oil crash. The line with buying bonds is one of degree and trust.
- Malaysia does catch some inflation from abroad, mainly through food and the US dollar. In 2022 its prices rose far less than America's, and the state's budget paid part of the bill. In 1998 it fixed its currency to the dollar.

- rb-credit: Chapter 02 said that money printed to pay a state's bills, while output falls, brings inflation, as in Zimbabwe.

### The claim on trial

[4 p] The reader who wrote in has three ideas about the price of money itself. Here they are, with a question the reader asked.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. Money obeys supply and demand. Make more of it than people want to hold, and each unit is worth less.
- Part 2. The speed at which money changes hands, its velocity, decides how hard a given amount of money pushes on prices.
- Part 3. A country can import inflation, or falling prices, from abroad, through its exchange rate and the things it buys.
- The question. If a body that holds reserves can create money, why do the countries that create the most end up among the poorest?


### A newspaper in the morning, and again at night

[7 p] In June 1922 the Austrian writer Stefan Zweig was at Westerland, a seaside town in Germany. From then on, he later wrote, the inflation took hold. Years later, in his memoir, he described what money had become.

[8 quote] "I have known days when I had to pay fifty thousand marks for a newspaper in the morning and a hundred thousand in the evening" (Stefan Zweig, The World of Yesterday, written in 1941 and 1942)

[9 p] He sent his publisher a manuscript and asked his publisher to pay him early for ten thousand copies. By the time the payment reached the bank, he wrote, it "hardly paid the postage". Some two weeks later, he wrote, one could find notes of a hundred thousand marks lying in the gutter.

[10 p] At the worst point, in October 1923, prices in Germany were doubling about every 3.7 days. The government was paying for almost all of its spending by printing notes. And Germans had learned a lesson that families in a runaway inflation soon learn. Do not hold money. Spend it the moment you are paid, before it shrinks again.

[11 p] Keep this in mind: money that loses value is spent at once. That is the key to this whole chapter.


### Test 1. Does money obey supply and demand?

[13 p] Picture a market with 100 loaves of bread and 100 ringgit to spend on them. Each loaf costs about 1 ringgit. Now double the money to 200 ringgit and keep the bread the same. If people spend it all, each loaf ends up costing about 2 ringgit. That is supply and demand for money. This is a made-up example with round numbers. Does the real world work like this? For this chapter, we used World Bank data to compare 144 countries from 2000 to 2019. It is our own calculation, not a published study. We asked: did countries whose money grew fastest also have prices that rose fastest? We scored the match on a scale where 1 is a perfect match and 0 is no link at all. Across all 144 countries the match scored 0.88, very strong. A few countries with very high inflation pull this score up.

[14 p] But look closer and the picture changes. The short version of the next table: the link is strong in high inflation and weak in calm times.

[15 table]
| Which countries, and which years | Match score | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| All 144 countries, 2000 to 2019 | 0.88 | Very strong. Countries that made money fastest had the fastest price rises |
| The 88 countries with inflation under 6% a year, 2000 to 2019 | 0.51 | Middling. In calm countries money and prices often go separate ways |
| America, year by year, 1960 to 2019 | 0.14 | Weak. In a single year, more money says little about prices |
| America, in blocks of five years, 1960 to 2019 | 0.65 | Fairly strong. Over five years the link shows up again |

[16 p] So the link is strong when inflation is high and weaker when it is low. In America from 1985 to 2019 the year by year match was even below zero, at -0.33, which means money and prices tended to move opposite ways. In a calm economy, the amount of money can rise a lot one year without prices following. Then came the biggest test of all. The next table counts the money central banks created and used to buy bonds, which are loans to a government or a company. The short version: America's central bank created huge sums, and for years shop prices barely moved. Europe and Japan did the same, as a box below shows.

|---|---|---|

[19 p] Those are some of the largest money creations in history, and for years shop prices barely moved. House and share prices did rise, by one estimate about 7% and 5%. Then America's money, counting cash and deposits, grew 26.8% in the year to February 2021, and by June 2022 prices were rising about 9% a year. Did the money cause it? Economists still argue. Ben Bernanke and Olivier Blanchard found that most of the rise came from sudden blows, such as dearer energy and food and shortages of goods. The link was loose and slow.

[20 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds over long periods and in high inflation. In calm times it is loose. The largest money creations of the 2010s barely moved shop prices for years, which supply and demand alone does not explain. They did lift house and share prices.


### Test 2. Does the speed of money matter?

[22 p] Here the reader points to a real piece of the puzzle. Picture one banknote of 10 ringgit, in a made-up example. If it is spent once a year, it does 10 ringgit of buying. If it is spent ten times a year, passing from shop to worker to shop, it does 100 ringgit of buying. The same note, ten times the push on prices. That speed is called the velocity of money.

[23 p] Now look at what happened to velocity in America.

[24 table]
| America | How many times a year each dollar was spent (velocity of cash and deposits, measured in one quarter of each year) |
|---|---|
| 1960 | 1.82 |
| 1997, the peak | 2.19 |
| 2008 | 1.94 |
| 2020, the low | 1.13 |
| 2026 | 1.42 |

[25 p] From the 1997 peak to the 2020 low the speed of money in America fell by almost half. But velocity is just a year's spending divided by the amount of money. A fall says that money grew faster than spending, not why. Many economists see it as a result of saving, interest rates and trust.

[26 p] So where did the new money go? When the central bank buys a bond from an investor, the investor's bank account grows, and the investor's bank gets new reserves, money that banks keep at the central bank. People cannot spend reserves, and reserves are not in the velocity table. The deposits could be spent, but by one reading much was saved. From October 2008 the central bank also paid banks interest to keep their reserves. So much of the money did not reach the shops.

[27 p] In a runaway inflation the opposite happens. People spend as fast as they can. In Germany from January 1922 to July 1923, prices rose about 2,000 times while the number of notes rose about 380 times. Prices ran more than five times ahead of the printing press, because each note changed hands faster. The economist Thomas Sargent reads this as Germans holding as little money as they could. In Zimbabwe the speed of money rose by about 70% from 2007 to 2008, by the IMF's count.

[28 p] So inflation is not just more money. It is more money multiplied by how fast people spend it, set against the goods there are to buy. Economists who study runaway inflations argue that this depends mostly on what people expect prices to do, which is a matter of trust. When people trust money to keep its value, they hold it. When they stop trusting it, they throw it at the nearest shop.

[29 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 holds as a description. A fall in speed is how the 2010s stayed calm, and a rise is how Germany ran ahead of its printing. But speed is measured by dividing spending by money, so it is a result of trust, saving and interest rates as much as a cause.

[30 check] Q: A friend says the American central bank printed trillions of dollars after 2008, so prices should have exploded. Why didn't they, for years?
A: Because much of the new money was not spent. Much became bank reserves, which people cannot spend, and much of what reached people's accounts was saved. Velocity fell by almost half from 1997 to 2020. More money that moves slowly can push prices less than less money that moves fast. But prices did rise fast in 2021 and 2022.


### Test 3. Why do the biggest printers end up poor?

[32 p] Now the reader's question. If creating money can be harmless, why did some countries print themselves into ruin? Look at what each one printed for, and what else was going on. The short version: each printed to pay its government's bills, and each was hit by another heavy blow at the same time.

[33 table]
| Country | What the printing paid for | How fast prices rose | The other big blow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany, 1923 | Almost all government spending | Doubling about every 3.7 days | War payments to the victors of the First World War, and the occupation of its industrial heart |
| Hungary, 1946 | A government rebuilding after war | Doubling about every 15 hours, the fastest ever | About 40% of the country's wealth destroyed in the war |
| Zimbabwe, 2008 | Central bank spending of many kinds, paid for partly by printing | Doubling about every 25 hours, by one estimate | Output had already fallen about 40% after a collapse in farming |
| Venezuela, 2018 | A large government deficit | Up over 1.6 million percent in a year, by one estimate | The oil price fell 71%, and later sanctions |

[34 p] Read the second and fourth columns together. In each case the record points to a government printing to pay its own bills, though Hungary's share was not found. And each economy had been badly hurt. Venezuela's output halved from 2013 to 2018, and Zimbabwe's fell 14% to 18% more in 2008 alone. Germany is different. By one study its output grew strongly until mid 1922, while prices already rose fast, then fell by about a third in 1923. Hungary was rebuilding from ruins. So the new money chased goods that were few, falling, or both.

[35 p] It looks like a clean line between buying bonds and printing for bills. In the 2010s America's central bank bought bonds, which are loans to a government or a company. The economy kept working, and much of the new money was held. Zimbabwe's central bank paid for spending a shrinking economy could not support, and few people wanted to hold the result. Trust broke, speed rose, and prices ran.

[36 p] But the line is not a wall. In 2020 the same central bank bought government bonds while the government spent a record 3.1 trillion dollars more than it took in. From March to August 2020 the central bank's share of all government bonds rose from 15% to 22%. In 2021 and 2022 prices rose fast. Both kinds of buying create new money and take the state's debt in return. What keeps them apart is degree, how much and for how long. It is trust, whether people believe the debt will be repaid from future taxes and the central bank will stop when prices rise. And it is output, whether the economy can make enough goods to meet the spending. So the reader's worry about modern central banks is not foolish.

[37 p] In Caracas in early 2018 a young man named Wilmer Rojas worked outside a subway station, sewing handbags out of banknotes that had lost their value. He needed up to 800 notes for one purse.

[38 quote] "People throw them away because they are no good to buy anything." (Wilmer Rojas, 25, Caracas (AFP, February 2018))

[39 p] Some cases are less clear. Turkey's central bank cut interest rates in 2021 and 2022 while prices rose, and inflation reached 85.5% in October 2022. But Turkey's economy grew every year from 2021 to 2024, and this chapter did not check how much of the budget its central bank paid for. The state did carry costs through a scheme protecting savers. Turkey looks more like a currency losing trust while interest rates were held far below inflation.

[40 p] And how do these stories end? Germany's ended in October and November 1923, by the economist Thomas Sargent's account. A new currency was set up with strict limits, the central bank stopped lending to the government, and taxes rose and spending fell. People believed the printing had ended, so they held money again, and prices stopped rising. The new currency was in place by 1924. Argentina, which has had high inflation for most of fifty years, saw prices rise 211% in 2023. In 2024 its central bank stopped paying for the government directly, the budget showed a surplus before interest, and inflation fell to about 118%. Zimbabwe dropped its own currency in early 2009, and its output grew 13.3% that year.

[41 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) The question has a partial answer. In the four cases here, printing to pay the state's bills deepened another heavy blow, until people would not hold the money. But 2020 to 2022 shows the line with ordinary bond buying is one of degree and trust, not a wall. Whether the biggest printers end up poorest was not tested.

[43 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): How the printers ended, Turkey, and Japan in the 1930s
[43.0 p] Germany, 1923, step by step. By October 1923 the government was paying for virtually all of its spending by printing notes. On 15 October the Rentenmark was set up, with strict limits on how much could be issued. From 15 November the central bank stopped lending to the government by buying its short-term debt. In late November prices suddenly stopped rising. Thomas Sargent's lesson is that the change worked because it was binding enough to be widely believed.

[43.1 p] Argentina, 2024. Besides ending direct payments from the central bank, the government took in more than it spent before paying interest. By an analysis from the bank Itaú, this surplus was about 1.8% of output.

[43.2 p] Turkey. From late 2021 a scheme promised savers that the state would make up their losses if the lira fell. By May 2023 it had cost the budget 95.3 billion lira, by the finance minister's figure, as reported by Daily Sabah. Economists link later losses at the central bank to it.

[43.3 p] Japan from 1932, a partial test of the first point under "What would change this verdict". From November 1932 the Bank of Japan bought new government bonds directly to pay for a budget deficit, and Japan recovered from the Great Depression. Inflation rose fast only after 1936, when the finance minister who ran the policy, Takahashi Korekiyo, was killed and those after him did not stop the funding. Historians still argue how much of the recovery to credit to the policy itself. This case was not checked in this chapter's own research. Sources: a Kobe University discussion paper and a Bank of Japan governor's speech.

### Test 4. Can a country catch inflation from abroad?

[45 p] In February 2022 a shopper named Meena Tharshini was buying food at a market in Kuala Lumpur. She told a reporter how her family's chicken had changed. They used to buy two a week.

[46 quote] "But nowadays, I can only budget for one." (Meena Tharshini, 41, on buying one chicken a week instead of two, Kuala Lumpur (Malay Mail, February 2022))

[47 p] The government had capped the price of a standard chicken at 8.90 ringgit a kilogram, from 5 February to 5 June 2022. One poultryman told the same paper, "We honestly barely make any profits from this." Sellers blamed the cap and festive demand. Trade reports point back to the farm. Chicken feed is largely imported maize and soy, and it cost more as the ringgit weakened and the war in Ukraine disrupted grain. That is imported inflation, as the next picture shows. The government set aside money to pay farmers part of the gap, and from 1 June Malaysia stopped exporting chickens for a time, as reported. The cost moved to farmers, the budget and buyers abroad.

[48 p] Picture a bag of flour that Malaysia buys from abroad for 1 US dollar. If 1 dollar costs 4 ringgit, the bag costs 4 ringgit. If the ringgit falls and 1 dollar costs 5 ringgit, the same bag now costs 5 ringgit, though nothing about the flour has changed. That is imported inflation. This is a made-up example with round numbers. Malaysia buys much of its food and many goods from abroad, and by an IMF estimate most of its trade is priced in US dollars.

[49 p] So how much of a fall in the ringgit reaches the shops? Bank Negara measured it.

[50 table]
| If the ringgit falls 5% against the dollar | What happens within a year, by Bank Negara's 2022 estimate |
|---|---|
| The price of imports | Rises about 2.1%, so about 40% of the fall gets through |
| Core inflation, the prices people pay for most things | Rises only about 0.2 points on average, and about 0.4 points in past sharp falls |
| Food | Rises more than other goods |

[51 p] Why so little? About a quarter of what Malaysian households spend is imported, counting imported parts. Firms often take some of the extra cost themselves. And the state caps some prices and pays part of the cost, called a subsidy. The test came twice.

[52 table]
|  | 1998 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| How far the ringgit fell against the dollar | 28.3%, comparing yearly averages | 5.4% over the year |
| Malaysia's inflation | 5.3% | 3.3% |
| Inflation elsewhere | Indonesia 58.4%. America 1.5% | America 8.0%. Britain 9.1% |

[53 p] In 1998 Bank Negara's own rough rule said a fall that big would add about 5 points to inflation. Prices rose 5.3%, close to the rule, and far less than Indonesia's but far more than America's. From September 1998 the ringgit was also fixed at 3.80 to the dollar. Economists still argue how much that helped. In 2022 Malaysia's inflation was less than half of America's. Fixed pump prices and price caps held it down. The state planned 37.3 billion ringgit of fuel subsidies that year, so the calm was paid for partly by the state's budget, now and later.

[54 p] A country can also catch falling prices. Hong Kong pegs, or ties, its dollar to the US dollar at a fixed rate. From November 1998 Hong Kong's prices fell for 68 months in a row, while America's kept rising. A strong US dollar was one cause. Because of the tie, Hong Kong's dollar rose with it, so imports got cheaper. A crash in property prices was another cause. Argentina, tied to the dollar until 2001, saw prices fall three years in a row. So a tie to the dollar does not simply pass on America's inflation. It takes away the choice to let the currency fall. So when times are hard at home, prices and wages have to fall instead.

[55 p] In 2017 Bank Negara's governor said the spread of world money conditions to economies like Malaysia's "is unavoidable", though countries "should not be starved of policy autonomy", meaning the freedom to set their own policy. In May 2022, when some called for the ringgit to be fixed again, Bank Negara's governor, by then a different person, said "pegging the ringgit will not be in the best interest of Malaysia".

[56 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 3 holds in part. Malaysia does catch inflation from abroad, mainly through food and the US dollar. In 2022 little of the currency fall reached the shops, but in 1998 about as much as Bank Negara's rough rule said it would. In 1998 its prices rose less than its hard-hit neighbours' but more than America's or Britain's. In 2022 they rose less than America's or Britain's.

[57.1 p] Malaysia, 1998. Bank Negara wrote that about 11% of the price basket had prices set by the state, for goods such as sugar, flour, petrol, bread, chicken and cooking oil. It added that there were "no price controls as such, contrary to general perceptions". The government also raised several of those ceilings in late 1997 and early 1998, which added nearly 1.1 points to inflation. In April 1998 the IMF asked Malaysia to scale price controls back to basic food eaten by the poorest households. The two institutions described the same system with different weight. On 1 September 1998 controls on money leaving the country began, and the next day the ringgit was fixed at 3.80 to the dollar. Economists still argue about the controls. Kaplan and Rodrik think they did more good than critics say, and Athukorala argues they did little.

[57.2 p] Malaysia, 2022. The ringgit ended the year 5.4% down, but at its weakest it had fallen 12.2% from its high. The state planned 37.3 billion ringgit of fuel subsidies. The Auditor-General later counted about 45 billion ringgit of claims paid for petrol, diesel and bottled gas from December 2021 to October 2022, as reported by Malay Mail. Malaysia also earns from oil and gas. Bank Negara expected petroleum to bring 27.3% of the government's revenue in 2022. So the high oil price that raised the subsidy bill also raised part of the income that paid it.

[57.3 p] Hong Kong, 1998 to 2004. Hong Kong's monetary authority estimated that the property crash explained about half of the fall in consumer prices. It also named prices moving closer to those in mainland China, and a world slowdown. Its chief, Joseph Yam, said in 2001 that Hong Kong's monetary arrangements had not been contributing to the fall.

[57.4 p] The governors. In 2017 Bank Negara's governor was Muhammad bin Ibrahim. He called the choice of interest rates for a small open economy a "catch 22": raise rates and money flows in, cut them and money may flow out. In 2022 the governor was Nor Shamsiah Mohd Yunus. She said a peg "will have substantial risks", and that an earlier peg had taken up a sizeable amount of reserves.

[60 p] Here is the reader's idea again, rebuilt so the record can carry it.

[61 steps]
- The value of money depends on how much there is, how fast it moves, and how much people trust it to keep its value. How fast it moves is partly a result of that trust.
- Creating money to buy bonds and printing money to pay the state's bills can blur. In the 2010s much new money sat still. In the four printer cases it was spent as fast as people could. The line is one of degree, trust, and whether output can keep up.
- A small open country like Malaysia catches part of the world's inflation through food and the dollar. It can soften the blow with subsidies and price caps, and someone still pays, through the budget, at the farm or through shortages.

[62 p] So why do the biggest printers end up poor? Not because creating money is poison in itself. In the cases here, they used it to cover a hole that a war, a farm collapse or an oil crash had torn open, and people stopped believing the hole would be closed. A newspaper that cost fifty thousand marks in the morning cost a hundred thousand at night. A family in Kuala Lumpur bought one chicken instead of two. Both were feeling prices rise. In Germany the money itself had lost people's trust. In Kuala Lumpur the ringgit still bought a chicken, and the strain showed up in the basket, the budget and the farms.

[63 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that a country printed to pay its government's bills on a large scale, in a growing economy, without high inflation. Second, that the slowdown in the speed of money after 2008 had a cause that will not reverse. Third, that Malaysia's subsidies cost more over time than the inflation they prevented. The third is a fair question this chapter did not settle. The first has a partial test, Japan from 1932, told in the box after Test 3.

[64 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Two more printer stories
[64.0 p] Hungary, 1946. After the Second World War about 40% of Hungary's national wealth had been destroyed, and its income was less than half its pre-war level. Prices then rose faster than anywhere ever recorded, doubling about every 15 hours at the peak. The new currency of August 1946, the forint, was set at 200,000 tax pengő each. This chapter could not find how much of Hungary's spending in 1946 was paid by printing. Secondary accounts point to the budget gap, the cost of rebuilding, war payments and weak tax income.

[64.1 p] Argentina. A senior official of Argentina's central bank has said that over two decades, the central bank moved financing in pesos and reserves worth more than 60% of a year's output to the Treasury, by the bank's own count. In 2023 its direct advances and transfers to the Treasury came to about 0.9% of output, plus about 2.7% through buying government bonds in the market. In 2024 they stopped.
