## 1. H4. Imported inflation and deflation

### 1a. Malaysia 1997 to 1998: the ringgit fell far, prices rose much less

Plain words. When the ringgit buys fewer dollars, imported goods cost more ringgit. How much of that reaches the shop shelf, and how fast, is called exchange rate pass-through. Full pass-through means a 10% fall in the currency lifts the import price by 10%. Consumer prices move much less, because only part of what people buy is imported, and because shops and importers can choose to absorb part of the cost.

Price controls and subsidies in 1998, a correction to a common view. Claim: "Price controls and subsidies held prices down in 1998." What BNM itself wrote: administered prices covered about 11% of the CPI basket (10.8% for 10 items: white sugar, wheat flour, cement excluded, petrol, diesel, condensed milk, LPG, bread, chicken, cooking oil, plus locally produced rice at 2.8% controlled by law). BNM wrote "there is no price controls as such, contrary to general perceptions" and, except for LPG, "no subsidy element in the system". The government in fact approved higher ceilings: cooking oil +5% and chicken +10% from mid-December 1997, flour +20%, sugar +21% and milk +6% from 1 Feb 1998. These five items (about 6% of the basket) added nearly 1.1 points to inflation. Source: BNM AR 1998, ch. 1, verified. The IMF in April 1998 still asked Malaysia to scale price controls back to "basic food products consumed by the poorest households" (PIN 98/31, verified), so the two institutions described the same system with different weight.

Reading for the chapter. The main brake on pass-through in 1998 was the slump itself (weak demand, firms cutting margins) and falling world oil and commodity prices, not subsidies. Rival causes for the low inflation: recession, cheap oil (BNM: US$13.00 a barrel in Q4 1998 against US$20.90 a year earlier), low inflation in trading partners, then the RM3.80 peg from September 1998.

Why Malaysia's rate was lower, with rival causes named:
1. Administered fuel prices. RON95 petrol and diesel were held at fixed retail prices through 2022 (see 1d), so the world oil spike did not reach the pump. BNM credits "price controls, subsidies" (EMR 2022, verified).
2. Malaysia is a net energy and palm oil producer, so high commodity prices also raised government and export income, which paid for the subsidies (EMR 2022 notes petroleum revenue was expected to be 27.3% of government revenue in 2022, verified).
3. The ringgit fell only 5.4% over 2022, and BNM's pass-through is partial (1b).
4. Spare capacity after the pandemic (BNM names it).
5. Demand differences. The US had large pandemic fiscal transfers and a tight labour market. The UK was hit by a gas price shock. These are rival causes that have nothing to do with being open or closed.
Against a simple "Malaysia is protected by its exchange rate" story: the UK also used a large energy subsidy. ONS estimated that without the Energy Price Guarantee, CPI in October 2022 would have been about 13.8% rather than 11.1% (ONS Oct 2022 bulletin, secondary via search snippet). So subsidies capped prices in both places. Malaysia's were set earlier and covered motor fuel.

Side note found in passing: Malaysia's annual inflation was 5.4% in 2008 (World Bank via FRED, verified), slightly above the 1998 figure of 5.27% in the same series and the highest of 1995 to 2024. The 2008 jump coincided with a large administered petrol price rise in June 2008 (memory, not checked). This is a case where ending a buffer, not the exchange rate, drove the price jump.

Reading for H4. In 2022 the buffer worked: fuel prices did not move at the pump, and BNM says so. The cost was moved from the shopper to the state budget (RM37 to 45 billion on petroleum products in one year). The RON95 "rationalisation" turned out to lower the subsidised pump price for citizens, so 2025 is not a clean test of removing a buffer. The best test of buffer removal is June 2008, when administered fuel prices rose 39% to 63% and inflation reached 5.4% for the year (World Bank series), higher than in 1998. For the chapter: a subsidy does not stop imported inflation. It decides who pays for it and when.

Hong Kong. Prices fell every year from 1999 to 2004 under the US$ link.
- HKMA material says falling import prices reflected world commodity prices and "the relative strength of our currency derived from the strength of the US dollar through the Linked Exchange Rate" (HKMA viewpoint "Deflation (1): Causes", search snippet, https://www.hkma.gov.hk/media/eng/publication-and-research/reference-materials/viewpoint/doc/deflation1_causes_eng.doc , secondary).
- HKMA's own research found the currency board "only one of a number of contributory factors", with price convergence with the Mainland as another (HKMA Quarterly Bulletin Aug 2002, search snippet, secondary).
- HKMA Chief Executive Joseph Yam, 20 Sep 2001: "Hong Kong's monetary arrangements have not been contributing to deflation", and the real effective exchange rate had "depreciated since the crisis period in 1998 by about 12%" (https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/news-and-media/insight/2001/09/20010920 , verified). He named the US slowdown and Mainland integration as causes.
- IMF 1998: consumer prices began falling in July 1998 (IMF mission statement 30 Oct 1998, search snippet, secondary).
- Against a simple "the peg imports US prices" story: US inflation was positive (1.5% to 3.4%) every year Hong Kong had deflation. A peg fixes the exchange rate, not the price level. What Hong Kong could not do was let its currency fall to cheapen itself, as neighbours did, so the adjustment came through falling local prices, wages and property values. Rival causes: the property crash after 1997, Mainland price convergence, the dot-com slowdown in 2001, SARS in 2003.

Argentina. Under convertibility, prices fell in 1999, 2000 and 2001.
- IMF Independent Evaluation Office (July 2004): "Deflation and output contraction set in", and a needed real depreciation was blocked after "the sustained appreciation of the U.S. dollar and the devaluation of the Brazilian real in early 1999" (https://www.imf.org/external/np/ieo/2004/arg/eng/index.htm , verified). Default came in December 2001, and convertibility ended in early January 2002. Prices then rose 25.9% in 2002, the imported inflation of a sudden devaluation.
- Rival causes the IEO names: a fiscal loosening in the 1999 election year and loss of market financing from late 2000.

Indonesia against Malaysia, 1998. The same regional shock gave 58.4% inflation in Indonesia and 5.3% in Malaysia. This is the strongest single sign that pass-through depends on more than the size of the fall: Indonesia's rupiah fell far more (not checked here, memory), its banking system and government collapsed, and rice and food supply were disrupted. Malaysia had weak demand and imposed capital controls and the peg in September 1998.

Reading. The sources support a weak form: a small open economy that creates money faster than its trading partners, or cuts rates far below theirs, risks capital outflow, a falling currency and imported inflation, and so has less room than the US. They do not support a strong form ("it can never create money"). In 2020 several emerging central banks bought bonds without a currency fall. The condition the IMF names is credibility, not size alone.
