## The short version

### No single heir, a Europe that slides but does not fall, and money that goes West

The owner asked: if everyone is praying for a US downfall, who takes over, China as it is or China with open capital controls? And if the US has no problems, does the EU have to fall, and where would its money go? Round 4's answer to the first question is that nobody takes over whole. Since 2000 the dollar's share of allocated reserves fell from about 71% to 56.70% (Q2 2026), but the yuan holds only 2.11%. The lost share went to gold (now about 26% of all reserves at market prices, worth about 1.8 times all euro reserves) and to a crowd of smaller currencies. China as it is cannot inherit, because a reserve currency must let money leave freely. A China that opened would first send money into dollars, and even at the fastest peaceful pace on record it would need 9 to 18 years after opening to reach 10% of reserves. The premise fails too: the three largest creditor nations, Germany, China and Japan, would lose most from a US crash, and what states actually do is hedge, not pray.

Europe is not falling. It is sliding slowly against the US, with one sharp edge. In purchasing power, EU income per head has held at about 70% to 75% of the US level since 2000 (72% in 2026). The viral 'from 80% to 50%' is an exchange-rate effect from the 2008 peak. The western core did slip (Italy from 87% to 70% of the US, France from 79% to 72%), and German industrial output is 15.5% below 2017. The sharp edge is France: its 10-year yield closed at 5.05% on 23 October, 171.8bp over Germany's and above Italy's, while the ECB has raised rates to 2.50% into 3.8% inflation and Marine Le Pen leads the 2027 market at 46%. The desk puts a France- or Italy-led euro crisis at 20% by 2028, a country leaving the euro at 0.5% and 'dirt poor' at effectively zero. Breaking away does not help on the evidence: Brexit cost the UK about 6% to 8% of GDP per head, and the best position is Poland's or Sweden's, inside the single market with its own currency.

The X thread that prompted the question gets trends right and end states wrong. Of 21 checkable claims in its replies, 6 are confirmed, 11 partly true and 4 false. About 13% of the 142 collected replies were hateful or ethnic content and were excluded as evidence. The zero-sum premise ('if the US is fine, the EU must fall') is refuted: both economies and both stock markets grew in 2025, and they are each other's biggest customers and financiers. What is zero-sum is shares, not wealth.

Where Europe's money goes depends on what kind of money it is. Financial capital goes West, to the US above all (the desk's model puts about 55% of Europe's net outbound capital in North America in 2028 to 2035), so the owner is right on direction. He is wrong to rank Latin America with North America (about 6%). The Southeast Asian belief is right only for factories. Markets agree with this picture: no market above $1M traded differs from the desk by more than 15 points. The scenario odds stay at 62/15/1/22, 44/23/3/30 and 31/28/10/31, with a display split of the neutral layer and one clean set of France conditionals.

### If the US weakens, who inherits the money center? (r4-succession)

The heir to a weaker dollar is a crowd, not a king: gold, the euro and a set of smaller currencies, with the yuan held back by China's own capital controls. And almost nobody who matters is actually praying for a US crash.

#### R4-1. If everyone prays for a US downfall, who takes over: China as it is, or China if it opens its capital controls?

**Answer.** Nobody takes over whole. If the US weakens, its role passes to a crowd, not a king: the dollar stays first but smaller, gold takes the 'cannot be frozen' slice, the euro holds or edges up, and the yuan grows only inside China's own trade orbit. The data already look like this. The dollar's share of allocated reserves fell from about 71% in 2000 to 56.70% in Q2 2026, yet the yuan holds only 2.11%, gold rose to about 26% of all reserves at market prices, and smaller currencies such as the Canadian and Australian dollars took about 11%. China as it is cannot inherit, because a reserve currency must let money leave freely and China's model depends on stopping it. A China that opened its capital account would first push money out into dollars (about 60% likely in the first two years), and at the fastest peaceful pace on record it would need about 9 to 18 years after opening to reach 10% of world reserves. Reaching 20% would need a war-scale shock to the dollar as well.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 75. **Horizon:** H1 for a visible multipolar shift, H2 for any rival above 20%.

China as it is: about 35% of China's own trade is now settled in yuan and Chinese banks lend abroad mostly in yuan, so a yuan orbit is real. Outside that orbit the yuan is about 1% of international debt securities, China's CIPS payment system moves about one twentieth of what the US CHIPS system moves, residents may move only $50,000 a year abroad, and the yuan's reserve share fell from 2.8% (2022) to 2.11%. China opened (the stated case): a 10% share needs about $1.24T of yuan reserve assets, 2.6 times all foreign holdings in China's interbank bond market today. If China opened in 2030, 10% arrives about 2039 at the euro's 1999 to 2009 pace or about 2048 at the yen's 1980 to 1991 pace. Desk odds: China fully opens by 2028, 2035 and 2050: 1, 8, 25. Yuan above 10% of reserves: 0.5, 2, 8 (8 by 2035 and 35 by 2050 if it opens by 2030).

The other candidates each miss a pillar. The euro is the only currency with enough depth, but EU joint debt is about €849B, about 3% of the US Treasury market, and its weak link (France) is inside the house. Japan has deep markets but a central bank that owns about half its government bonds and depends on a US alliance. India is young and fast-growing but keeps capital controls. The Gulf pegs to the dollar and asked Washington for a swap line in 2026, so it is part of the dollar system. Gold cannot invoice trade or fund firms, and Bitcoin ($1.64T) has almost no official holders. The dollar's daily plumbing moved the other way: it was on one side of 89% of FX trades in April 2025 (up from 88%), and dollar credit to borrowers outside the US reached $14.7T in March 2026, up 7.3% in a year. When the US itself was the shock in April 2025, money ran to the euro (+13.8%), the yen and gold, not to the yuan.

Desk odds: the dollar's share below 50% at any quarterly print by 2028, 2035 and 2050: 2, 25, 50. The dollar still the largest reserve currency at each horizon end: 99, 97, 90. Any single currency overtaking the dollar by 2050: 5. On the six-pillar scorecard (produce, protect, promise, plumbing, people, place) the US scores 27 of 30, an opened China 21, the euro area and China as it is 18 each. No rival is within 6 points.

**Three views.**
- Governance: A successor needs all six pillars, and the US is the only economy strong on all six. The real threat to the dollar is self-inflicted damage to 'promise' (Fed independence, fair treatment of foreign holders) and overuse of sanctions. For Beijing and Brussels the lesson is the same: to become a money center you must give up control, by opening the capital account or by building a common safe asset.
- Business: Plan for a dollar-first, multi-rail world. Invoice and borrow in dollars by default, keep euro and local-currency options in Europe and inside China's trade orbit, and expect two or three currencies to matter instead of one. A dollar crisis would raise hedging costs, not hand you a new single currency.
- Ordinary person: If the US weakened, your dollars would buy less abroad, but you would not wake up paid in yuan. The change would be slow: more prices quoted in several currencies, more gold in central bank vaults, more payment apps that switch currencies for you.

#### R4-2. Is it true that 'everyone is praying for US downfall'? Who would gain and who would lose?

**Answer.** No, not as a description of what states do. Russia and Iran want it and say so. China wants a relatively weaker US and a sanctions-proof yuan, not a crash that would wreck its $3.4T of reserves and its biggest market. Europe, Japan, Korea, the Gulf and most of Southeast Asia rely on US security and US markets and would be hurt most by a disorderly break. The three largest creditor nations (Germany, China and Japan) and the holders of $9.33T of foreign-held Treasuries would take the first losses. What the money shows is hedging, not praying: central banks buy gold, foreign governments sold $254B of Treasuries in the 2026 crisis to raise cash, and several states build back-up payment rails, while dollar credit to the rest of the world keeps growing. A slow US decline helps gold holders, sanctioned states and emerging-market borrowers in dollars. A fast one helps almost nobody.

- **Status:** refuted. **Confidence:** 75. **Horizon:** H0 to H1.

Who gains and who loses in a disorderly break: foreign Treasury holders (Japan $1.07T, the UK above $1T, China $590.7B) lose from price falls and frozen collateral. Creditor nations lose wealth and their export markets at once. Exporters to the US (China, the EU, Mexico, Japan, Korea, Vietnam) lose demand. Emerging-market dollar borrowers (about $4.4T of dollar credit) lose first, because a crisis creates a dollar shortage as in 2008 and 2020, and gain only later. Gold holders and sanctioned states gain. US households lose through inflation and higher rates. Larry Summers called this mutual dependence a 'balance of financial terror': the actors able to push the dollar over are the ones who would be hit first.

The markets tested the 'US downfall' trade in real time. The euro peaked at 1.2018 dollars on 28 January 2026 and gold at $5,318 a day later. Since then the dollar index is up 3.6% for the year, the S&P 500 is up 11.5% and US 10-year yields rose from 4.16% to 4.99%. Decoded grades: 'everyone wants the US to fall' is folklore (D3) for most states and stated intent (D1) only for Russia and Iran. A coordinated official exit from Treasuries by 2035: 3%.

**Three views.**
- Governance: Treat rival rhetoric and allied hedging as different things. Allies buying gold and building back-up rails are insuring against US policy risk, which Washington can lower by protecting Fed independence and using sanctions sparingly.
- Business: Do not position for a coordinated sell-off of US assets. Position for slow diversification: more gold in reserves, more euro and local-currency invoicing at the edges, and periodic dollar rallies in a crisis.
- Ordinary person: A US crash would not make the rest of the world richer. It would hit pensions, exports and jobs almost everywhere, including in the countries said to be hoping for it.

#### R4-12. Is gold now a bigger reserve asset than the euro?

**Answer.** Yes, at today's prices. Central banks hold more than 36,000 tonnes of gold, worth about $4.64T at about $4,006 an ounce. That is about 26% of all reserves (foreign currency plus gold) and about 1.8 times all euro reserves (about $2.56T). The ECB itself reported in June 2026 that at end 2025 gold (27% of global official reserves) had passed both the euro (15%) and US Treasuries (22%). Gold stays bigger than euro reserves unless its price falls below about $2,208 an ounce, so the desk gives 90, 80 and 70 that gold is still worth more than all euro reserves at the end of 2028, 2035 and 2050.

- **Status:** confirmed. **Confidence:** 85. **Horizon:** H0 (share at today's prices), H1 to H2 for the odds.

Much of the rise is price, not tonnes. At end-2023 prices gold would be about 15% of reserves. Gold rose 64.4% in 2025, peaked at $5,318 in January 2026 and is down 7.6% this year. Buying also slowed: central banks reported about 130 tonnes in January to July 2026 against about 160 a year earlier, though China has bought for 23 straight months. The driver since 2022 is the freezing of Russia's reserves, which made an asset with no issuer and no counterparty uniquely attractive to states that fear sanctions.

Gold inherits a role, not the crown. It can store value that nobody can freeze if held at home, but it cannot invoice trade, fund firms or settle payments quickly. Desk odds that gold is above 25% of total reserves at horizon end: 55, 50, 50 (the breakeven price is about $3,800 today and rises as currency reserves grow).

**Three views.**
- Governance: Reserve managers have already voted for a neutral layer. Any euro or yuan ambition has to compete with gold for the 'safe from sanctions' slice, not only with the dollar.
- Business: Gold demand from central banks is a structural bid, but the share swings with price. A 20% fall in gold would erase most of the share gain without a single tonne being sold.
- Ordinary person: Gold's new status is a sign of distrust between governments, not a forecast that money will go back to gold coins. Your bank account stays in your national currency.

### Is the EU falling, and would breaking away help? (r4-europe)

Europe is sliding slowly against the US, not collapsing. The sharp edge is France, the best catch-up story is Poland, and on the evidence breaking away costs more than it gains.

#### R4-3. Will the EU fall, what would 'fall' mean, and how likely is each version?

**Answer.** The EU is not heading for a fall. It is sliding slowly against the US, with one sharp edge in France. Measured in purchasing power, EU income per head has stayed at about 70% to 75% of the US level since 2000 (72% in 2026), because the east caught up while the western core slipped: Italy fell from 87% to 70% of the US, France from 79% to 72% and Germany from 88% to 83%. The viral 'from 80% to 50%' is an exchange-rate effect measured from the 2008 peak, when the euro was near 1.47 dollars. 'Fall' can mean very different things, with these desk odds by end 2028, 2035 and 2050: slow relative decline 65, 70, 60. A euro sovereign crisis led by France or Italy 20, 32, 45. A country leaving the EU 1, 5, 10. A country leaving the euro 0.5, 3, 6. A breakup of the euro under 0.5, 2, 4. 'Dirt poor', or a big member falling to middle income, is effectively zero (under 0.5 to 2 by 2050), because Italy would need to lose about two thirds of its income relative to the World Bank line.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 70. **Horizon:** H0 for France, H1 to H2 for the slide.

The slide is real and measurable. IMF October 2026 growth for 2026: US 2.3%, EU 1.4%, euro area 1.1%, France 0.4%. Since 2008 the US grew 45.9%, the EU 24.5% and Italy 3.6%. German industrial output is 15.5% below its 2017 level, chemicals 24% and cars 28% below. EU industry pays about twice the US price for electricity and about four times for gas. The EU hosts about 5% of the world's AI computing power against 78% in the US. Old-age spending (10.6% of GDP) already exceeds the whole public payroll (10.2%), and the ratio of people over 65 to working-age people rises from 34.5 per 100 (2025) to 48.2 (2045).

The sharp edge is France. Its 10-year yield closed at 5.05% on 23 October, 171.8bp over the German Bund (174.6bp the day before, 135.2bp on 15 October), and it pays more than Italy and Greece. The ECB is raising rates (deposit rate 2.50% since 16 September) into 3.8% inflation, the budget vote is still open, Moody's France decision is pending, and Marine Le Pen leads the 2027 race at 46% on a $155.7M market.

Against a fall: euro support hit a record 83% in 2025, about three quarters of Europeans say membership helped their country, Hungary voted out its most EU-hostile government in April 2026, and markets price a discount, not a collapse (Italy's spread about 115bp, Spain's about 64bp, against more than 500bp for Italy in 2011).

**Three views.**
- Governance: Plan for a French stress test in 2027, not a European collapse. The binding constraints are energy prices, pension arithmetic and a missing common safe asset, and none of them needs an exit to fix.
- Business: Europe is a high-income, slow-growth, high-energy-cost market. That is good for consumer and defence suppliers and bad for energy-intensive production and AI compute. The hedge that matters is French and Italian spread risk, not a euro breakup.
- Ordinary person: Living standards are not collapsing. Wages in the western core will keep losing ground to the US, and the realistic bad case is a recession with higher borrowing costs in 2027 to 2028, not your euros turning into lira.

#### R4-4. If a country breaks away from the EU, does it stand a better chance? Brexit, leaving the euro, and the Poland and Sweden model.

**Answer.** On the evidence, no for leaving the EU, mostly no for leaving the euro, and yes for staying in the EU but outside the euro if a country joined from strength. Brexit cost the UK about 6% to 8% of GDP per head by 2025 (an NBER study built with Bank of England survey data), about 4% of long-run productivity and about 15% of trade intensity (OBR), and the UK grew 13.5% from 2016 to 2026 against 18.1% for the EU and 14.6% for the euro area. Switzerland and Norway are rich, but both sit inside the single market by treaty and were rich before any EU question. Leaving the euro would mean a bank run and capital controls before any gain: Greece lost about €41B of deposits in 2015 and still chose to stay. A model of Italy back in lira shows its debt ending higher than on the euro path within six to seven years. The best position on the data is Poland's or Sweden's: in the single market, with its own currency. But the currency is not what made them win: Lithuania in the euro converged as fast as Poland outside it, and Hungary with its own currency lagged.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 70. **Horizon:** H1 to H2.

Candidate by candidate: Poland would be worse off leaving the EU and is already in its best position. The Netherlands, a low-debt trade hub, would lose market access and gain little from its own currency. Finland is the only plausible gainer from leaving the euro in theory (it grew 6.0% from 2008 to 2026 against Sweden's 35.1%), but the exit cost exceeds the gain. Italy would be worse off in the transition and its debt worse in the model. Hungary would be much worse off without EU funds and single-market factories. France would face a run on its banks first. Germany would see a new mark revalue and hurt its exporters.

Odds and markets: a member leaving the EU by 2028, 2035 and 2050: 1, 5, 10. Leaving the euro: 0.5, 3, 6. Every prediction market on a country leaving the EU trades under $10,000 at 1% to 8%, and there is no market on leaving the euro. Markets give the UK 3% to 10% of rejoining the EU by 2035 (under $1,000 traded).

**Three views.**
- Governance: Exit is a cost paid up front for a gain that never showed up in the UK data. The lever that worked for Poland and Lithuania was institutions plus single-market access, not the currency.
- Business: On exit, firms lose the single market at once, while any competitiveness gain from a cheaper currency takes years. Brexit moved booking entities to Paris, Frankfurt and Dublin and listings to New York.
- Ordinary person: The people who pay for an exit are depositors and wage earners in the first two years, through bank limits, a weaker currency and higher prices.

#### R4-10. Is France now the euro area's weak link?

**Answer.** Yes, for now. France pays more than Italy and Greece to borrow for ten years, its deficit is about 5.4% of GDP with debt at 118.5% and rising to 123.6% by 2031 (IMF), its 2027 budget vote is still open, and the 2027 presidential favourite on the deepest market is Marine Le Pen at 46%. The gap between French and German 10-year yields closed at 174.6bp on 22 October and 171.8bp on 23 October, the widest since the 2011 to 2012 euro crisis and up from about 55bp in February. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.50% in September into 3.8% inflation and has said France must fix its own budget, because its bond-buying backstop (the Transmission Protection Instrument, TPI) requires compliance with EU fiscal rules. The desk puts a France- or Italy-led euro crisis at 20% by end 2028, about 70% of it France-led. The base case is a political climbdown, as Italy made in 2022, not an exit.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 70. **Horizon:** H0.

The arithmetic is tight. With nominal growth near 2.8%, France needs a primary surplus of about 2.6% of GDP to stabilise its debt at today's 5.05% yield, against a primary deficit of about 3.3% now. With no consolidation and borrowing costs drifting up as old debt rolls over, the model path runs from 121% of GDP in 2027 to 161% in 2036. Moody's rates France Aa3 with a negative outlook and was due to decide on 23 October, but no decision had been published at the time of writing. Fitch and S&P already rate it A+.

It is still a French problem, not a euro problem. The German Bund is not acting as a haven (its yield is rising with France's), Italy's spread is about 115bp and Spain's about 64bp, and French 5-year default insurance (81bp to 87bp in early October) is far below euro-crisis levels. The desk lines to watch are 200bp on the French spread for five sessions and 150bp on Italy's.

**Three views.**
- Governance: The ECB has put the burden on Paris. A credible budget is the cheapest way to make the backstop available, and an open clash with EU fiscal rules after the 2027 election is the path to a real crisis.
- Business: Price French risk separately from euro risk. The concentrated exposures are French government bonds, French banks and the CAC 40 (down 4.5% this year), not European assets in general.
- Ordinary person: For French households the risk is higher borrowing costs and possible tax rises, not losing the euro. Insured deposits up to €100,000 are protected by law.

#### R4-11. Is Poland the model to copy, and is it safe?

**Answer.** Poland is Europe's best catch-up story, but it is not fiscally safe. Its income per head in purchasing power rose from 52% of the EU average in 2004 to 79% in 2024, and from 33% to 63% of the US level between 2000 and 2026. Its economy grew 82% from 2008 to 2026 against 25% for the EU, and Polish shares rose about 69% in dollars in 2025 and 24% this year. But Poland ran the EU's largest deficit in 2025 (7.2% of GDP) and plans 7.1% for 2027, its public spending rose from 41.4% to 50.9% of GDP in six years (defence is part of it), its 10-year yield rose above 6% in September, and Fitch rates it A- with a negative outlook. The X thread's 'France goes first, Poland is safe' is half right: France is the market's weak link today, and Poland's growth is real, but its budget is the EU's most stretched.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 70. **Horizon:** H0 to H1.

What made Poland work was single-market access, EU funds and institutions, not the zloty alone. Lithuania (euro) gained as many points against the EU average since 2004 as Poland (38 each), and Hungary, also with its own currency, gained only 12. Its own currency did help in shocks such as 2009, which is why the 'in the EU, outside the euro' model is attractive. The test is the October 2027 parliamentary election and whether the deficit falls while defence spending stays high.

**Three views.**
- Governance: Copy the institutions and the single-market strategy, not the deficit. Defence at more than 3.5% of GDP is a deliberate choice, and it needs a tax base to match.
- Business: Poland is a growth market with fiscal risk: strong earnings and banks, but bond yields above 6% and a weaker zloty in 2026 (EUR/PLN 4.40).
- Ordinary person: Poles have gained more than almost anyone in Europe since 2004. The risk ahead is higher taxes or slower public spending, not a reversal of the catch-up.

### The X thread against the data, and the zero-sum premise (r4-thread)

The thread is a source of questions, not evidence. Its trends mostly check out, its end states do not, and its core premise, that one side must fall for the other to rise, fails the test.

#### R4-5. Is the X thread right that Europe will become very poor, and what is the crowd really asking?

**Answer.** Wrong on the end state, right on several trends. John Loeber (2026-10-22) framed a 'very poor Europe' in 10 to 20 years as an extreme case with a small but growing probability. Johannes Koenraadt's quote (2026-10-23) dropped the hedge and stated it as certain. Tested against official data, of 21 checkable claims in the replies 6 are confirmed, 11 partly true and 4 false, and one more could not be checked. The crowd gets trends right: Chinese brands took a record 11.7% of EU car sales in August 2026, German and Italian power is costly, Europe has about 5% of world AI compute, old-age spending now exceeds the whole public payroll, fertility is very low and France is the weak link. It gets end states wrong: income relative to the US has not halved in purchasing power, the public payroll has not 'ballooned' (11.0% of GDP in 1995, 10.3% in 2025), the EU is not the world's largest consumer market, the UK does not take half of European venture capital, and 'middle income' would need France's income to fall by about two thirds. About 13% of the 142 collected replies (11% of likes) were hateful or ethnic content. They were counted only and excluded as evidence.

- **Status:** refuted. **Confidence:** 75. **Horizon:** H0 for France and energy, H1 to H2 for the decline question.

The crowd's real questions are better than its answers: Where should we go, the US? Which country gets richer if everyone gets poorer, and who will China sell to? Will Europeans move to Latin America as Italians did a century ago? Is this real poverty, a slide to middle income, or just stagnation? Does France go first, and is Poland safe? Where will the data centers go with these power prices? Are these hard limits or political choices? Will AI wealth concentrate in the US and China? Is 'Europe' even one thing? One legitimate fiscal question, what new residents cost or contribute to the state, has official studies behind it (Danish and Dutch) and should be answered only with that data. It was not tested this round.

The crowd is far darker than prices. Only 16% of replies, holding 1.6% of likes, pushed back. Markets price stagnation and a French problem: European shares beat US shares in dollars in 2025 (+32.5% against +16.4%), Polish shares rose about 69%, the US valuation premium is near its ten-year median, and the CAC 40 is down this year. Households are gloomier than markets (euro-area consumer confidence -17.9 in October, 29% expect living standards to fall, 44% in France) but they are saving more, not fleeing.

Decoded 'hidden plan' claims: the EU as a joint project with Moscow is not supported (D4, under 3%). 'States are preparing to rob the middle class' is folklore as stated (D3), but higher taxes on capital income are real and openly stated (D1): the UK raised dividend tax from April 2026 and savings tax from April 2027, and France's flat tax on capital income is 31.4% in 2026. The desk gives 75 that at least two of the UK, France, the Netherlands and Germany enact further capital-income or inheritance tax rises by end 2028.

**Three views.**
- Governance: The measurable constraints are specific (power prices, pension spending, the French deficit, slow AI build-out), not a general culture story. Fixing grid capacity and pension indexation would move more than any of the thread's proposals.
- Business: Read the thread as a sentiment signal from US tech-adjacent investors, who have been bearish on Europe for years, including in 2025 when European shares beat US shares.
- Ordinary person: The realistic risk for an ordinary European is higher taxes on savings income, later retirement and higher energy bills, not becoming poor. Know your own country's rules before acting on a viral post.

#### R4-6. Is it true that 'if the US has no issues, the EU has to fall'?

**Answer.** No. The US and Europe are not on a seesaw where one must fall for the other to rise. In 2025 both stock markets rose strongly (European shares +32.5% in dollars, the S&P 500 +16.4%), and since 2008 both economies grew in real terms (US 45.9%, EU 24.5%). They are each other's biggest customers and financiers: the US bought $633.2B of EU goods in 2025, the US hosts 28.3% of the EU's direct investment abroad, and Europe holds $16.2T of US securities, 46% of all foreign holdings. A Europe in crisis would hurt US exporters, US banks and the Treasury's biggest foreign buyers. What is zero-sum is narrower: shares. Reserve shares add up to 100%, and a euro of savings invested in New York (about €300B a year by the European Commission's count) is not invested in Frankfurt. So a strong US can pull capital and talent from a slow Europe, which makes Europe relatively poorer, not absolutely poorer.

- **Status:** refuted. **Confidence:** 80. **Horizon:** H1.

The evidence of relative, not absolute, decline is the flat purchasing-power ratio: about 70% to 75% of US income per head for 25 years, while both sides got richer. The desk gives only 6% that EU real income per head is lower in 2028 than in 2025, and 8% by 2050. The April 2025 episode also shows the link runs both ways: when US policy was the shock, the euro rose 13.8% in six months.

Where it can turn more zero-sum: the race for AI compute (the US hosts about 78% of the world's AI compute and the EU about 5%), tariffs, and capital-market pull. A euro crisis would also push money into Treasuries and lower US borrowing costs for a while, as in 2010 to 2012. On intent, the US National Security Strategy of December 2025 decodes to wanting a weaker EU as a rule-maker and a stronger Europe as an arms buyer (D2, 60), not an impoverished Europe (D3, 10).

**Three views.**
- Governance: Relative decline is a policy problem Europe can work on (energy, capital markets, compute). Framing it as a zero-sum fight with the US invites the wrong remedies, such as trade wars against Europe's biggest customer.
- Business: The biggest European firms earn a large share of their profits in the US. A strong US economy is good for them even when it pulls capital out of European markets.
- Ordinary person: Your job in Europe does not depend on America doing badly. It depends far more on energy prices, interest rates and whether your employer can sell into the US and China.

### Where Europe's money, factories, people and the wealthy would go (r4-flows)

Money goes to the deepest safe market, factories to cost and customers, people to the same language, and the wealthy to tax and safety. The four flows give four different answers.

#### R4-7. Where does Europe's money go if the US holds and the EU declines: to Southeast Asia, as many there believe, or back to the West, North America and Latin America, as the owner argues?

**Answer.** Mostly West, but 'West' means the US, Switzerland, the UK and Canada, not Latin America. Split the flows and the answer changes by flow. Money (pensions, insurers, funds, savers) goes to the deepest safe market: Europe already holds $16.2T of US securities, 3.8 times its 2008 level, and the desk's model puts about 55% (range 45% to 65%) of Europe's net outbound capital in North America in 2028 to 2035, 12% in the UK, Switzerland and Norway, 15% in all of Asia, 6% in Latin America, 3% in the Gulf, 2% in Africa and under 1% in Central Asia and Russia. Factories follow cost, energy and customers, so here the Southeast Asian belief is partly right: Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and India score as high as the US for new plants, and Asia should take about a quarter of relocated European capacity. People move short distances to the same language (Germans to Switzerland, Austria and Spain). The wealthy go to tax and safety: Switzerland, Italy, Portugal, Greece and the UAE before the US. Verdict: the owner is right on the direction of money (70) and wrong to rank Latin America with North America (80 that Latin America gets under 10%). The Southeast Asian belief is refuted for financial capital (85) and plausible for factories (55).

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 70. **Horizon:** H1, pattern already visible in H0.

Judged through culture (as measured), manufacturing, goods and regulation: language and law favour the US, UK, Canada and Switzerland for money and people, and Spanish, Portuguese and Italian ties give Latin America a real people channel (Argentina has the most registered Italians abroad, 989,901, and Spain was Mexico's second largest investor in 2025). Rule of law cuts the other way for Latin America: on the World Justice Project index Mexico scores 0.40 (rank 121 of 143) and Brazil 0.50, against 0.64 for the UAE, 0.68 for the US and 0.78 for Singapore. Manufacturing weight favours China (27.7% of world manufacturing value added), the US (17.3%) and Asia, while Mexico has 2.2%. Industrial energy costs about $22 per million BTU in the US and $18 in Indonesia, against $42 in the UK. Trade deals (CETA with Canada, EU-Mexico, EU-Mercosur signed on 17 January 2026) help goods trade, not portfolio money. Capital-market depth decides money: US market value is about 224% of GDP, Mexico's about 28%.

Southeast Asia's FDI boom is real but mostly Asian money landing in Singapore. The region took a record $244B in 2025, but more than 60% of ASEAN's inflows go to Singapore, East Asia supplies about 30% and the EU is only the third largest outside source. FDI into supply-chain manufacturing there halved from $31B to $14B in 2025. Latin America took $188B, but Mexico's record $40.9B was two thirds reinvested profits of firms already there, and Argentina's inflows fell 56%.

History points the same way. In the 1930s about $9B of gold fled to the US, $1.6B of it in twelve weeks around Munich. Hong Kongers before 1997 went to Canada, Australia, the UK and the US, all English-speaking and common law. Russians in 2022 went to the first open door (Armenia, Kazakhstan, Georgia, Israel, Dubai). After Brexit only about 7,000 finance jobs left London, to Paris, Frankfurt and Dublin. The rule: money goes to the deepest market, people to the same language, and both to the first open door.

**Three views.**
- Governance: Europe's real competitor for its own savings is the US market, not Asia. Keeping more of the €300B a year at home takes a deeper EU capital market and cheaper energy. For Latin America the opening is narrow: Mexico as a door into the US market and Mercosur if ratified.
- Business: Financial capital goes to New York. Plants go to the customer and to cheap power: the US to get inside its tariff wall, Southeast Asia and India for cost and Asian demand, Mexico if the USMCA review holds, Morocco and eastern Europe to serve Europe.
- Ordinary person: Most Europeans who move go somewhere near with the same language or legal system. The US takes top talent but is harder to enter. Latin America draws mainly people with family ties and Spanish speakers.

#### R4-8. The owner says the Middle East is messy, Africa faces civil wars, and Central Asia and Russia are too segregated. Where do the data support this, and where is it too broad?

**Answer.** Right about the averages, too broad about the countries. Middle East: supported for 2026, when the Iran war closed Hormuz for much of the year, Dubai was attacked and expats left in March. But the UAE was still the world's top magnet for millionaires in 2025 (+9,800), it took $48B of FDI (more than Mexico), its rule-of-law score (0.64) beats every Latin American country except Uruguay, Costa Rica and Chile, and Gulf funds are investing in Europe. Africa: supported for Nigeria, Sudan, the Sahel and eastern Congo. But Morocco shares Europe's time zone and sits outside major conflict zones, and Rwanda (0.63), Namibia (0.61), Botswana and Mauritius (0.60) outrank Brazil (0.50) and Mexico (0.40) on rule of law. Central Asia and Russia: supported for Russia (war, sanctions, the largest rule-of-law fall of any country in 2025), and Central Asia drew only $5B of FDI. But 'segregated' is the wrong word: the measurable problems are small markets, distance from the sea, Russian-language legal systems and sanctions exposure, and Kazakhstan (0.54) scores level with Argentina. The same tests cut against the owner's own pick: Mexico, Brazil and Ecuador are among the ten most violent countries in ACLED's 2025 index, and Mexico scores below Russia on rule of law.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 65. **Horizon:** H1.

On the desk's destination scorecard for financial capital (0 to 10, judgment on stated indicators): Switzerland 9.3, the US and UK 8.2, Singapore 8.1, Canada 8.0, Chile 6.3, the Gulf 6.0 at war-time security (6.5 at pre-war security), India 5.8, Morocco 5.0, Brazil 4.9, Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand together 4.8, Mexico 4.5, a mix of large African economies 4.4, and Central Asia and Russia 2.9. For the wealthy and retirees the Gulf would score 7.0 with its pre-war security, close to Canada.

Fairness note: these are judgments about institutions, security and markets, which change, not about peoples. Africa's 2025 FDI ($70B) was its third highest in 25 years, Egypt and Morocco host large projects, and the Gulf was the top destination for the mobile wealthy until a war, not culture, changed the picture.

**Three views.**
- Governance: Regional labels hide the countries that matter. Morocco, Rwanda, Botswana, Kazakhstan and the UAE each clear bars that Mexico and Brazil do not, and policy toward them should follow the measured data.
- Business: Screen countries, not continents. For a plant serving Europe, Morocco can beat Mexico. For a regional treasury, Dubai or Singapore can beat any Latin American city, war risk permitting.
- Ordinary person: If you are thinking of moving, look up the rule-of-law score, the safety data and the visa route for the specific country. Continental reputations are often years out of date.

### What markets price, and the desk's final odds (r4-markets)

Markets price a French problem and a slow European discount, not a collapse, and not a US downfall. No deep market disagrees with the desk by more than 15 points.

#### R4-9. What do markets and prediction markets price on these questions, and what are the desk's final odds?

**Answer.** Markets price a France problem, not a European collapse and not a US downfall. The deepest contract on these questions is France's 2027 presidential election ($155.7M traded), with Marine Le Pen at 46% and any National Rally candidate about 47%. France's 10-year yield closed at 5.05% on 23 October, 171.8bp over Germany (up from 135.2bp eight days earlier) and above Italy, but there is no breakup pattern: the Bund is not falling as a haven, Italy is about 115bp and Spain about 64bp. The 'US downfall' trade peaked in January 2026 and reversed: the dollar index is up 3.6% this year and the S&P 500 up 11.5%. No market above $1M traded differs from the desk by more than 15 points. The desk's final odds put the real risk in slow relative decline (60 to 70 at every horizon) and a France- or Italy-led euro crisis (20 by 2028), and give breakups and successions low single digits.

- **Status:** plausible. **Confidence:** 70. **Horizon:** H0 for markets, H1 to H2 for the base-rate table.

Rates: the ECB deposit rate is 2.50% after hikes in June and September 2026, euro-area inflation was 3.8% in September (Italy 4.2%, France 3.4%, Germany 3.3%), a hold on 29 October is priced at 97.85% and a December hike at 75.5% (thin). Equities: Europe's forward price-earnings ratio is 14.3 against 19.3 for the S&P 500, a US premium of 35%, close to its ten-year norm. Households are gloomier than markets but save rather than flee: the EU saving rate is 14.1%, 2.4 points above its 2015 to 2019 average.

Where markets and the desk differ by more than 15 points, the markets are thin. A Moody's or S&P downgrade of France by 31 October is priced at 90.5% on $10,200 against the desk's 70, because Moody's has held through earlier French crises. China overtaking US GDP by 2030 trades at 19% to 21% on $134,000 against the desk's 3, because China would need its dollar GDP to rise about 43% relative to the US in four years.

Scenario odds are unchanged: dollar extension, neutral reserve layer, energy or compute standard and disorderly reset stay at 62/15/1/22 to 2028, 44/23/3/30 to 2035 and 31/28/10/31 to 2050. The neutral layer is now shown split into gold and Bitcoin (13, 17, 18) and multipolar fiat currencies (2, 6, 10), and one clean set of French spread conditionals replaces the lanes' overlapping proposals.

**Three views.**
- Governance: The market is telling Paris, not Brussels, that it has a problem. The policy tests are the budget vote, the rating decision and the April 2027 election, and the ECB has made clear its backstop comes with conditions.
- Business: European assets are priced as a discount market, not a distressed one. The cheap-Europe trade is a valuation bet with a France-shaped tail, while US prices reflect resilience, not decline.
- Ordinary person: Prices do not say your euros or dollars are about to change. They say French borrowing is getting dearer and that European growth will stay slow, which shows up as higher loan rates and modest wage gains.
