## 1. Answers

### R4-1. If the US declines, who inherits the role of the world's money center?

**Answer.** Nobody inherits it whole. The most likely successor to a weaker dollar is a crowd, not a king: the dollar stays first but smaller, the euro holds or edges up, gold takes the "neutral, cannot be frozen" slice, and the yuan grows inside its own trade orbit. The data already look like this. The dollar's share of allocated reserves fell from 70.8% (2000) to 56.70% (Q2 2026), but almost none of that went to one rival: it went to gold (now about 26% of total reserves at market prices, worth about 1.8 times all euro reserves), to "other" currencies (CAD, AUD, KRW, SGD and others, 6.06% plus 4.72% for CAD and AUD), and only 2.11% sits in yuan. Meanwhile the dollar's grip on the plumbing tightened: it was on one side of 89% of FX trades in April 2025 (up from 88%), and dollar credit to borrowers outside the US hit $14.7T in March 2026, up 7.3% in a year. China as it is now cannot inherit, because a reserve currency must let money leave freely and China's model depends on stopping it. A China that opened its capital account (the stated case) would at first push money into dollars, and history (the yen after 1980, the euro after 1999) says a newly open currency gains about 0.4 to 0.9 points of reserve share a year, so 10% takes about 9 to 18 years after opening and 20% needs either about 20 years at euro speed or a war-scale shock to the dollar. The premise "everyone is praying for US downfall" fails the payoff test: the three largest creditor nations (Germany, China, Japan) and the holders of $9.33T of foreign-held Treasuries would take the first losses, and what the world actually does (buy gold, trim Treasuries, build back-up rails) is hedging, not praying.

- **Status:** Plausible (T0 reserve, FX, credit and gold data plus a coherent mechanism. The probabilities are judgment).
- **Confidence:** 75.
- **Horizon:** H1 for a visible multipolar shift (dollar share near or below 50%). H2 for any rival above 20%.
- **Three views:**
  - Governance: A successor needs all six pillars, and the US is the only economy that scores high on all six today (27 of 30 in this lane's scorecard against 18 for the euro area and 18 for China as it is). The real policy danger for the US is self-inflicted damage to "promise" (Fed independence, rule of law for foreign holders) and "plumbing" (sanctions overuse), because those are the pillars rivals cannot copy but US actions can erode. For Beijing and Brussels the lesson is the same trade-off: to be a money center you must give up control (open capital account, a common safe asset), and neither has been willing.
  - Business: Plan for a dollar-first, multi-rail world: invoice and borrow in dollars by default, keep euro and local-currency options in Europe and inside China's trade orbit, and treat gold as the one asset every central bank is still adding. A dollar crisis would not hand your business a new single currency. It would raise hedging costs and make two or three currencies necessary instead of one.
  - Ordinary person: If the US weakened, your dollar would buy less abroad, but you would not wake up paid in yuan. The likely change is slow: more prices quoted in several currencies, more gold in central bank vaults, and more payment apps that switch currencies for you. Savers outside the US would see their local currency rise against the dollar for a while, which helps importers and hurts exporters.

### Sub-answer A. What would "US downfall" mean for each dollar role?

Four very different things. Relative decline (the 2000 to 2026 pattern) erodes the reserve share by about 0.5 to 0.85 points a year while invoicing, FX trading and funding stay put or even grow. A dollar crisis (a 1971 to 1973 or April 2025 style fall) dents the reserve and safe-asset roles first and helps dollar debtors. A debt crisis (failed auctions, inflation or a technical default) first raises demand for dollars (debtors scramble, as in 2008 and March 2020) and only later damages the safe-asset role. Political breakdown (Fed capture, courts ignored, seizures of foreign assets) is the one that would move reserves fastest, because it attacks the "promise" pillar that everything else rests on. Status: Plausible. Confidence: 70. Horizon: H0 to H2.

### Sub-answer B. China as heir, now and opened

Now: no. Capital controls, a managed fixing, about RMB 3.2T of foreign holdings in a large bond market, CIPS volumes about one twentieth of CHIPS, and a 2.11% reserve share say so. China's own officials (Xi in Qiushi, Pan Gongsheng) state a goal of a "powerful currency" with reserve status in a "multipolar" system, not a dollar replacement. Opened: WIF's sequence holds with a refinement. The first effect is likely to help the dollar (outflows from about $25T of household deposits), but China in 2026 would be opening into strength (record surplus, yuan at 6.67), which is the UK 1979 pattern where the first wave was smaller. Even opened, 10% of reserves needs about $1.24T of foreign official yuan assets, 2.6 times all foreign holdings in the interbank bond market today. Status: Plausible. Confidence: 75. Horizon: H2.

### Sub-answer C. Other candidates

The euro is the only currency with the depth to absorb a big shift, but it has no single safe asset (EU joint debt about €849B, about 3% the size of the US marketable Treasury stock) and its weak link (France, OAT-Bund spread 157.5bp) is inside the house. Japan has depth but a central bank holding about 49% of its government bonds and an ally's security dependence. India has the youngest people but capital controls and a currency that hit record lows in 2026. The Gulf pegs to the dollar and asked Washington for a swap line in 2026, so it is part of the dollar system, not an exit from it. Gold is already the largest non-dollar reserve asset at market prices but cannot invoice trade or fund firms. Bitcoin ($81,343, $1.64T market value, about 35% of official gold's value) holds almost no official reserves. Status: Plausible. Confidence: 75. Horizon: H1 to H2.

### Sub-answer D. "Everyone is praying for US downfall"

Refuted as a description of what states do. Russia and Iran want it. China wants a relatively weaker US and a sanctions-proof yuan, not a crash that would wreck about $3.4T of reserves and its biggest market (Summers' "balance of financial terror"). Europe, Japan, Korea, the Gulf and most of Southeast Asia rely on US security and would lose the most from a disorderly break. What the money shows (T0) is hedging: gold buying, $254B of official Treasury sales in a crisis, and new back-up rails, while private dollar credit keeps growing. Status: Refuted (as stated). Plausible as "many hedge against US policy risk". Confidence: 75. Horizon: H0 to H1.

### Sub-answer E. Probabilities

See section 6. Headline: dollar share below 50% of allocated reserves by 2028: 4, by 2035: 30, by 2050: 55. Yuan above 10%: under 1, 2, 8. Euro above 25%: 3, 12, 20. Gold above euro in reserves at horizon end: 90, 80, 70. A single currency overtaking the dollar by 2050: 5. Dollar still the largest reserve asset among currencies in 2050: 90, which means the "no single successor" outcome is the main way the dollar loses ground.

## 2. Key findings

1. **Succession is already happening, but to a crowd.** Since 2000 the dollar lost about 14 points of reserve share. The euro is at 20.60%, about where it was at its launch decade, the yuan 2.11%, while "other" currencies plus CAD and AUD hold 10.78% and gold rose to about 26% of total reserves at market prices (R4A-01, R4A-07). The ECB said at end 2025 that gold (27%) had passed both Treasuries (22%) and the euro (15%) as a share of global official reserves (R4A-06).
2. **The plumbing roles are moving the other way.** The dollar's FX turnover share rose to 89% (2025 vs 88% in 2022), dollar credit to non-residents reached $14.7T, and Fed staff find about 60% of foreign-currency debt issuance and about 50% of non-euro SWIFT payments in dollars (R4A-03 to R4A-05). Reserves are the most visible role and the least important one for daily use.
3. **The real competitor in funding is the euro, not the yuan.** Euro credit to non-residents (€5.1T) grew 12% a year to March 2026 against 7.3% for dollar credit, and the euro's share of global foreign-currency credit rose from 22% (Q3 2022) to 28% (Q1 2026) (R4A-05). That is the one measurable succession trend outside gold.
4. **The April 2025 test.** When the US itself was the shock, the dollar fell 10.8% in H1 2025 (worst half since 1973), the euro rose 13.8% and the yen 9% (R4A-15). Money ran to the euro, the yen and gold, not to the yuan. That is a live test of who the market treats as the emergency heir.
5. **China's ceiling is set by control, not size.** China is the largest trading nation, but yuan reserve share has gone from 2.8% (2022) to about 2.1% (2026) (RBA, R4A-11). Its internationalization is real inside its own orbit (about 35% of China's trade settled in yuan, Chinese banks' overseas lending now majority yuan) and absent outside it (about 1% of international debt securities, CHIPS more than 20 times CIPS).
6. **History says succession takes decades and a shock.** The US economy passed Britain's in the 1870s, the Fed opened in 1913, the dollar passed sterling in reserves in the mid-1920s only after World War I, lost the lead again in the 1930s, and sterling still held over 20% of reserves in the mid-1960s and about 7% around 1970 (R4A-16, R4A-17). The fastest peaceful gains on record are about 0.9 points a year (euro 1999 to 2009) and 0.4 points a year (yen 1980 to 1991, peak 8.7%) (R4A-18).
7. **"Below 50%" is not the end of the dollar.** The dollar's share was lower than today before 1994 (Q3 2025 was called the lowest since 1994) and, in older IMF data, around 46% to 50% in 1990 to 1991 [memory], while the dollar remained the money center (R4A-02, R4A-30). A crossing of 50% would be a headline, not a regime change.
8. **The six-pillar scorecard is lopsided.** US 27, China opened 21, euro area 18, China now 18, Japan 17, India 16, Gulf 12 (section 4). No candidate is within 6 points of the US. The two pillars where rivals beat the US are "produce" (China) and "people" in the sense of young population (India).
9. **The losers from a dollar break are the creditors.** Germany (net external assets ¥675.5T), China (¥636.3T) and Japan (¥561.8T) are the three largest creditor nations (R4A-22), and Japan ($1.07T), the UK (above $1T) and China ($590.7B) are the top foreign Treasury holders (R4A-25). EM dollar debtors (about $4.4T of dollar credit) would gain from a slow dollar fall and lose badly from a dollar funding squeeze.
10. **"First effect helps the dollar" survives the test, with a caveat.** In three of four capital-account openings (Japan early 1980s, Korea 1990s, China 2015 to 2016) the first wave was outflows and a weaker home currency [memory for Japan and Korea]. The UK in 1979 opened into an oil windfall and high rates and sterling rose first [memory]. China in 2026 has a record surplus and a strong yuan, so the dollar-positive first wave would likely be smaller than WIF's 70% estimate implies. Revised: 60.

The plumbing is a network: a trader in Jakarta uses dollars because the other side does. That is why reserve share can fall for 25 years while FX share rises.

**Anomaly.** The PBoC calls the yuan the "third-largest payment currency" while SWIFT ranks it 5th or 6th. The gap comes from counting China's own cross-border flows (where yuan now beats the dollar) and trade finance. Both are true. Neither is a reserve role. (R4A-10)

That is a defensive success and a reserve-currency failure, and the second follows from the first: the same controls that make the orbit safe for Beijing make it unsafe for foreign savers.

**What history says.**
- Sterling to dollar took about 50 years from economic overtaking (1870s) to reserve overtaking (mid-1920s), then reversed in the 1930s, and about 25 more years after 1944 for sterling to fall from over half to about 7% (R4A-16, R4A-17).
- The dollar's 1914 to 1925 jump needed three things at once: a new central bank acting as market maker for trade acceptances, Britain's war debts and suspension of gold convertibility, and US creditor status. Its 1944 coronation needed Britain bankrupt and the US holding most of the world's monetary gold [memory].
- The rise came with policy support, not market forces alone (Eichengreen and Flandreau on trade credit, R4A-16). China's state is willing to supply the policy support (swap lines, CIPS, mBridge) but not the openness.
- Reserve currencies can share the stage: the dollar and sterling coexisted for decades (R4A-16). This supports the multipolar reading over a single-heir story.

## 10. Anomalies (7 moves)

1. **Gold, not a currency, is the biggest gainer from dollar diversification.** Default: a falling dollar share would show up as a rising euro or yuan share. Observed: euro flat, yuan down since 2022, gold up to about 26%. Incentive: sanctions risk after the 2022 freeze of Russian reserves makes an issuer-free asset uniquely attractive. Test: COFER yuan share and WGC buying in the next two prints.
2. **The dollar's FX share rose while its reserve share fell.** Default: both fall together. Observed: FX 88% to 89%, reserves down. Mechanism: network effects in trading and funding are stronger than in official portfolios.
3. **Euro credit is growing faster than dollar credit.** Default: the dollar wins funding. Observed: 12% vs 7.3%, euro share of FX credit 22% to 28%. Possible drivers: higher US rates making euro funding cheaper, and Europe's own pull. Test: BIS GLI end-June 2026 (October 2026 release).
4. **The yuan lost reserve share while China's trade surplus hit records.** Default: trade weight pulls reserves (Banque de France 2023). Observed: 2.8% to 2.11%. Explanation: sanctions fear cuts both ways (yuan held in China is freezable by Beijing), plus low Chinese yields.
5. **In the April 2025 US-made shock, money did not go to China.** The market's revealed choice of emergency heir was the euro, the yen and gold.

## 11. Mechanism chains

1. **US promise failure chain.** Fed independence lost or foreign holders taxed or frozen, then foreign official buyers stop rolling Treasuries, then term premium rises and the dollar falls, then reserve managers shift to gold, euro, CHF and JPY (not yuan unless China has opened), then the dollar share falls 1 to 2 points a year instead of 0.5, and the multipolar state arrives in H1 rather than H2.
2. **China opening chain (stated case).** Quota lifted and fixing freed, then household outflows (about $1.25T to $2.5T at 5% to 10% of deposits), then a weaker yuan and Chinese buying of dollar assets (dollar up), then if courts and exit rules are trusted, index inclusion and steady foreign official buying at 0.4 to 0.9 points of reserve share a year, then 10% in the 2040s.
3. **Euro safe-asset chain.** A permanent joint bond (defense or MFF 2028 to 2034 borrowing) of several trillion euros, then a euro "Treasury" that reserve managers can buy in size, then euro share back toward 25%. Breaker: a France-led spread crisis (the R3 weak link).
4. **Dollar debt-crisis paradox.** US debt stress, then global deleveraging, then dollar demand from $14.7T of offshore dollar debtors, then the dollar rises in the first phase (2008 and 2020 pattern), then Fed swap lines decide who gets dollars, which deepens dependence on the Fed in the short run.

## 14. Proposed changes to scenario odds

Current: H0 62/15/1/22, H1 44/23/3/30, H2 31/28/10/31.

**Headline odds: no change.** The evidence in this lane mostly confirms what the desk already holds. Gold above the euro in reserves (R1 L5-02, L5-03) was already priced in S2. The dollar's growing plumbing share (89% FX, $14.7T credit) supports S1 and offsets the slow reserve erosion.
