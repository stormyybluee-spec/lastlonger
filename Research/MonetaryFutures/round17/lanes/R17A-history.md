## 2. Railway mania, Britain
- 1846 peak: 263 Acts for new railway companies, routes totalling 9,500 miles. About a third of lines authorised 1844 to 1846 were never built. 6,220 miles were built from 1844 to 1846 authorisations. Source Wikipedia Railway_Mania citing Casson 2009, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Mania. Status secondary (opened, but tertiary).
- Railway investment as share of GDP 5.7 percent in 1846, 6.7 percent in 1847, 4.7 percent in 1848 (Mitchell 1964, quoted in a Queen's University Belfast paper, https://pure.qub.ac.uk/files/17386220/RailwayDeriving.pdf). Odlyzko puts 1847 at about 43 million pounds, slightly over 10 percent of GDP. Status secondary for both (search summary, papers blocked: HTTP 403 and 503 on Odlyzko's host).
- Odlyzko (2010) abstract: investors expected 10 percent dividends, which needed railway revenue of 30 to 60 million pounds a year by about 1850 against about 6 million actual. Secondary, https://www.kwharrison13.com/notes/collective-hallucinations-and-inefficient-markets-the-british-railway-mania-of-the-1840s/ (opened, a reader's notes).
- Critic: Campbell (2010) MPRA, evidence against the bubble claim, railway shares not obviously overpriced even at the peak. Secondary (search summary and Wikipedia).
- Money: Bank Charter Act 1844. Bank Rate was cut from 4 percent to 2.5 percent in the two years after it, raised in April 1847, crisis in 1847, government letter 25 October 1847 let the Bank lend more. Source Bank Underground, https://bankunderground.co.uk/?p=2449. Verified (opened). Year-by-year rates not found yet.

## 7. Electricity
- Pearl Street: the Edison Electric Illuminating Co. paid about $300,000 to acquire the properties and build the station and distribution system. With administrative costs, interest, canvassing and patent licence fees to the Edison Electric Light Co. the total chargeable to the first district was more than half a million dollars. The company did not charge customers for current until early 1883. Service began 4 September 1882. Source: Thomas Edison Papers (Rutgers), headnote Doc. 2243, https://edison.sas.rutgers.edu/images/archive/innovations/EL/PearlStreetHeadnote.pdf . Verified (PDF opened, text extracted). Scholar edition, T2.
- Backers: Edison Electric Light Co. incorporated 1878 with $300,000 capital, investors mostly tied to Western Union or Drexel, Morgan and Company, Vanderbilt interests also named. J. P. Morgan was in the room on 4 September 1882 (ETHW milestone page, https://ethw.org/Milestones:Pearl_Street_Station,_1882, opened, says "one of his principal investors" and "not an immediate financial success"). Capital amount from a search summary, secondary.
- REA: Richmond Fed Econ Focus 2020 Q1, https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/econ_focus/2020/q1/economic_history . Verified (opened). About one in 10 farms had power in 1930, about half by the end of World War II, nearly caught up with cities by the mid-1950s. Congress set aside $100 million in 1935, co-ops borrowed at 2 to 3 percent, almost all loans repaid, default under 1 percent. Only 33 co-ops existed in 1930.
- A USDA 1954 summary quoted in search results says 77 percent of farms had service in 1950 and the 1954 census 93 percent. Secondary, not opened.

## 8. Perez on bubbles and easy money (own page, opened)
- Source: Carlota Perez, https://carlotaperez.org/?p=5425 (page of her writings). Verified (opened, first 100,000 characters). She writes of major technology bubbles that in her 2009 Cambridge Journal of Economics paper they "are the result of opportunity pull rather than of easy credit push". She calls bubbles "massive episodes of credit creation" tied to assimilating a technological revolution (2002 framework). The NASDAQ boom "facilitated enough over-investment in telecommunications and fibre optic cables to interconnect the global space digitally" (2007 essay). Golden ages "have followed post-bubble recessions" (2013). She contrasts the 2000s, "from opportunity pull to easy credit push".
- Critic: Palma, Cambridge chapter, argues the installation period holds contradictions and that productivity growth has declined since the ICT age began. Secondary (search summary).
- Dark fibre: TeleGeography as quoted around 2002: 2.7 percent of installed fibre in use, and 10 percent of channels lit on the 10 percent of fibres carrying traffic. Secondary (mailing-list mirror, search summary). Odlyzko says the "traffic doubling every 100 days" claim was wrong and real growth was about 75 to 150 percent a year. Secondary.

## 9. Railway benefits, Crafts and others

Verified (PDF opened and text extracted). ## 10. Railway mania money conditions, Campbell (2014), verified (PDF opened, text extracted)
- Source: Gareth Campbell, "Government Policy during the British Railway Mania and the 1847 Commercial Crisis", in Dimsdale and Hotson (eds), British Financial Crises since 1825, OUP 2014, pp. 58 to 75, https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/files/18161511/RailwayCrisis.pdf . Scholar's own chapter, T2.
- Construction was 5.7, 6.7 and 4.7 percent of GDP in 1846, 1847 and 1848 (citing Mitchell 1964). In 1846, of 516 Bills considered, 81 were thrown out on Standing Orders, 83 withdrawn, 106 rejected on merits and 246 received Royal Assent.
- Bank discount rate: 3.0 percent raised to 3.5 and then 4.0 in January 1847, raised to 5 in April 1847 after the poor harvest of 1846 and gold outflow, 5.5 in August 1847, and a government letter in October 1847 told the Bank to lend more at a rate "not less than 8 per cent". Banking reserve fell from 10.3 to 3.1 million pounds, Issue Department notes from 29.9 million in September 1846 to 22.5 million in April 1847.
- Secondary (search): Bank minimum discount rate 2.5 percent from September 1844, rates only 2.5 to 3.5 percent before 1847 (Hansard 1856 speech), raised in autumn 1845. So the mania ran under a low and stable Bank rate, then the crisis came with rising rates and a gold drain.

## 14. Evidence for and against H1, H2 and H3

Evidence for H1 (big technologies cost a lot before payback)
- Railways took 5.7 to 6.7 percent of GDP a year for three years (Campbell, verified) and investors earned little for years (secondary).
- Rural electrification needed a $100 million public fund in 1935 and about 20 years to reach most farms (Richmond Fed, verified). Pearl Street cost over $500,000 and did not bill until 1883 (Edison Papers, verified).
- Early integrated circuits were so costly that only a government buyer would pay, a gate at $120 in 1961 (secondary).

Evidence against H1
- The payback is often not to the investor. Railway investors lost while the economy gained (Crafts, verified). So "paid back" depends on who is counted.
- Railway freight benefit was 4.1 percent of GNP in 1865 and Crafts says such upper bound figures may be about twice too large (verified). The benefit is real but smaller than the legend, about a fifth of productivity growth at most (secondary).
- I found no single total cost and payback year for most cases. "Years to payback" is not cleanly measurable, which is a limit on H1 as stated.

Evidence for H2 (paid for with promises about the future)
- Railways: shares bought with small deposits and later calls, 10 percent dividends expected (Odlyzko via secondary, Campbell verified on instalments). Venture money: $31.3 billion in 2010 and $130.9 billion in 2018 (NVCA pack, verified), with the majority of firms not returning capital (Ghosh, secondary).
- Government promises: REA loans at 2 to 3 percent, repaid with under 1 percent default (verified). Military buying of ICs: 100 percent in 1962 (secondary table of Nelson).

Evidence against H2
- Taxes and contracts are not "promises about the future" in the same sense. Federal funding paid 67 percent of US R&D in 1964 and 18 percent in 2023 (NSF, verified), and business now pays about 75 percent, much of it from current profits, not borrowing. I did not find the share of business R&D paid from retained profit against borrowing, so this is untested.
- Pearl Street was paid for by private investors in Edison companies and the first ICs by military buyers. I found no evidence that either rested on credit creation, but I did not test it.
- About 40 percent of basic research in 2022 was still federal, against 70 percent in the 1960s (NSF, verified). The state share has fallen but not vanished.

Evidence for H3 (central bank or bank money made it possible)
- The 1840s railway mania ran with Bank rate at 2.5 to 3.5 percent from September 1844 (secondary, Campbell verified 3.0 percent before January 1847). Rates then rose with a gold drain and the 1847 crisis (verified).
- The venture boom of 2020 and 2021 came with fed funds at 0.09 and 0.08 percent and M2 up 26.8 percent in a year (FRED, verified). VC reached about $331 to $358 billion in 2021 (secondary).
- Perez calls bubbles "massive episodes of credit creation" (verified, her page).

Evidence against H3
- The dot-com boom ran at fed funds 5.25 to 5.56 percent in 1995 to 1998 (FRED, verified), not near zero. The Nasdaq peaked on 10 March 2000 with the rate at 5.85 and still rising. M2 grew about 5.9 percent a year over 1995 to 1999 (computed). That is ordinary money, not an easy money surge.
- Perez herself writes that major technology bubbles "are the result of opportunity pull rather than of easy credit push" (verified). She says the 2000s housing bubble was the credit push type.
- Rural electrification, the integrated circuit and ARPANET were funded by public budgets and contracts in 1935 to 1969, not mainly by bank credit growth. Pearl Street came under the gold standard.
- The railway money was tight in 1847 when the crash came, so the crash is tied to gold and harvest as much as to the earlier low rate. A low rate is a rival cause with harvest, gold flows and the Bank Charter Act 1844.
