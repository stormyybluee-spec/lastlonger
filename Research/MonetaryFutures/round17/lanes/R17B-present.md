### 1d. Cost per kilogram to low Earth orbit

Source: Harry W. Jones, NASA Ames Research Center, "The Recent Large Reduction in Space Launch Cost", ICES-2018-81, July 2018. Opened and read. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001093/downloads/20200001093.pdf

| Falcon 9 (advertised price) | 62 | 22,800 | 2,720 |
| Falcon Heavy (advertised price) | 90 | 63,800 | 1,410 |

Cautions, all from the paper or plainly implied by it.
- The Falcon figures are advertised prices, not NASA cost accounting.
- The paper dates the "major drop" to 2010, the first Falcon 9 flight. That is before any booster was landed (December 2015) or reflown (2017). So most of the measured fall came from a cheaper way of building and buying rockets, not from reuse alone. This matters for H4: the case is about procurement method as much as about reuse.
- For cargo to the ISS, with the Dragon capsule, the paper finds a smaller fall, about 4 times ($93,400 to $23,300 per kg).
- The paper notes Soviet and Chinese systems with low costs "may be subsidized".

What is still unproven (as of 2026-11).
- Whether large constellation satellite internet pays back its capital over a full satellite replacement cycle. The rival constellation OneWeb went bankrupt in 2020 (see 1e). Starlink revenue is estimated, not disclosed.
- Whether a fully reusable super heavy vehicle (Starship) reaches routine, cheap operation. Still in test as of this writing.
- Whether travel to Mars has any commercial payback. No revenue exists. This is a stated aim, not a business result.

Nuance for the claim. The mRNA case shows that the early cost was small ($337M over decades) and the enormous price came after the technology worked, as a purchase. That cuts against the simple story that a huge price must be paid first. The rocket case is similar: the public outlay that mattered was a few hundred million dollars, paid on results. Theranos shows that a large price paid in advance can buy nothing. A fair reading: new technology often needs patient early money, but the size of the early bill does not predict success, and much of the large spending comes after proof.

## 2. H5, late developers

### 2a. South Korea

Main tools: state control of the banks (nationalised in 1961), central bank refinancing of export and policy loans at low rates, the National Investment Fund (1974) for the Heavy and Chemical Industry (HCI) drive from 1973, and a few large business groups (chaebol) as the chosen borrowers.

Best case for. Directed credit, much of it backed by central bank money, built steel, shipbuilding, chemicals and electronics firms that still lead world markets. Exports shifted almost wholly to heavy and high technology goods.
Best case against. The same tool left a debt overhang. Inflation ran at 10 to 20% for two decades. By 1997 the largest groups carried debt about five times their equity, and the crisis forced an IMF rescue, mass layoffs and the break-up of Daewoo. The 1979 to 1980 slump (oil shock, overcapacity in heavy industry, political crisis) was a first warning. Rival causes for the growth: education, land reform, US aid and the export discipline of world markets.

Rival causes: returning overseas engineers, an export processing zone network from 1966, and the timing of the fabless design boom in the US that created demand for a contract foundry.

### 2c. Malaysia

Main tools, in order. (1) Tax incentives for foreign firms: Investment Incentives Act 1968, pioneer status tax holidays, the Free Trade Zone Act 1971, and MIDA (set up 1967 as FIDA) to find and sign investors. (2) Penang's Bayan Lepas Free Trade Zone from 1972, pushed by the Penang state government after unemployment reached about 16% in 1969. (3) The heavy industries push of the 1980s through a state holding company, HICOM (November 1980), with Proton (1983, a joint venture with Mitsubishi) and the steel maker Perwaja. (4) Khazanah Nasional (1993), the state investment fund. (5) The Multimedia Super Corridor (1996). (6) Development finance institutions, which lend at policy rates.

Note on money creation. Unlike Korea, the electronics zone was paid for mostly with foreign firms' own capital, attracted by tax holidays and cheap disciplined labour. The state's cost was mostly taxes not collected. The heavy industries push was paid for with state equity, state backed loans and tariff protection, that is, from the budget and by car buyers paying higher prices.

What worked. The cheapest tool. Tax holidays and a well run zone drew foreign capital that built an electronics base employing tens of thousands, and E&E is still about 40% of exports half a century later. Penang today hosts chip testing and packaging for many of the same firms.
What did not. The costliest tool. The heavy industries push, paid with state money and tariff protection, left Proton needing a soft loan and a foreign buyer by 2017, and Perwaja became a byword for losses. Scholars (Rasiah) argue that Malaysia stayed in assembly and testing rather than moving into design, and call this "structural regression" or premature deindustrialisation. The MSC met its company count but not its hope of home grown technology firms.
Rival causes. The electronics boom rode a global wave of US chip firms seeking cheap assembly (also in Singapore, Hong Kong, the Philippines). Penang's English speaking workforce and port mattered. Malaysia's growth also rested on oil, palm oil and rubber.

Lesson for H5 in Malaysia. The part that paid back fastest needed little borrowing from the future. The part paid for with large public promises did worst. This is evidence against the simple form of the owner's point.

## 3. Human detail for narration

Each scene uses only what the source gives. Short quotes under 25 words.

Scene 1. Haworth, Yorkshire, 1846 to 1849. Railway mania, seen from a parsonage.
In 1842 the sisters Anne, Emily and Charlotte Brontë were each left about £300. Emily managed the joint money and put it into York and North Midland Railway shares, one of George Hudson's lines. On 30 January 1846 Charlotte wrote to her friend Margaret Wooler: "I have been most anxious for us to sell our shares ere it be too late." She could not persuade her sisters. On 27 September 1849 she wrote to her publisher George Smith that the shares had started at £50, once rose to 120, paid 10% for some years, and were "now down at 20".
Source: Anne Brontë website, 27 Sept 2020, quoting the letters. https://www.annebronte.org/2020/09/27/2164/ Status: verified (letters as quoted by a secondary site, originals in the Smith edition of the letters, not opened).
Writer's note: this is ordinary savers lending to a new technology. The railway was built, the line ran, but these investors lost most of the price they paid at the peak.

Scene 2. Penang, around 1980. The women on the first electronics lines.
The American writer Rachel Grossman reported in March 1980 that about 19,000 women worked in Penang's electronics plants. At one plant of a large Northern California chip firm, about 1,400 people worked and women were 90% of the assembly workforce. Many were recent school leavers from villages. Malaysia had exempted electronics employers from the ban on night shifts for women in 1970. A Korean study of 1975 she cites found nearly half of microscope workers had become short sighted. Malaysian researchers note that townspeople called these migrant factory women "Minah Karan", a mocking nickname.
Sources: Grossman, "Bitter Wages", Multinational Monitor, March 1980, https://multinationalmonitor.org/hyper/issues/1980/03/grossman.html (verified). "Minah Karan": Universiti Malaya eprint, https://eprints.um.edu.my/11220/1/0001.pdf (secondary, search summary).
Writer's note: this is the cheap side of the late developer story. Foreign capital paid for the plants. The local price was paid in long shifts and eyesight, and the gain was wages and skills.

Scene 3. Somerville, New Jersey, and Hsinchu, 1976 to 1977. Taiwan's engineers learn to make chips.
On 5 March 1976 ITRI signed a CMOS technology transfer agreement with RCA. In April 1976 it sent 19 young engineers to RCA sites in the United States for a year of training in chip design, process, testing and equipment. One of them, Shih Chin-tay, led the process team and on return in 1977 became the first manager of ITRI's demonstration plant. Another trainee, Robert Tsao, later chaired UMC. By the end of 1977 the plant was making chips for electronic watches, and within six months reached about 70% yield, higher than RCA's own US plants.
Sources: Taiwan Today (government publication), via search, https://taiwantoday.tw (secondary). Ling-Ming Huang, Taiwan Insight, 10 May 2024 (verified for 19 engineers, watches, 70% yield). Asterisk magazine (secondary).
Writer's note: the whole licence cost about $10M. The early price of Taiwan's chip industry was small and the payback very large, which is a test case against "enormous price first".

Scene 4. Cape Canaveral, 21 December 2015, 8:29 pm EST. The first booster comes back.
A Falcon 9 lifted off from Complex 40 carrying 11 ORBCOMM communications satellites. It was the company's first flight since a launch failure in June 2015. About ten minutes later, the first stage came down upright on Landing Zone 1, a former launch pad about 9 km away, the first time a first stage from an orbital launch had landed. The upper stage released all 11 satellites.
Sources: US Air Force 45th Space Wing news release, 22 Dec 2015, https://www.patrick.spaceforce.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/732882/45th-space-wing-successfully-launches-orbcomm-historically-lands-first-stage-bo/ and Space.com, https://www.space.com/31420-spacex-rocket-landing-success.html (secondary, via search). Note: some called it a "suborbital booster stage", since the first stage itself does not reach orbit.
Writer's note: the landing came five years after the first Falcon 9 flight. NASA's paper (1d) dates the big fall in launch price to 2010, before any landing.

Scene 5. United States, 2001. The dot-com layoffs.
The outplacement firm Challenger, Gray and Christmas counted 100,925 jobs cut at internet companies in 2001, more than double the 41,515 of 2000. The worst month was April 2001, with 17,554. Across all US industries it counted 1,956,876 announced job cuts in 2001, the most since it began counting in 1993.
Source: Computerworld, "Dot-com layoffs eased at end of 2001", January 2002, https://www.computerworld.com/article/1354274/dot-com-layoffs-eased-at-end-of-2001.html (secondary, via search). No named individual found. Gap.
