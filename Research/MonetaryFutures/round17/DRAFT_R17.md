## L3.6 Paying for tomorrow: who funds the things that pay back later? (id rb-future)

Kicker: Railways, electricity, microchips, the internet and rockets all cost a fortune before they paid anyone back. A reader says promises about the future, credit and money made by a central bank, are what paid for them. This chapter follows the money behind five great technologies, and then asks how Korea, Taiwan and Malaysia paid for theirs.

[0 tldr]
- The reader is right that new technology costs a great deal before it pays back. British railway building took about 6% of the whole country's output each year at its peak. Many bets failed.
- Promises did pay for it, but not mostly the kind the reader means. The biggest early payers were savers buying shares and governments buying the product. Easy money from central banks lines up with only some of the booms.
- The government's most useful role was often as a first customer, not a lender. It bought every microchip sold in 1962. America's space agency, NASA, paid a rocket company for results, not for promises.
- Late developers did use created money and directed credit. Korea grew fast with it and paid with inflation and a crisis. Malaysia's biggest success, electronics in Penang, needed little borrowing at all.

- rb-peasant: Chapter 04 found the first factories were built mostly from their owners' money, while canals and railways needed promises from many people.

### The claim on trial

[4 p] The reader who wrote in has a large hope about money. Here it is in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. New technology and new discovery cost an enormous price before they make life better and more stable.
- Part 2. Promises about the future, credit, are what pay that price.
- Part 3. Money made by a central bank, and by banks when they lend, is what makes those promises possible.
- Part 4. The reader's example: a company building reusable rockets, and the growth and new technology that may follow from it.

[6 p] Chapter 04 found a hint that the reader's picture fits countries that came late better than the first industrial country. A fifth test checks that hint.


### Three sisters and a railway

[8 p] In 1842 three sisters in a cold parsonage in Yorkshire each received about 300 pounds from an aunt. Their names were Charlotte, Emily and Anne Brontë. Within a few years all three would publish novels that are still read today. But in the 1840s they were simply three careful women with a little money to keep safe.

[9 p] Emily looked after the money. She put it into shares of the York and North Midland Railway. It was the age of railway fever. In 1846 alone Parliament considered 516 bills for new railways and passed 246. People of every kind bought shares, from lords to clergymen's daughters.

[10 p] Charlotte grew worried. On 30 January 1846 she wrote to a friend.

[11 quote] "I have been most anxious for us to sell our shares ere it be too late." (Charlotte Brontë, 30 January 1846, as quoted from her letters)

[12 p] She could not persuade her sisters. In September 1849, after Emily and Anne had both died, she wrote to her publisher about the shares. They had been issued at 50 pounds. At the top they were worth 120. For some years they paid 10% a year. Now they were "down at 20".

[13 p] Here is the strange part. The railway was built. Trains ran on it. The line did exactly what it promised to do for the country. It was the people who paid for it at the top of the fever who lost most of their money.

[14 p] Keep the sisters in mind. Every test in this chapter comes back to the same question: when a society pays for tomorrow, who holds the promise, and who is left holding the loss?


### Test 1. Does new technology cost a fortune before it pays back?

[16 p] Yes. This part of the claim is the easiest to support.

[17 table]
| Technology | What it cost before it paid | What it left behind |
|---|---|---|
| British railways, 1840s | Railway building took 5.7% of Britain's whole yearly output in 1846, 6.7% in 1847 and 4.7% in 1848. Shareholders were promised returns the traffic could not pay. About a third of the lines approved in 1844 to 1846 are reported never to have been built | A network that, by one generous estimate, saved the economy about 4% of its yearly output in freight costs by 1865 |
| Electric light, New York, 1882 | Thomas Edison's first power station cost about 300,000 dollars. With the wires and other charges the first district cost over half a million. For months it charged nothing for the power | The start of the electric grid |
| The microchip, 1960s | The first integrated circuits were costly and few. Their first buyers were rocket and space programmes | Chips cheap enough for every phone |
| The internet boom, 1995 to 2000 | Investors poured money into new internet firms. The Nasdaq share index then fell 77.9% from March 2000 to October 2002. Firms cut about 100,000 jobs in 2001, by one count | Cables, data centres and firms that survived |

[18 p] And most single bets fail even when the technology wins. Among American firms backed by venture capital, money from investors who buy a share of young companies, one study found about three in four did not return what their investors put in. Another count found about two thirds of such deals returned less than they cost, and fewer than 4% returned ten times or more. The winners pay for the losers.

[19 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds. New technology costs a great deal before it pays back, and most single bets fail along the way. The sisters' railway was built. Their money was lost.


### Test 2. Who actually paid?

[21 p] The reader says promises about the future pay the price. That is true, but there are several kinds of promise, and they are not the same.

[22 table]
| Kind of promise | What it means | Where it paid |
|---|---|---|
| Shares | Savers buy a part of the business and share its future profit or loss | The railways. The internet boom. Young firms backed by venture capital |
| Loans from banks | A bank lends now and is paid back with interest later | Late developers such as Korea, as Test 5 shows |
| Government as a buyer | The state promises to buy the product, which lets the maker raise money from others | The microchip. Rockets |
| Government as a payer for research | Taxes pay for science that no single firm would fund | The internet's first network. Basic science |
| Profits kept in the firm | A business pays for its own next step | Most research by business today |

[23 p] Look at research spending in America, which is the largest in the world.

[24 table]
| American research spending | 1964 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| Share paid by the federal government | 67% | 18% |
| Share paid by business | 31% | 75% |
| Share of basic research paid by the federal government | 70%, in 1969 | 40% |

[25 p] So the money behind research moved from the state to business over sixty years. Business pays mostly from its own profits and from shares. But the state still pays for a large part of the most basic science, the kind that may not pay back for decades, if ever.

[26 p] The microchip shows the state's other role. In 1962 the American government bought every integrated circuit made in the country. In 1966 it bought 53%, and by 1968 it bought 37%. A huge order of chips for the computers that guided the Apollo flights to the Moon was, until 1965, the largest single use. The government did not lend the chip makers money. It bought what they made, and the guaranteed sales let them grow until ordinary customers could afford the chips.

[27 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 holds, with a correction. Promises did pay for new technology. But the main promises were shares bought by savers, profits kept in the firm, and a government that promised to buy the product. Bank loans mattered more in some countries than in others.


### Test 3. Was it created money that made the promises possible?

[29 p] This is the boldest part of the claim. If money made by central banks and by bank lending is what paid for new technology, the great technology booms should come when money is easy: when interest rates are low and the supply of money grows fast. Here is what happened.

[30 table]
| Boom | Interest rates at the time | Money conditions |
|---|---|---|
| British railway fever, 1844 to 1846 | The Bank of England's rate was 2.5% to 3.5%, low for the time, as reported | Easy. Then the rate rose to 5.5% in 1847 and the boom broke |
| The internet boom, 1995 to 2000 | America's main interest rate was between about 4.6% and 5.9% | Ordinary. The money supply grew about 33% over five years, about 6% a year |
| The young-firm boom, 2020 to 2021 | America's main interest rate was close to zero | Very easy. The money supply grew 26.8% in a single year |

[31 p] So two booms fit the reader's picture and one does not. The internet boom, the one that built the most lasting technology of the three, happened while money cost about 5% to borrow. Carlota Perez, an economist whose life's work is on how bubbles pay for new technology, puts it in a phrase.

[32 quote] "opportunity pull rather than easy credit push" (Carlota Perez, on what drives the great technology bubbles)

[33 p] In plain words: the great booms come because people see a huge chance, not mainly because money is cheap. Cheap money can add fuel. It did in 1845 and in 2021. But the spark was the technology.

[34 p] There is a second point. When money is cheap, it can just as easily pour into old things, such as houses, as into new ones. Chapter 02 showed that most bank lending in rich countries now goes to property. Created money is not aimed at technology unless someone aims it.

[35 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds only in part. Easy money fed two of the three booms, and none of them needed it to start. The deeper driver was the size of the chance people saw.

[36 check] Q: A friend says central banks printing money is what built the internet. What would you check?
A: The interest rate during the internet boom. From 1995 to 2000 America's main rate was about 5%, not near zero, and the money supply grew about 6% a year. The first internet network was paid for by the government's research budget, from taxes, and the boom was paid for by investors buying shares.


### Test 4. The reader's rocket

[38 p] The reader's own example is a company building rockets that land and fly again. It is a fair test, because it is happening now. Here is how it has been paid for, as far as the public record shows.

[39 p] In 2006 NASA, America's space agency, tried something new. Instead of designing a rocket and paying a contractor its costs, it offered fixed payments to private companies for reaching set goals. The company that went on to build reusable rockets was paid 396 million dollars when it had met all 40 of its goals by 2012. Another company was paid 288 million dollars on the same terms.

[40 table]
| What a rocket like this was expected to cost to develop | Estimate |
|---|---|
| NASA's model, the traditional way, where NASA pays the builder's costs | About 1.4 billion to 4.0 billion dollars, depending on the version of the model |
| NASA's model, the commercial way, with a fixed price | About 0.4 billion to 1.7 billion dollars |
| What the company told Congress it spent on the rocket | About 300 million dollars, as reported |

[41 p] NASA itself warns that these are not budget estimates and that it did not study the gap closely. Still, the pattern is clear. The public money was small next to the old way of doing things, and it was paid for results, not for promises. NASA drew one lesson above all: a firm promise to buy a service makes it much easier for a company to raise money from investors.

[42 p] That lesson is the microchip story again. The state as first customer, not as lender.

[43 p] Did reuse make launches cheaper? Yes, but the timing is a surprise. The old space shuttle cost about 54,500 dollars to lift each kilogram to low orbit. The new rocket advertised about 2,720 dollars, about 20 times less. But a NASA study dates most of the fall to around 2010, before the first booster landed safely in December 2015. The cheaper way of building rockets did most of the work. Reuse came on top.

[44 p] And for every winner there were losers who also raised large sums. A satellite internet company called OneWeb raised 3.4 billion dollars and went bankrupt in March 2020, with 74 of about 650 planned satellites in orbit. A rocket company called Virgin Orbit flew six missions, two of which failed, and went bankrupt in April 2023. Its assets sold for about 36 million dollars.

[45 p] What is still unknown? A great deal. Whether satellite internet on a huge scale will repay what it has cost, and whether travel to Mars will ever pay back at all, cannot be known yet. The reader's hope about what may follow is a hope, and the record so far supports only its first steps.

[46 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 holds in part. The rocket company was paid for by private investors and by a government that promised to buy its services, at a fraction of the old cost. It was not paid for by created money. Other companies that raised billions failed. What the industry will lead to is still unknown.


### Test 5. How did the late developers pay?

[48 p] Chapter 04 left a hint: maybe the reader's chain of promises fits countries that came late better than the first. Here the evidence is strongest, and it cuts both ways.

[49 p] Korea fits the reader's picture most closely. From 1973 to 1981 its banks relied on the central bank for 89% of the money they lent to exporters. Most of the growth in the money banks could lend came from the central bank itself. In other words, Korea paid for its push into steel, ships and chemicals largely with created money, aimed by the state. It worked. Heavy and chemical goods rose from 13% of Korea's exports in 1970 to 92% by 2008. But prices rose 10% to 20% a year through the 1960s and 1970s. And by 1997 the biggest business groups are reported to have owed about five times what they owned, and the country needed a rescue of 58.4 billion dollars.

[50 p] Taiwan took a different road. In 1976 its state research institute paid an American firm about 10 million dollars to teach it how to make chips, and sent 19 engineers to learn. By the end of 1977 its small test plant was making watch chips and, by its own count, wasting fewer than the American teacher did. In 1987 the state helped found a chip maker, reported to own about 48% of it at the start. That firm is reported to have made 61% of the world's chips made to order by 2023. The money was public, but it went into skills and one well-run firm, not into a flood of loans.

[51 p] Malaysia took a third road, and its biggest success needed little borrowing at all. Penang had high unemployment in 1969. From 1971 it opened a free trade zone and offered foreign firms years free of tax. In 1972 Intel opened its first factory outside America there, and other chip and electronics firms followed. Foreign firms paid for the factories with their own money.

[52 p] In 1980 about 19,000 women worked in Penang's electronics plants. Many were school leavers from villages, and women made up about nine in ten of the assembly workers. The work was hard: long shifts, often at night, and close work under microscopes. But the wages and the skills stayed. In 2023 electrical and electronic goods were about 40% of everything Malaysia sold abroad, as reported.

[53 p] Malaysia's heavily funded push into heavy industry did less well. A state car maker, Proton, was set up in the 1980s behind high protection. In 2016 it received a soft loan of 1.5 billion ringgit, and in 2017 a Chinese car maker bought almost half of it, as reported.

[54 table]
| Late developer | Main way of paying | What it bought | What it cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Korea | Created money and bank loans aimed by the state | Steel, ships and chemicals that now sell worldwide | Inflation of 10% to 20% a year, and a crisis in 1997 |
| Taiwan | Public money for skills and one firm | A world-leading chip maker | Small next to Korea's loans, by these accounts |
| Malaysia, Penang | Tax holidays that drew foreign firms' own money | Electronics, about 40% of exports today | Taxes not collected, and hard factory work |
| Malaysia, heavy industry | State money and protection | A national car maker | Losses, a rescue loan, and a foreign partner |

[55 p] Scholars still argue about all of this. Some, such as the economist Alice Amsden, argue that East Asia grew because the state steered credit. A World Bank report in 1993 doubted that steering credit was central. A later look at Korea's heavy industry drive says the case that it failed is harder to make now than it was then.

[56 insider] (What test 5 does to the claim) Test 5 gives the reader's claim its strongest support and its clearest warning. Korea did pay for its rise largely with created money aimed by the state, and it worked, with high inflation and a crisis as the price. But Taiwan and Malaysia's Penang show that skills, a few good firms and foreign money could do as much with far less borrowing.


### The verdict on paying for tomorrow

[58 table]
| The claim | Finding | The fact that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. New technology costs an enormous price before it pays back | Holds | Railway building took 6.7% of Britain's output in 1847. About three in four venture-backed firms, in one study, did not return their investors' money |
| 2. Promises about the future pay that price | Holds, with a correction. Mostly shares, profits and the state as buyer, less often bank loans | The government bought every American microchip in 1962. Business paid 75% of American research in 2022 |
| 3. Created money makes those promises possible | Holds only in part | The internet boom happened with America's main interest rate near 5% |
| 4. The rocket example | Holds in part | NASA paid 396 million dollars for results, against an old-style estimate of billions. Companies that raised billions also failed |

[59 p] Here is the claim again, rebuilt so the record can carry it.

[60 steps]
- New technology does cost a fortune before it pays back, and most single bets fail. Society pays for the winners by accepting many losers.
- The promises that paid were mostly shares bought by savers, profits kept in firms, and governments promising to buy the product. A state that buys first can do more than a state that only lends.
- Cheap money fed some booms. It did not start them. The spark was the chance people saw.
- Some late developers, Korea most of all, did aim created money at new industry, and it worked at a price. Others did as well with skills and foreign money. The reader's chain fits late developers better than the first country, but it is one road among several.

[61 p] And the sisters? They were not wrong to back the railway. The railway was built and it served the country. They were unlucky to buy near the top, and to be among the many whose money paid for something everyone else would use. That is the hidden side of paying for tomorrow. Someone holds the promise, and someone holds the loss.

[62 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that the great technology booms came mostly in years of very cheap money, across many more cases than these three. Second, that countries which aimed created money at industry grew faster than those which did not, once other causes are set aside. Third, that the new space industry pays back on the scale its backers hope. The third will take years to know.

[63 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Two more stories of public money and new technology
[63.0 p] Electricity for American farms. In 1935 the American government set aside 100 million dollars to lend to farm groups at 2% to 3% interest so they could string power lines into the countryside. Fewer than 1 in 100 of the loans went bad. By about 1945 around half of American farms had power, as reported. This is a case where public lending did pay for a new network, and was repaid.

[63.1 p] The internet's first network was paid for by America's defence department from 1969. A later network run by the national science agency carried much of the traffic until it was closed on 30 April 1995, when private firms took over, as reported. Public money paid for the slow, early years. Private money paid for the boom.
