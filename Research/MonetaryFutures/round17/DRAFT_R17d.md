## L3.6 Paying for tomorrow: who funds the things that pay back later? (id rb-future)

Kicker: Railways, electricity, microchips, the internet and rockets all cost a fortune before they paid anyone back. A reader says promises about the future, credit and money made by a central bank, are what paid for them. This chapter follows the money behind five great technologies, and then asks how Korea, Taiwan and Malaysia paid for theirs.

[0 tldr]
- The reader is right that new technology costs a great deal before it pays back. British railway building took 4.7% to 6.7% of the whole country's output each year from 1846 to 1848. Many bets failed.
- Promises did pay for it, but not only the kind the reader means. Large early payers were savers buying shares and governments buying the product. Easy money lines up with only some booms. Government borrowing was not tested.
- One of the government's most useful roles was as a first customer. It bought every microchip sold in 1962. America's space agency, NASA, promised to pay a rocket company as it reached set goals.
- Korea's central bank supplied most of the money for export loans, and heavy industry grew, at a price in debt and inflation. Malaysia's best-known success, electronics in Penang, was built mostly with foreign firms' money.

### The claim on trial

[4 p] The reader who wrote in has a large hope about money. Here it is in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. New technology and new discovery cost an enormous price before they make life better and more stable.
- Part 2. Promises about the future, called credit, are what pay that price. A promise to pay later is credit.
- Part 3. Money made by a central bank, and by banks when they lend, is what makes those promises possible.
- Part 4. The reader's example: a company building reusable rockets, and the growth and new technology that may follow from it.


### Three sisters and a railway

[7 p] In 1842 three sisters in a parsonage in Yorkshire were each left about 300 pounds. Their names were Charlotte, Emily and Anne Brontë. In the 1840s they were three careful women with a little money to keep safe.

[8 p] Emily looked after the money. She put it into shares of the York and North Midland Railway. It was the age of railway fever. In 1846 alone Parliament considered 516 bills for new railways and passed 246. A buyer often paid only a small deposit at first, about a tenth, as reported, and the rest when the company called for it. So owners could be asked for more money later.

[9 p] Charlotte grew worried. On 30 January 1846 she wrote to her friend Margaret Wooler.

[10 quote] "I have been most anxious for us to sell our shares ere it be too late." (Charlotte Brontë, 30 January 1846, as quoted from her letters)

[11 p] She could not persuade her sisters. In September 1849, after Emily and Anne had both died, she described the shares in a letter to her publisher. They had been issued at 50 pounds. At the top they were worth 120. For some years they paid 10% a year. Now they were "down at 20". Each share was worth 20 pounds, less than half of the 50 pounds first paid.

[12 p] Here is the strange part. The railway was built. Trains ran on it. But the company had not told its owners the truth. The website that quotes the letters says its profits were greatly overstated, and part of its dividends, the yearly payouts to owners, came out of the owners' own capital, the money they had put in. So some of that 10% was their own money coming back.

[13 p] The sisters show one thing plainly. A railway can be built, and work, and still leave many of its owners with a loss. Keep them in mind. Every test in this chapter comes back to the same question: when a society pays for tomorrow, who holds the promise, and who is left holding the loss?


### Test 1. Does new technology cost a fortune before it pays back?

[15 p] Yes, for these cases. In each one the technology itself worked in the end, even where investors lost. Not every start was costly. Taiwan's first chip lessons cost about 10 million dollars, as reported.

[16 table]
| Technology | What it cost before it paid | What it left behind |
|---|---|---|
| British railways, 1840s | Railway building took 5.7% of Britain's whole yearly output in 1846, 6.7% in 1847 and 4.7% in 1848. By one account, investors hoped for returns the traffic could not pay. About a third of the lines approved in 1844 to 1846 are reported never to have been built | A network that, by one generous estimate, saved the economy about 4% of its yearly output in freight costs by 1865 |
| Electric light, New York, 1882 | The site, the first power station and its wires cost about 300,000 dollars. With interest and fees the first district cost over half a million. For months it charged nothing for the power | The start of the electric grid |
| The microchip, 1960s | The first integrated circuits were costly. Their first buyers were military missile and space programmes | Chips cheap enough for ordinary buyers |
| The internet boom, 1995 to 2000 | Investors poured money into new internet firms. The Nasdaq, an American index of share prices, then fell 77.9% from March 2000 to October 2002. Firms cut about 100,000 jobs in 2001, by one count | Cables, data centres and firms that survived |

[17 p] And many single bets fail even when the technology wins. Among American firms backed by venture capital, money from investors who buy a share of young companies, one study of firms funded from 2004 to 2010 found about three in four did not return what their investors put in. Another count, of deals from 2004 to 2013, found about two thirds returned less than they cost, and fewer than 4% returned ten times or more. Narrower counts find lower shares, down to about a quarter.

[18 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds. New technology costs a great deal before it pays back, and many single bets fail along the way. The sisters' railway was built. Their shares fell from 120 to 20.


### Test 2. Who actually paid?

[20 p] The reader says promises about the future pay the price. That is true, but there are several kinds of promise, and they are not the same.

[21 table]
| Kind of promise | What it means | Where it paid |
|---|---|---|
| Shares | Savers buy a part of the business and share its future profit or loss | The railways. The internet boom. Young firms backed by venture capital |
| Loans from banks | A bank lends now and is paid back with interest later | Late developers such as Korea, as Test 5 shows |
| Government as a buyer | The state promises to buy the product, which lets the maker raise money from others | The microchip. Rockets |
| Government as a payer for research | Taxes, or government borrowing, pay for science that no single firm would fund | The internet's first network. Basic science |
| Profits kept in the firm | A business pays for its own next step | Some research by business today. The share was not measured |

[22 p] In America the money behind research moved from the state to business in nearly sixty years, and business now pays about three quarters. How business raises its share was not checked here. But the state still pays for a large part of the most basic science, the kind that may not pay back for decades, if ever.

[24 p] The microchip shows the state's other role. In 1962, by one study's figures, the American government bought every integrated circuit made in the country. In 1966 it bought 53%, and by 1968 it bought 37%. A huge order of chips for the computers that guided the Apollo flights to the Moon was the largest single use until 1965, when a military missile programme overtook it. The government bought what the chip makers made while chips cost too much for most other buyers.

### Test 3. Was it created money that made the promises possible?

[28 p] This is the boldest part of the claim. Think of an interest rate as the price of borrowing money. When that price is low, borrowing is cheap, and banks and central banks are creating new money fast. If created money is what paid for new technology, the great technology booms should come when borrowing is cheap. Here is what happened in three booms.

[29 table]
| Boom | Was money cheap to borrow? | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| British railway fever, 1843 to 1845 | Yes. The Bank of England's rate was 2.5% to 3.5%, low for the time, as reported | Railway shares broke in late 1845. In the crisis of 1847 the rate rose to 5.5% and then 8% |
| The internet boom, 1995 to 2000 | Not especially. America's main rate stayed between about 4.6% and 6.5%, well above zero, with a short cut in late 1998 | The amount of money in the economy grew at an ordinary pace, about 6% a year to the end of 1999 |
| The boom in young firms, 2020 to 2021 | Yes, very. America's main rate was close to zero | The amount of money in the economy grew 26.8% in the year to February 2021 |

[30 p] So borrowing was cheap in two of the three booms, the railway fever and 2020 to 2021. But the internet boom, which left the most lasting technology of the three, grew while America's main rate was between about 4.6% and 6.5%. Carlota Perez, an economist who studies technology bubbles, wrote this in 2009.

[31 quote] "They are the result of opportunity pull rather than of easy credit push." (Carlota Perez, 2009, on the major technology bubbles)

[32 p] In plain words, she says great technology booms start because people see a huge chance to win, not mainly because borrowing is cheap. Cheap money can pour fuel on the fire once it is lit. But she also calls bubbles "massive episodes of credit creation", and says the 2000s bubble was driven by easy credit.

[33 p] There is a second point. When money is cheap, it can just as easily pour into old things, such as houses, as into new ones. Chapter 02 showed that in 17 rich countries about 60% of bank lending went to property by 2007. Created money is not aimed at technology unless someone aims it.

[34 p] How could created money reach new technology? By two roads. A bank can lend to someone who buys new shares in a young firm, and the bank creates the new money as it makes the loan. In March 2000, as the internet boom peaked, borrowing to buy shares in America hit a record, by one count. Or a government can borrow for research by selling bonds, which are promises to repay later. A central bank can buy those bonds with money it creates. This chapter did not measure that road, so it is left open, not rejected.

[35 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds only in part. Rates were low in two of the three booms, but not in the internet boom. Three booms are too few to rank causes. Government borrowing bought by a central bank was not tested.

[36 check] Q: A friend says central banks printing money is what built the internet. What would you check?
A: The interest rate during the internet boom. From 1995 to 2000 America's main rate stayed between about 4.6% and 6.5%, not near zero, and the money supply grew about 6% a year to 1999. The first internet network was paid for by the defence budget, as reported, and the boom mostly by investors buying shares, some with borrowed money.


### Test 4. The reader's rocket

[38 p] The reader's own example is a company building rockets that land and fly again. It is a fair test, because it is happening now. Here is how it has been paid for, as far as the public record shows.

[39 p] In 2006 NASA, America's space agency, wanted cargo flights to the space station. Instead of designing a rocket and paying a contractor its costs, it offered fixed payments to private companies for reaching set goals. The company that went on to build reusable rockets was paid 396 million dollars, for a rocket and a capsule, when it had met all 40 of its goals in 2012. Another company was paid 288 million dollars by 2013. Was that cheap? NASA had a model that guessed what the rocket alone would have cost to build. The model gave two guesses. One was for the old way, where NASA pays all the builder's costs. The other was for the new way, where NASA pays a fixed price.

[40 table]
| What the rocket alone was expected to cost to develop | Estimate |
|---|---|
| NASA's model, the old way, where NASA pays all the builder's costs | About 1.4 billion to 4.0 billion dollars, depending on the version of the model |
| NASA's model, the new way, with a fixed price | About 0.4 billion to 1.7 billion dollars |
| What the company told Congress it spent on the rocket | About 300 million dollars, as reported |

[41 p] NASA itself warns that these are not budget estimates and that it did not study the gap closely. And the two sides differ: the 396 million also paid for a capsule, while the model priced the rocket alone. NASA's first lesson was that the companies paid over half of the development. Another was that a firm promise to buy makes it much easier to raise money from investors.

[42 p] That lesson is the microchip story again: the state as first customer. And the promises grew: up to 12 cargo flights for about 1.6 billion dollars in 2008, and up to 2.6 billion dollars of astronaut flights in 2014, as reported.

[43 p] Did reuse make launches cheaper? The timing is a surprise. A 2018 paper by a NASA engineer found the price per kilogram to low orbit fell about 20 times, from the old shuttle's cost to the new rocket's advertised price. But it dates the big drop to 2010, before the first rocket stage flew back and landed safely in December 2015. A cheaper way of building and buying rockets came first. Reuse came later.

[44 end of deeper box]

[45 p] And beside the winner there were losers who also raised large sums. A satellite internet company called OneWeb announced it had raised 3.4 billion dollars, went bankrupt in March 2020 with 74 of about 650 planned satellites up, and was then bought by a group led by the British government, as reported. A rocket company called Virgin Orbit flew six missions, two of which failed, and went bankrupt in April 2023. At auction its assets drew bids of about 36 million dollars.

[46 p] What is still unknown? A great deal. Nobody yet knows how much reuse itself saves, because the price fell before the first landing. Whether satellite internet on a huge scale will repay what it has cost, and whether travel to Mars will ever pay back at all, cannot be known yet. The reader's hope about what may follow cannot yet be tested, and the record so far supports only its first steps.

[47 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 holds in part. NASA promised to pay the rocket company for results, and later to buy its flights. NASA says private partners paid over half of the development. Nothing here links the rocket to created money, but its private funding was not fully traced. Other companies that raised billions failed. What the industry will lead to is still unknown.


### Test 5. How did the late developers pay?

[49 p] Chapter 04 left a hint: maybe the reader's chain of promises fits countries that came late better than the first. Here the evidence is richest, and it cuts both ways.

[50 p] Korea fits the reader's picture most closely. From 1973 to 1981 its banks relied on the central bank for 89% of the money they lent to exporters. Most of the growth in the money banks could lend came from the central bank itself. In other words, the central bank made new money, and much of it went out as loans to exporters. In those years Korea pushed into steel, ships and chemicals. Heavy and chemical goods rose from 13% of Korea's exports in 1970 to 92% by 2008. Schooling and land reform also helped, some scholars say. Prices often rose 10% to 20% a year, by one Korean account. That rise began before the heavy industry push. Savers paid too, as their money bought less. By 1997 the biggest groups are reported to have owed about five times what they owned, and Korea needed a rescue package of 58.4 billion dollars. That crisis also had newer causes, such as short-term foreign loans.

[51 p] Taiwan took a different road. In 1976 its state research institute paid an American firm about 10 million dollars, as reported, to teach it how to make chips, and sent 19 engineers to learn. By the end of 1977 its small test plant was making watch chips, reportedly with fewer faulty chips than the teacher's plants. In 1980 the institute set up a first chip company, as reported. In 1987 the state helped found a second, reported to own about 48% of it, with the Dutch firm Philips holding about 27.5%. That firm is reported to have made 61% of the world's chips made to order in late 2023. So the money was public and foreign, and it went into skills and a few firms. Its cost was not measured here against Korea's loans.

[52 p] Malaysia took a third road. Penang had unemployment of about 16% in 1969, by one report. In 1972 a free trade zone opened, and foreign firms were offered up to eight years free of tax. In 1972 Intel opened its first factory outside America there, and other chip and electronics firms followed. A state agency built the zone, as reported. The foreign firms paid for their own factories, as far as these sources show. The cost to Malaysia was the tax it did not collect and the cost of the zone.

[53 p] In 1980 about 19,000 women worked in Penang's electronics plants. Many were young women just out of school in their villages. At one large plant, women were about nine in ten of the assembly workers. The work was hard. The law let these firms employ women at night, and microscope work could blur the eyes. The writer who counted them called her report Bitter Wages. Their work built an industry that lasted. In 2023 electrical and electronic goods were about 40% of everything Malaysia sold abroad, as reported.

[54 p] Malaysia's push into heavy industry did less well. A state car maker, Proton, was set up in the 1980s behind high protection, and by the late 1980s led car sales at home, as reported. In 2016, after years of losses, it was approved a soft loan of 1.5 billion ringgit, and in 2017 a Chinese car maker bought almost half of it. Its sales have since more than doubled, as reported.

[61 p] Here is the claim again, rebuilt so the record can carry it.

[62 steps]
- In these cases new technology did cost a fortune before it paid back, and many single bets failed.
- The promises that paid were largely shares bought by savers and governments promising to buy the product. A promise to buy can help a firm raise private money, as NASA found.
- Money was cheap in some booms but not in the internet boom. Created money reaching technology through government borrowing was not tested.
- Korea aimed central bank money at export loans while its heavy industry grew, at a price. Taiwan and Penang built industries by other roads. The reader's chain fits Korea best, but it is one road among several.

[63 p] And the sisters? The railway they helped to pay for was built. But their shares fell from 120 to 20, and the company had paid dividends partly from its owners' own money. A promise about the future is only as good as the honest accounts behind it. That is the hidden side of paying for tomorrow. Someone holds the promise, and someone holds the loss.

[64 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of four things were shown. First, that the great technology booms came mostly in years of very cheap money, across many more cases than these three. Second, that countries which aimed created money at industry grew faster than those which did not, once other causes are set aside. Third, that governments paid for much research with bonds that central banks bought. Fourth, that the new space industry pays back on the scale its backers hope. The fourth will take years to know.

[65 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Two more stories of public money and new technology
[65.0 p] Electricity for American farms. In 1935 the American government set aside 100 million dollars to lend to farm groups at 2% to 3% interest so they could string power lines into the countryside. Fewer than 1 in 100 of the loans went bad. By about 1945 around half of American farms had power, as reported. This is a case where public lending did pay for a new network, and was repaid.

[65.1 p] The internet's first network was paid for by America's defence department from 1969, as reported. A later network run by the national science agency carried much of the traffic until it was closed on 30 April 1995, when private firms took over, as reported. Public money paid for the slow, early years. Private money paid for the boom.

[67 callout] (Not advice) This chapter weighs an argument about how new technology is paid for. It does not tell you to buy any share, fund or company. Many single bets on new technology lose money.
