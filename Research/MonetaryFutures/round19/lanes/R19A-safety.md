## Section 1. Bills of exchange (H1)

- Plain meaning (verified, Munro handout, Univ. of Toronto, https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/munro5/Bill of Exchange.pdf, opened as PDF): a bill was "an informal letter by which one merchant ordered his agent-banker in some other city to make payment". It combined a loan in one city with a transfer to another, "obviated the necessity of shipping specie and bullion between countries, except when a country's trade was not in balance". It needed four parties in two cities. Basically unchanged 14th to 18th centuries. Munro has a Datini Archive example from de Roover (1948).
- Scene (verified, same source, quoting de Roover 1948 from the Datini Archive, Prato): a bill drawn in Bruges on 12 December 1399 on a bank in Barcelona, 600 ecus at 10s 5d per ecu, accepted 11 January 1400. The return bill (recambium) of 12 February 1400 was 625 ecus at 10s 0d. Short quote of the order: "Pay at usance by this first [letter of exchange]". Arithmetic below.
- Arithmetic (python3 -I): 600 ecus at 10s 5d = 75,000 Barcelonese pence and 625 ecus at 10s 0d = 75,000 pence, so the two Barcelona sums match. The lender in Bruges handed over 600 ecus and got back 625, a gain of 4.17 percent over the usance period (about two to three months across both bills). The gain came from the exchange-rate difference. So this scene shows credit and profit as much as safety.
- Usury and safety (verified, Munro same handout): the return "was not, however, predetermined and fixed", so "it was profit and not interest. Hence bills of exchange escaped the Church's usury ban." Safety detail: several numbered copies were issued "to insure the transaction against theft, loss, or fraud". Also: a loan in one currency repaid in another "obviated the necessity of shipping specie and bullion between countries, except when a country's trade was not in balance." So the same source gives transfer, credit and usury-avoidance together, and avoids shipping metal as a side-effect of settling by balance.

## Section 4. Treasure fleets and shipping risk (H1)
- Verified (Haring, Trade and Navigation between Spain and the Indies in the Time of the Hapsburgs, Harvard 1918, full text opened from https://archive.org/download/tradenavigationb00haririch/tradenavigationb00haririch.pdf): in 1628 the Dutch West India Company's admiral Piet Heyn took the Spanish fleet at Matanzas Bay, Cuba. Haring: "Of the entire Spanish fleet only three vessels escaped". The Dutch sold the booty for 15,000,000 guilders and the company paid a 50 per cent dividend (Haring cites Blok and Fernandez Duro). Haring also lists many storm wrecks, for example 1601 fourteen ships at Vera Cruz in a norther "one thousand men perishing and two millions lost in merchandise". Fifteen driven ashore at Cadiz in a storm in 1563 and again in 1574. So weather was a larger recurring risk than capture. The fleets sailed in convoy under armed escort (Haring, throughout).
- Secondary only (search summaries, not opened): Casado Alonso analysed 893 Burgos insurance policies for the Carrera de Indias, 1565 to 1598 (https://digital.csic.es/bitstream/10261/186650/1/XIII%20R.Cient%c3%adfica_Sevilla_2014_pp.943-960_Casado_Alonso.pdf), and Baskes (Staying Afloat) gives a Cadiz premium near 5.25 per cent in the 18th century and convoy discounts. Not opened, so NOT verified.
- Share of fleet or treasure lost: NOT FOUND from a primary or scholarly source. Do not print any percentage.

## Section 11. Why bills spread: safety versus credit (H1 evidence for and against)
- Verified (Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli, "Your flexible friend", Economic History Review 74(4), 2021, pp. 873-891, https://flore.unifi.it/retrieve/e398c381-830e-179a-e053-3705fe0a4cff/GUIDI%20BOLTON%20Your_flexible_friend_PRINT.pdf, PDF text read): from nearly 2,000 bills and related letters in the Borromei ledgers of Bruges and London, 1436 to 1438, the authors argue the bill was a "far more flexible instrument than has previously been thought". They say the bill provided "a means of remittance" and was also credit: "All bills had some form of inbuilt credit function". On usury they write the debate "is not our prime concern here". Against H1: they do not rank transport safety first.
- Verified (Kohn, "Risk instruments in the medieval and early modern economy", Dartmouth working paper 1999, https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/1999-kohn.pdf, PDF text read): the bill provided remittance "without having to ship specie or bullion" and "was also an instrument of credit, the basis on which merchant banks built an efficient international system of commercial credit". Kohn also treats the shortage of bullion as a major problem, so avoiding shipping metal had a supply side as well as a safety side.
- Secondary (search summaries, not opened): Heidelberg study of Florentine banking says gold transports were easy prey for robbers (https://heiup.uni-heidelberg.de/catalog/view/1284/2206/119499). Spufford is quoted as calling the Champagne fairs "the forcing house" for the bill of exchange (not opened). A Moore chapter says bills cut transport costs and got around state limits on exporting precious metals (not opened).
- Reading: scholars name three motives together, remittance without shipping metal (which includes safety and cost), credit across time and currency, and avoiding the usury ban. None of the opened sources ranks safety as the main motive, and none gives a robbery rate or a safety share. H1 as "safer" is plausible but not established as the main reason.
- Verified (Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli 2021 abstract, same file): they "confirm the arguments of other historians that the main purpose of this instrument was the transfer of capital back and forth across western Europe", with exchange and re-change "playing only a minimal role" in the Borromei's business. So transfer and credit lead, and disguised interest was minor in their ledgers. This cuts against the usury-first story and fits a transfer story, where safety is one part of why transfer by bill beat shipping metal.

## Section 12. Cost and risk of carrying (H1)
- Verified (Statute of Winchester, 13 Edward I, 1285, English text at https://statutes.org.uk/site/the-statutes/thirteenth-century/1285-13-edward-1-the-statute-of-winchester/, page text read): highways between market towns were to be cleared so "neither dyke, underwood, nor bush whereby a man may lurk to do hurt" stood "within two hundred foot" on either side. A lord who failed was "answerable for the felony" if robberies followed there. This shows the Crown of England treated roadside robbery as a standing problem in 1285. It gives no rate of loss.
- Verified (Casado Alonso, "El seguro maritimo en la Carrera de Indias en la epoca de Felipe II", XIII Reunion Cientifica FEHM, Univ. of Seville 2014, pp. 943-960, https://digital.csic.es/bitstream/10261/186650/1/XIII%20R.Cient%c3%adfica_Sevilla_2014_pp.943-960_Casado_Alonso.pdf, PDF text read, in Spanish): in the Burgos insurance records, one-way premiums on the Indies run were about 7 percent (Caribbean islands), 7.3 percent (Colombia routes) and 9.1 to 9.3 percent (New Spain), and a round trip about 20 percent. He writes that, contrary to what is often said, the policies "no eran muy costosos" (were not very costly) despite long voyages and pirates. He compares other routes in the same years at about 4.5 to 12 percent (for example Seville to Rouen 7 percent in 1568, 11 percent in 1587). These are premiums on goods and ships in policies, mostly 1565 to 1587, not on silver shipments alone. Status: verified for the numbers, but the sample is Burgos policies.
- Reading: a 7 to 9 percent price of risk on the Atlantic is large next to a bill-of-exchange spread of about 4 percent over a few months (Scene A), but those are different routes and goods, so this is not a like-for-like comparison. Do not print them as a rivalry.
- Not found: an insurance rate for silver or gold specifically, the Manila galleon loss share, and the share of Spanish silver fleets lost. Do not print any.

## Section 3b. Hawala, firmer source (replaces the thin IMF row above)
- Verified (FATF, "The role of hawala and other similar service providers in money laundering and terrorist financing", October 2013, original at https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/Methodsandtrends/Role-hawalas-in-ml-tf.html, text read from a copy at https://cbr.ru/StaticHtml/File/36805/The_role.pdf): hawala is "traditionally associated with a money transfer mechanism that operated extensively in South Asia many centuries ago", with links along trade routes in the Middle East and East Africa, "a closed system within corridors linked by family, tribe or ethnicity". Providers settle "through trade, cash courier or net settlement, often without any direct wire transfer". Today they serve migrant remittances, and in places such as parts of Somalia and Afghanistan they are "often the only channel", described as "the safest, easiest and cheapest way". FATF's main concern is money laundering and terrorist financing risk, and it says most are legitimate services, with hybrid and criminal types also found. No dollar volume was found. No exact start date: "many centuries ago".
- Link to H1: hawala is the same idea as the bill of exchange, a debt between two partners settled by trade or net balance so no coins travel. The FATF text names a lack of banking access as the reason it persists today, which points to access and cost as much as safety.

# Evidence for and against H1 and H2

## H1: paper promises replaced carried metal because they were safer
For:
- Munro (primary source on how bills worked) says a bill "obviated the necessity of shipping specie and bullion", and bills were issued in numbered copies against "theft, loss, or fraud".
- England in 1285 ordered roadsides cleared 200 foot each side against robbers, and made lords liable for robberies there (Statute of Winchester).
- Hawala today is called "the safest, easiest and cheapest way" in places with no banks (FATF 2013), the same logic as the bill.
- The Spanish fleets sailed under armed escort, and Haring records a large storm and capture loss record, so states did treat the sea route as dangerous.
Against or not showing it:
- The scholars who studied the ledgers (Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli 2021, Kohn 1999) list remittance and credit together, call the bill an instrument of credit, and do not rank safety first.
- Munro ties the bill to the usury ban: the uncertain return was "profit and not interest". Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli say usury is "not our prime concern" and exchange and re-change had only a "minimal role" in the Borromei books.
- The Atlantic insurance premium of 7 to 9 percent one way (Casado Alonso) was judged "not very costly" by him, and the Carrera loss record in Haring is mostly storms, which a paper bill does not cure.
- No source found gives a robbery rate, so "safety was the main reason" is untested. Coin shortage (Kohn) is another reason.

## H2: the danger moved from robbers on the road to fraud, hacks and scams
For:
- US bank robberies fell from 8,496 (2001) to 1,263 (2023), down 85 percent, while IC3 reported losses rose to $20.877 billion in 2025, five times the 2020 figure of $4.2 billion.
- Malaysia: online scam losses RM1.57 billion (2024) to RM2.97 billion (2025), up 89 percent (Home Minister's written reply).
- 95 percent of mule account transactions were authorised by the victim (Deputy Finance Minister, Feb 2025), so money leaves by persuasion, as in the TD case.
- Crypto theft was $3.4 billion in 2025 with a single $1.5 billion theft.
Against or not showing it:
- UK payment fraud losses were flat across six years (£1,204.6m in 2020, £1,279.8m in 2025, +6 percent), and unauthorised fraud fell.
- Crypto theft in 2025 ($3.4 billion) was lower than in 2022 ($3.8 billion).
- The bank robbery fall may come from cameras, cash handling and fewer branches as much as from a move of criminals. The FBI 2012 average take was $8,263 and the 2001 average was $4,587, but they come from different report formats and are not a clean series.
- IC3 reported-loss growth also reflects more reporting. Different countries, definitions and years cannot be added. A robbery count and a fraud dollar total are not the same unit, so the move is a claim about kind, not a measured transfer.
- Highway robbery and fraud both existed before: no source here counts medieval or early modern losses, so there is no old baseline to compare with.
