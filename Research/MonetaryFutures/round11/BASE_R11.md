## L1.2 Commodity money and the gold standard (id rb-02)

Kicker: Tying money to a metal makes it honest. It also makes it stiff, and stiff money breaks people who owe it.

[0 p] Chapter 1 showed that money is a promise. The oldest way to make a promise believable is to tie it to something hard to fake. For thousands of years that something was metal, mostly silver and gold. This chapter is about what metal money did well and why it was finally abandoned.


### Coins: a promise you can bite

[2 p] The first known coins appeared around 600 BC in Lydia, in what is now Turkey. They were lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, stamped with a mark. The stamp was the important part. It said: the king guarantees the weight and purity of this metal. You no longer had to weigh and test every piece.

[3 p] Metal money has a built-in brake. Nobody can create gold by decree. It must be mined, which takes work and time. That scarcity is why people trusted it as a store of value.

[4 callout] (Picture this) Think of a coin as a sealed jar of honey with a royal label. The label saves you from opening and tasting every jar. But the label is only as good as the person who sealed the jar. If the king starts adding water and keeping the label, the jars still look the same while the honey gets thinner.


### Debasement: watering the honey

[6 p] Rulers who needed more money than they had found a trick. Melt the coins, mix in cheaper metal, strike more coins with the same face. This is called debasement. It is the ancient version of printing money.

[7 p] Rome is the classic case. The silver denarius, introduced around 211 BC, was nearly pure silver for a long time. Under Nero in the 60s AD its weight and silver content were trimmed. Through the crisis years of the 200s AD, emperors who had to pay armies kept cutting. By the 260s and 270s the denarius and its successor coins held only a few percent of silver, a thin coating over bronze. Prices soared. In 301 AD the emperor Diocletian issued an edict fixing maximum prices for over a thousand goods, on pain of death. It failed. Goods vanished from markets, and the edict was quietly ignored.

[8 insider] Debasement and modern inflation share a pattern. The first people to spend the new coins, the emperor and his soldiers, got full value. By the time the extra coins spread through the economy, prices had risen, and the last people to receive them lost. Chapter 16 calls this the Cantillon effect.


### Gresham's law: bad money drives out good

[10 p] When two coins have the same face value but one contains more real metal, people spend the bad one and hoard the good one. The good coins disappear from circulation. This is Gresham's law, named after Sir Thomas Gresham, a financial agent to Queen Elizabeth I. The idea is older. Nicole Oresme in the 1300s and Nicolaus Copernicus in 1526 described it. The name was attached in 1858.

[11 p] You can see Gresham's law today. In the United States, silver dimes and quarters minted before 1965 vanished from pockets within a few years after cheaper copper-nickel coins arrived. People kept the silver ones.


### The classical gold standard, about 1870 to 1914

[13 p] Britain drifted onto a gold standard after 1717, when Isaac Newton, as Master of the Mint, set a gold price for the guinea that left silver undervalued. Britain made it law in 1821. After 1871 the newly united Germany switched to gold, and much of the world followed. The United States stopped coining silver dollars in 1873, resumed paying gold for its paper in 1879 and made gold the legal standard in 1900.

[14 p] The rule was simple. Each currency was defined as a fixed weight of gold. Banks and central banks promised to swap paper notes for gold at that fixed rate. Because every major currency was tied to gold, they were also tied to each other. A pound was worth about $4.86 for decades.

[15 diagram] goldstd: Under the gold standard, paper notes are claims on gold at a fixed price. Because each currency is fixed to gold, exchange rates between currencies are fixed too.

[16 p] The system had an automatic balancing idea, first described by the Scottish philosopher David Hume in 1752. A country that imported too much paid in gold. Gold flowed out. Its money supply shrank. Prices and wages fell. Its goods got cheaper, exports recovered, and gold flowed back. Elegant on paper. Painful in real life, because falling wages and prices mean layoffs and bankruptcies.

[17 table]
| What the gold standard did well | What it did badly |
|---|---|
| Kept long-run prices fairly stable | Could not expand money when the economy needed it |
| Made exchange rates predictable for trade and lending | Forced painful deflation on countries losing gold |
| Limited a government's ability to print its way out of trouble | Tied money growth to mining luck, not to the needs of the economy |
| Built trust for international investment | Turned every banking panic into a scramble for scarce gold |


### Why fixed money hurt debtors

[19 p] Here is a key idea of the whole rulebook. When money is fixed but the economy grows, there is more stuff to buy with the same amount of money. Prices fall. That is deflation. Falling prices sound pleasant until you owe money.

[20 callout] (Picture this) A farmer in Kansas borrows $1,000 in 1880 when wheat sells for $1 a bushel. The debt is worth 1,000 bushels. By 1894 wheat sells for about half that. The farmer still owes $1,000, but now it takes about 2,000 bushels to pay. The farmer works twice as hard to pay the same debt. The lender, without lifting a finger, is repaid in money that buys twice as much wheat.

[21 p] That is roughly what happened. From 1873 to 1896, prices in the United States and Britain drifted downward for more than two decades. The economy grew, but farmers and other debtors were crushed by rising real debts. The American Populist movement demanded more money, especially free coinage of silver, to stop the squeeze.

[22 quote] "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." (William Jennings Bryan, Democratic National Convention, 1896)

[23 p] Bryan lost the 1896 election. Then something unplanned ended the deflation anyway: new gold. Huge finds in South Africa and the Klondike, plus a new chemical process for extracting gold from ore, expanded the world's gold supply. From 1897 prices rose gently, and farm distress eased. The money supply had been set by miners, not by anyone's plan.

[24 myth] Myth: Gold-backed money is always fair to everyone.
Reality: Gold money is fair to lenders and savers in a growing economy, because it gains buying power. It is harsh on borrowers, because their debts get heavier as prices fall. Whether a money is fair depends on whether you owe it or own it, and on which way prices are moving. Chapter 8 calls these the seasons of money.

[25 insider] The owner's project tested a popular claim that the world's gold, times ten, equals the world's debt. The ratio is real today, about 12 times, but in the past it wandered from about 35 times in 1971 to about 53 times in 2000 and 23 times in 2010. A real mechanism would show a stable ratio. This one moves mainly with the gold price, so it is a coincidence, not a rule. No legal link between gold and dollar credit has existed since 1971.

[26 check] Q: Why did falling prices from 1873 to 1896 hurt farmers more than bankers?
A: Farmers owed fixed dollar debts while the prices they received for crops fell, so each dollar of debt took more crops to repay. Bankers were owed those fixed dollars, which bought more as prices fell.

[27 check] Q: What is Gresham's law, in one sentence?
A: When two moneys have the same face value but different real value, people spend the worse one and hoard the better one, so the bad money circulates and the good money disappears.

Rules: Hard money protects savers and squeezes borrowers. Know which one you are. / When rulers need money they cannot raise honestly, they water down the currency. Watch for it. / When two moneys circulate side by side, the one people hoard is the one they trust.

## L1.3 War, Bretton Woods and the dollar's crown (id rb-03)

Kicker: Two world wars moved the world's gold to America, and in 1944 the dollar became the hub of every other currency.

[0 p] The gold standard of Chapter 2 worked best in peacetime. War is the most expensive thing a nation ever does, and governments at war will always choose victory over a fixed gold price. This chapter follows how the wars broke the old system and how a new one was designed in a New Hampshire hotel.


### The wars break the rules

[2 p] When the First World War began in 1914, the warring countries suspended gold convertibility. They printed money and borrowed heavily to pay for the fighting. Between 1914 and 1920 prices roughly doubled in the United States, rose even more in Britain, and rose far more again in Germany.

[3 p] After the war, countries tried to rebuild the gold standard. Britain returned to gold in 1925 at its old prewar rate of about $4.86, a decision made by Winston Churchill as Chancellor. The pound was now overvalued, so British exports were too expensive, and the country suffered high unemployment. John Maynard Keynes attacked the decision in a pamphlet called The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill.

[4 quote] "In truth, the gold standard is already a barbarous relic." (John Maynard Keynes, A Tract on Monetary Reform, 1923)

[5 p] When the Great Depression struck after 1929, the gold standard spread the damage. Countries losing gold had to raise interest rates and shrink their money supply in the middle of a collapse. Britain left gold in 1931. The United States effectively left domestic gold in 1933, when President Roosevelt ordered citizens to hand in most gold coins and bullion, then raised the official price from $20.67 to $35 an ounce in 1934. Research by economists Barry Eichengreen and Jeffrey Sachs found that countries that left gold earlier generally recovered sooner.


### Why America ended up with the gold

[7 p] Through the 1930s and the Second World War, gold poured into the United States. Europeans sent gold to safety. Allied countries paid for American weapons, food and fuel. By the late 1940s the US government held roughly two thirds of all the gold owned by the world's governments. America also had the only large industrial economy that had not been bombed.

[8 callout] (Picture this) Imagine a long poker night where every player keeps losing chips to one player who also owns the casino's only working kitchen. By dawn that player holds most of the chips. When the others want to start a new game, they have little choice but to use that player's chips as the house standard. That is roughly the position of the United States in 1944.


### Bretton Woods, July 1944

[10 p] In July 1944, delegates from 44 Allied nations met at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. Two men shaped the talks. Keynes, for Britain, proposed a new international clearing unit called bancor, which would push both deficit and surplus countries to adjust. Harry Dexter White, for the US Treasury, proposed a system built around the dollar. The country with the gold won the argument.

[11 list]
- The US dollar was fixed to gold at $35 an ounce. Foreign central banks, not private citizens, could swap dollars for gold at that price.
- Other currencies were pegged to the dollar at fixed rates, allowed to move only about 1% either way. Pegs could be changed only for a serious imbalance.
- The International Monetary Fund (IMF) was created to lend to countries with short-term payment problems, so they would not need to devalue or crash their economies.
- The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, now part of the World Bank, was created to finance rebuilding and development.

[12 diagram] bw-system: Bretton Woods, 1944: other currencies pegged to the dollar, and the dollar pegged to gold at $35 an ounce. The dollar became the hub, gold the anchor.

[13 insider] This made the dollar the hub of a wheel. To defend its peg, a country needed a stock of dollars to buy its own currency when it fell. So central banks around the world began to hold dollars, and soon US Treasury bills, as their main reserves. The reserve role of the dollar, which still shapes your mortgage rate today, was born in this design.


### A system that worked, then strained

[15 p] For about two decades the system delivered. Europe and Japan rebuilt quickly. The United States gave about $13 billion in Marshall Plan aid from 1948 to 1952, which put dollars into European hands. Trade grew fast, and inflation was mostly moderate. Many economists call the 1950s and 1960s a golden age of growth.

[16 p] But the system had a crack in its foundation, spotted early by the Belgian economist Robert Triffin. He laid it out in testimony to Congress in 1959 and in a book in 1960.


### The Triffin dilemma

[18 p] The world needed more and more dollars as trade grew. The only way foreigners could get more dollars was for the United States to send more dollars abroad than it took back, by buying imports, investing overseas, or spending on military bases and aid. Year after year, that piled up dollars in foreign hands.

[19 p] But each of those dollars was a claim on American gold at $35. As foreign dollar holdings grew, they eventually exceeded the gold the US held. By the early 1960s that line had been crossed. If everyone asked for gold at once, the US could not pay.

[20 callout] (Picture this) A popular bakery issues tickets, each good for one loaf. Tickets are so trusted that the whole town uses them as money. The town grows and wants more tickets. The bakery keeps printing them, but it cannot bake much more bread. Every new ticket makes the town happier today and the bakery's promise weaker tomorrow. If the bakery stops printing, the town's trade seizes up. If it keeps printing, one day a crowd shows up wanting bread for every ticket. That is the Triffin dilemma.

[21 p] The dilemma is this. If the US stops supplying dollars, the world economy runs short of money. If it keeps supplying them, confidence in the gold link erodes. Either way, the system is on a timer.

[22 p] Governments tried patches. In 1961 the US and European central banks formed the London Gold Pool to hold the market price of gold at $35. France, under President Charles de Gaulle, began swapping its dollars for gold and publicly called for a return to a pure gold standard in 1965. The French finance minister Valery Giscard d'Estaing complained of America's exorbitant privilege: the ability to pay for things with its own currency that others must earn. The Gold Pool collapsed in 1968. The countdown to 1971, the subject of Chapter 5, had begun.

[23 myth] Myth: The US took the world's gold by force or trickery.
Reality: Most of it arrived through ordinary channels: European capital fleeing war and instability in the 1930s, and Allied payments for American goods during the war. The result gave the US enormous leverage at Bretton Woods, but it was mostly the product of the US being the safe, unbombed workshop of the world, not a heist.

[24 insider] The United States still holds about 261.5 million ounces of gold, about 8,134 tonnes, according to the Treasury's own records for September 2026. It books that gold at $42.22 an ounce, a price set by law in 1973, so the official value is about $11 billion. At market prices it is worth about $1.08 trillion. The gap is a fossil of the Bretton Woods era still sitting in today's government accounts.

[25 check] Q: Under Bretton Woods, could an American citizen walk into a bank and swap dollars for gold?
A: No. Only foreign central banks and governments could convert dollars to gold at $35. American citizens were not even allowed to own most gold bullion from 1933 until 1975.

[26 check] Q: State the Triffin dilemma in two sentences.
A: The world needed a growing supply of dollars, which the US could only provide by running deficits with the rest of the world. But the more dollars piled up abroad, the less believable the promise to convert them into gold became.

Rules: Wars break money rules. Expect any fixed money promise to be suspended in a great crisis. / The country that holds the reserves usually writes the rules of the next system. / A promise that must grow to keep the world supplied will eventually outgrow what backs it.

## L1.7 The musical chairs question (id rb-07)

Kicker: If all money is borrowed and interest is owed on top, can everyone ever pay? The honest answer is yes in a quiet world, and not always in a real one.

[0 p] Chapter 6 showed that money is born as debt. That raises a question almost everyone hits sooner or later, and the owner of this project put it in a sharp form.

[1 quote] "Imagine I give a person my own currency YYY$10, but I tell them they must pay me back 5% interest, but no matter how much they work they can't repay if there is only YYY$10 in circulation." (The owner of this project, in their own words)

[2 p] It feels like musical chairs. Ten chairs, ten and a half people. Someone must always be left standing, or someone must always borrow more to keep the music playing. Let us test the intuition carefully, because it is half right, and the half that is right matters a great deal.

[3 diagram] musical-chairs: The worry: YYY$10 in circulation, YYY$10.50 owed. If the stock of money is all there is, someone must default or borrow more.


### Why it feels impossible

[5 p] The worry treats money as a fixed pile, a stock. You hand out 10. You ask for 10.50 back. There are only 10 in existence. Arithmetic seems to say no.

[6 p] The missing piece is time. Money is not used once. It moves. The same coin can pay many debts in a year, because each person who receives it spends it again. Economists call how often money changes hands its velocity. Debts are paid from flows over time, not from the stock all at once.


### The real mechanism: the lender spends

[8 callout] (Picture this) You are the lender on a small island. You lend YYY$10 to Bea, a fisher, at 5% a year. Each year Bea owes you YYY$0.50 of interest. Where does she get it? From you. You need to eat, so you buy YYY$0.50 of fish from Bea each year. She takes that YYY$0.50 and pays you the interest. Next year, the same thing. The money supply never changes. It stays at YYY$10. Bea pays her interest forever. Nobody is left standing.

[9 table]
| Year | Money in circulation | Bea's loan | Interest Bea pays you | You spend back on fish | Anyone short? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | YYY$10 | YYY$10 | YYY$0.50 | YYY$0.50 | No |
| 2 | YYY$10 | YYY$10 | YYY$0.50 | YYY$0.50 | No |
| 10 | YYY$10 | YYY$10 | YYY$0.50 | YYY$0.50 | No |
| Total over 10 years | Still YYY$10 | Still YYY$10 | YYY$5.00 paid | YYY$5.00 spent | No |

[10 p] Interest is a payment for a service, the use of money over time. As long as the person who receives interest spends it back into the economy, on goods, wages, rent or taxes, the money circulates and can be earned again. Even repaying the principal is possible. Bea can sell you more fish over time and pay down the YYY$10. When she finishes, the money she borrowed is gone, because the loan that created it is gone. That is not a failure. It is the loop from Chapter 6 running in reverse.

[11 p] Economists who modeled this carefully, including critics of mainstream economics such as Steve Keen, have shown that a pure credit economy can pay interest in a steady state without needing endless new loans. So the strict version of the musical chairs claim, that interest can never be paid without more debt, is not correct.

[12 myth] Myth: Because banks only create the principal and never the interest, the system needs ever more debt just to pay interest, or it collapses.
Reality: Interest is paid out of the circulating flow of money, and banks spend their interest income on salaries, rent, dividends and taxes, which puts it back into circulation. In a steady state, interest can be paid indefinitely. The real dangers come from somewhere else, and they are listed below.


### Where the intuition is right

[14 p] Now the important part. The owner's instinct points at real weaknesses. They are just different from the arithmetic one.

[15 list]
- Savers who do not spend. If the people receiving interest save it instead of spending it, money piles up with them and drains from everyone else. If savers as a group want their savings to keep growing, someone else must keep going deeper into debt. This is an accounting fact, made famous by the economist Wynne Godley: for every saver accumulating financial assets, some sector, households, businesses, government or foreigners, must be adding debt.
- Debt can outgrow income. Paying interest is possible. Paying more and more interest on debts that grow faster than incomes is not sustainable. Global debt is about $365 trillion, roughly 310% of world output, according to the Institute of International Finance in 2026.
- Repayment destroys money. If many borrowers try to pay down debt at once, deposits vanish, spending falls, prices and incomes drop, and the remaining debts get harder to pay.
- Panics need cash now, not over time. In a crisis everyone wants to settle at once. Then the stock of money really is too small for the pile of claims, unless a central bank steps in.

[16 quote] "The more the debtors pay, the more they owe." (Irving Fisher, The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions, 1933)

[17 p] Fisher saw this in the Great Depression. When everyone sells assets and cuts spending to repay debt, prices fall. Falling prices make each remaining dollar of debt heavier. Paying down debt can make debt burdens worse.


### Minsky: stability breeds instability

[19 p] The economist Hyman Minsky described three kinds of borrowers. Hedge borrowers can pay both interest and principal from income. Speculative borrowers can pay interest from income but must keep rolling over the principal. Ponzi borrowers cannot even pay the interest from income, and must borrow more or sell assets that they hope keep rising.

[20 p] Minsky's key idea is that long calm periods push people from the first kind toward the third. Good times make lenders and borrowers confident, so they take on more debt against rising asset prices. Then a small shock, like a dip in house prices, reveals that many borrowers were Ponzi borrowers all along. The 2008 crisis is often called a Minsky moment, a phrase coined by economist Paul McCulley.

[21 diagram] treadmill: The debt treadmill: when debt grows faster than income, the interest bill rises, so more borrowing is needed just to keep pace. Calm years tempt more people onto the faster belt.


### Why credit growth became structural after 1971

[23 p] Under gold, a bank boom eventually ran into a gold shortage and stopped. After 1971 that brake was gone. Each time a slump threatened, central banks could cut rates and governments could borrow, so recessions were shorter but debt rarely got cleared out. Over decades, debt grew faster than incomes. The system became like a bicycle that is stable only while it keeps moving forward.

[24 p] The US government is the clearest current case. According to Treasury data gathered by this project, the government spent about $7.40 trillion in fiscal year 2026 and collected about $5.40 trillion, a deficit of about $1.99 trillion. Even before interest, it ran a primary deficit of about $941 billion, close to 2.9% of the economy. Interest itself cost about $1.05 trillion. The average rate on its marketable debt is about 3.5%, but new borrowing costs closer to 4.9%, and about 36% of the debt reprices within a year. Unless deficits shrink or rates fall, the interest bill climbs on its own.

[25 insider] The owner's notes say there is only about $15,000 of liquid money per person on earth against about $346 trillion of global debt, and conclude that we live in a world of scarce settlement capacity, not scarce money. The figures need updating: world broad money is now about $19,500 per person, and global debt is about $365 trillion. But the insight is sharp. Normally debts are paid slowly from flows, so a small stock of money services a huge stock of debt. In a panic, everyone wants settlement at once, and the stock is far too small. That is exactly why central banks act as lenders of last resort in a crisis.

[26 check] Q: In the island story, what single behavior would turn the YYY$10 economy into a real musical chairs game?
A: If you, the lender, stopped spending your interest and hoarded it. Then money would drain out of circulation into your savings, and Bea would eventually be unable to pay without borrowing more or defaulting.

[27 check] Q: Is it true that interest can never be paid without new loans?
A: No. In a steady state, interest can be paid indefinitely as long as interest income is spent back into circulation. The real risks are debt growing faster than income, savers hoarding, mass repayment shrinking the money supply, and panics.

Rules: Interest can be paid from a circulating flow. Debt that grows faster than income cannot be paid forever. / When everyone repays at once, money shrinks and debts get heavier. Pace your debt so you never have to sell in a panic. / Calm years breed risky borrowing. Be most careful when lending feels safest.

## L1.8 The seasons of money (id rb-08)

Kicker: Money has weather. In some decades it melts, in others it hardens, and the same choice can make you rich in one season and poor in the next.

[0 p] So far we have looked at how money is made. Now we look at how it behaves over time. Prices do not move in a straight line. They move in long seasons, some inflationary, some deflationary. Most people live through only two or three seasons, so they assume the one they grew up in is normal. The rulebook exists so you do not make that mistake.


### Two kinds of weather

[2 list]
- Inflation: prices in general rise, so each unit of money buys less. Debts get lighter in real terms. Savings in cash shrink in real terms.
- Deflation: prices in general fall, so each unit of money buys more. Debts get heavier in real terms. Cash gains real value, but jobs and incomes usually suffer.

[3 callout] (Picture this) Think of money as ice. In a warm season, inflation, ice held in your hand melts, so you want to turn it into something that does not melt, like a house or a tool, and owing ice to someone is cheap because it shrinks while you owe it. In a cold season, deflation, ice keeps its shape and even grows, so holding ice is smart and owing ice is dangerous because your debt grows heavier.


### A timeline of seasons, 1870 to 2026

[5 diagram] seasons: Seasons of money from 1870 to 2026: inflationary eras (wars, the 1970s, 2021 onward) and deflationary or disinflationary eras (1873 to 1896, 1929 to 1933, 1982 to 2020).

[6 table]
| Years | Season | Main cause | Who tended to win | Who tended to lose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1873 to 1896 | Long deflation | Gold standard, money growth slower than output | Lenders, savers | Farmers and other debtors |
| 1897 to 1914 | Mild inflation | New gold discoveries | Debtors, farmers | Fixed-income holders, slightly |
| 1914 to 1920 | War inflation | War spending, gold suspended | Debtors, governments | Savers, bondholders |
| 1929 to 1933 | Deflationary collapse | Bank failures, money supply fell about a third | Holders of safe cash | Debtors, workers, banks |
| 1941 to 1951 | War and postwar inflation with capped rates | War debt, Fed held rates down | The government as debtor | Savers in bonds and deposits |
| 1966 to 1981 | The Great Inflation | Deficits, oil shocks, loose policy, no gold anchor | Fixed-rate borrowers, owners of gold and real assets | Savers, bondholders, fixed incomes |
| 1982 to 2007 | Disinflation, long bond summer | Volcker's credibility, globalization | Bondholders, stock owners | Late borrowers at high fixed rates |
| 2008 to 2020 | Low inflation, zero rates | Debt overhang after 2008, cautious banks | Asset owners | Savers earning nearly nothing |
| 2021 to 2023 | Inflation surge | Pandemic spending, supply shocks, energy | Borrowers with low fixed mortgages | Renters, savers, long bond holders |
| 2024 to 2026 | Sticky, energy-led inflation | Oil shock, deficits, AI investment boom | Owners of energy and real assets | Wage earners whose pay lags prices |

[7 p] The last row comes from this project's 2026 data. US consumer energy prices rose about 20.4% over a year, oil traded near $98 a barrel in October, and a key inflation measure ran at 5.0% annualized in the second quarter. Real hourly pay fell about 0.7% in the year to September 2026. The Fed under Kevin Warsh raised rates in September 2026, against the President's wishes.

[8 insider] Financial repression is the polite name for a quiet trick governments use to shrink debt. They keep interest rates below inflation, so lenders to the government lose real value every year. Economists Carmen Reinhart and Belen Sbrancia showed in 2011 that the US and UK used this after the Second World War to cut their debt burdens substantially. A mild version is happening now: the average interest rate on US government debt is about 3.2 to 3.5%, while nominal income has grown about 5.7% a year over the past decade, so inflation is quietly shrinking the debt relative to the economy.


### Why governments prefer mild inflation

[10 list]
- It shrinks debt. A government that owes trillions benefits when each future dollar is worth a little less.
- Wages are sticky downward. People resist pay cuts. With mild inflation, a business can let real wages adjust by giving smaller raises instead of cutting pay, which keeps more people employed.
- It leaves room to cut rates. If inflation is 2% and rates are 4%, a central bank can cut rates enough to make borrowing cheap in real terms during a slump. With zero inflation, there is less room.
- Deflation is scarier. Once people expect falling prices, they delay buying, firms delay hiring, and debts grow heavier. That spiral is hard to stop.

[11 p] New Zealand adopted the first formal inflation target in 1990. The Federal Reserve formally adopted a 2% target in 2012. The owner calls inflation a circulation incentive, a leaking bucket that pushes people to spend or invest rather than hoard cash. That is a fair description of how a mild target works, and it is also why holding idle cash is a slow loss in most modern seasons.

[12 quote] "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." (Milton Friedman, 1963)

[13 p] Friedman's line is famous and partly true. Over long periods, sustained high inflation does come with fast money growth. But most economists today add that supply shocks, like the oil embargoes of the 1970s or the 2026 energy shock, can drive prices up for a while even without fast money growth, and that expectations matter a great deal. Where scholars disagree is how much weight to give each.


### Deflation traps

[15 p] The United States from 1929 to 1933 is the classic trap. About 9,000 banks failed. The money supply fell by about a third, as Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz documented. Prices fell by about a quarter. Unemployment reached about 25%. Fisher's debt deflation, from Chapter 7, ran at full speed.

[16 p] Japan is the modern case. After its stock and property bubble burst around 1990, banks were slow to admit losses, households and firms paid down debt for years, and prices drifted slightly downward for much of the period from the late 1990s to the early 2010s. The Nikkei stock index did not regain its 1989 peak until 2024. The economist Richard Koo called this a balance sheet recession: everyone repaying debt at once, so nobody borrows to spend.


### A store of value changes jobs with the season

[18 p] Here is a subtle point that most people never notice. An asset is not simply safe or risky. It is a tool that works in some seasons and fails in others.

[19 table]
| Asset | Works as a store of value in | Fails in | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and savings accounts | Deflation | Inflation | Cash gained real value in 1930 to 1933, lost it in the 1970s |
| Long-term government bonds | Disinflation and deflation | Inflation and rising rates | Great from 1982 to 2020, lost close to half their market value from 2020 to 2023 |
| Gold | Inflation, crisis and loss of trust | Disinflation with high real rates | Rose from $35 to $850 by January 1980, then fell to around $255 by 1999 to 2001 |
| Fixed-rate debt (as a borrower) | Inflation | Deflation | A 3% mortgage from 2021 became a gift as prices rose |
| Stocks of strong companies | Moderate inflation and growth | Deep deflation and very high real rates | Fell sharply in 1929 to 1932 and in 1973 to 1974 |

[20 insider] Gold is usually called an inflation hedge, but from 1980 to 2001, a period that still had inflation, gold lost most of its value in real terms. What gold really hedges is a loss of trust in the money or the people managing it. When trust is high and real interest rates are high, gold tends to struggle. When trust falls, gold shines. In 2025 central banks bought about 863 tonnes of gold, a sign that some governments trust the system less.

[21 myth] Myth: There is one safe asset you can buy and forget.
Reality: Safety depends on the season. Long-term US government bonds are free of default risk, but holders lost close to half their market value from 2020 to 2023 as rates rose. Cash is safe from crashes but loses value to inflation. The most reliable protection is to understand which season you are in and to avoid betting everything on one season continuing.

[22 check] Q: You have a fixed-rate mortgage and inflation jumps to 8%. Are you better or worse off in real terms?
A: Better off as a borrower, as long as your income keeps up. Your fixed payments and remaining debt shrink in real terms because each dollar you repay buys less.

[23 check] Q: Why did Japan's long stagnation happen even though interest rates were near zero?
A: Households, firms and banks were focused on repairing their balance sheets after the 1990 bust, so they paid down debt instead of borrowing to spend. Cheap money did not help when nobody wanted to borrow.

Rules: Know your season. Ask whether money is melting or hardening before you decide to save, borrow or invest. / Mild inflation is the default policy goal. Idle cash slowly loses in most modern seasons. / No asset is safe in every season. Spread your bets across seasons, not just across names.

## L1.9 The deflation debate: would anyone spend a money that grows? (id rb-defl)

Kicker: If a dollar buys more tomorrow, why spend it today? The oldest argument against hard money, why it never ends, and what both sides miss.

[0 p] Chapter 8 showed that money has seasons. This chapter takes one question from the cold season and follows it all the way down, because it sits under almost every fight about gold, Bitcoin and central banks. The question is simple: if I know my $1 will buy more tomorrow, why would I spend it today?


### The case against a growing money

[2 steps]
- Prices are expected to fall, so people wait to buy cars, homes and machines.
- Shops and factories sell less, so they cut prices further and cut jobs.
- Workers who lose income spend even less. Borrowers find their debts heavier, because the money they owe is worth more while their income shrinks.
- Defaults rise, banks pull back, and the loop feeds itself. Economists call this a deflationary spiral. Irving Fisher described its debt side in 1933 and called it debt deflation.

[3 p] This is why almost every major central bank aims for a little inflation, usually 2%, rather than zero or falling prices. The cases they point to are the Great Depression and Japan's lost decades, both in chapter 8.


### The case for a growing money

[5 list]
- You still need things today. Food, rent, medicine and school fees do not wait. People keep buying phones and televisions even though they know next year's model will be cheaper and better.
- Waiting has a cost. A sofa you buy next year is a year without a sofa. Economists call this time preference: people value having things sooner.
- History has good deflations. From the 1870s to the 1890s US prices fell for years while output, railways and industry grew fast, because new technology made things cheaper to produce.
- The data are weaker than the fear. A 2015 Bank for International Settlements study covering about 140 years and up to 38 economies found only a weak link between falling consumer prices and slower growth, mostly driven by the Great Depression. The damage came mainly from falling property prices combined with private debt.
- Discipline. When money cannot be printed, governments and companies must fund projects from real savings, so fewer bad projects get started.


### Why the fight never ends: two sides, two different deflations

[7 diagram] two-deflations: The same falling prices with opposite causes. Good deflation starts with cheaper production. Bad deflation starts with collapsing demand and heavy debt.

[8 p] The argument goes in circles because each side pictures a different deflation. The hard-money side pictures good deflation: things get cheaper because we got better at making them, incomes hold steady, and every paycheck buys more. The central-bank side pictures bad deflation: prices fall because nobody is buying, while debts stay fixed. Both have happened. Which one you get depends mostly on one thing: how much debt is in the system when prices start to fall.

[9 insider] Today's world carries record debt. The US government alone owes $40.44T. In a deflation every one of those dollars gets heavier to repay. That is the real reason no heavily indebted government will choose a deflationary money. The debate sounds like it is about shoppers delaying purchases. Underneath, it is about who carries the debt.


### Spend the worse money, keep the better one

[11 p] An older rule explains what people actually do when they can choose between two monies. It is Gresham's law: bad money drives out good. People spend the money that is losing value and hoard the one that is gaining. In Tudor England people spent the clipped and debased coins and kept the full-weight ones at home.

[12 callout] (Picture this) On 22 May 2010 a programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas, the first well-known purchase of real goods with Bitcoin. At about $86,000 a coin in early October 2026, those coins would be worth roughly $860 million. After stories like that, almost nobody spends Bitcoin on pizza. They spend dollars and keep the Bitcoin. That is Gresham's law in a single slice.

[13 p] So a money expected to grow tends to become a store of value, while a money that slowly melts becomes the one people spend. That is why the medium of exchange argument against deflationary money is partly right and partly beside the point. People do not stop spending. They spend the other money. Today that other money is the dollar, or a dollar stablecoin, sitting next to gold or Bitcoin in the same wallet.

[14 table]
| Question | Mild-inflation camp | Hard-money camp |
|---|---|---|
| Will people stop spending? | Enough of them to start a spiral | No, needs and time preference keep them buying |
| What about debt? | Inflation shrinks debts in real terms | Debt should be smaller to begin with |
| Who gains? | Borrowers, including governments | Savers and lenders |
| Who loses? | Savers and people on fixed incomes | Borrowers and the heavily leveraged |
| Wages | Mild inflation lets real wages adjust without cutting paychecks | Falling prices raise real wages without raises |
| Best evidence | 1929 to 1933, Japan after 1990 | 1870s to 1890s, technology prices today |

[15 myth] Myth: Falling prices always mean a weak economy.
Reality: Prices of televisions, computers and phones have fallen for decades while the industries making them boomed. What hurts is falling prices plus heavy debt plus collapsing demand, not falling prices alone.

[16 insider] This matters for the future this desk studies. Robots, AI and cheap solar push the cost of making things down, a good-deflation force. A debt-heavy system cannot let that show up as falling prices, so it creates enough new money to keep prices rising gently. The productivity gain does not vanish. It flows to whoever receives the new money first and to owners of scarce things such as land, energy and platforms. That is the link to the Cantillon effect and the platform toll booths in chapter 16.

[17 check] Q: If prices are expected to fall 2% a year, why might a family still buy a fridge today?
A: Because it needs a fridge now. Waiting a year to save 2% means a year without a fridge. Real needs and time preference keep spending going. A spiral starts when incomes fall and debts bite, not from cheaper prices alone.

[18 check] Q: Why do most people spend dollars and keep gold or Bitcoin?
A: Gresham's law. When two monies circulate side by side, people spend the one expected to lose value and hoard the one expected to gain.

Rules: A deflation is good or bad depending on its cause and on how much debt sits in the system. / People do not stop spending when one money grows. They spend the other money and keep the growing one. / The fight over inflation is a fight over who carries the debt.

## L1.12 The view from a smaller currency: Malaysia, savings and where money lands first (id rb-em)

Kicker: Why a calm local price tag can hide a shrinking passport, how compulsory savings fund the home government, why talent and capital crowd into a few hubs, and whether Singapore was luck, strategy or someone else's plan.

[0 p] Most of this Rulebook looks at money from the top, from Washington and the dollar. This chapter looks at it from the ground in a smaller economy. Malaysia is the worked example because its story touches every idea in this book, but the same logic applies to Thailand, Indonesia, the Philippines, Turkey, South Africa and most of the roughly 180 currencies on earth.


### The comfortable picture

[2 p] A Malaysian worker sees a reassuring picture. Consumer inflation was 1.8% in September 2026 by the statistics department's count. The Employees Provident Fund (EPF), where most private-sector workers' retirement money must go by law, paid a 6.15% dividend for 2025 on investment assets of about RM1.41 trillion. Savings grow faster than prices. Why worry?

[3 p] The picture is true, but it is measured with only one ruler: the ringgit, inside Malaysia. There is a second ruler most people never check.


### Two rulers: the home price tag and the passport price tag

[5 diagram] two-yardsticks: What one ringgit buys abroad, then and now. Rates are approximate. The 1997 figures are before the Asian crisis.

[6 p] Before the 1997 Asian crisis a US dollar cost about 2.50 ringgit. In October 2026 it costs about 4.08. So a ringgit buys roughly 39% fewer dollars than it did 29 years ago. Against the Singapore dollar the slide is steeper, from about 1.75 ringgit per Singapore dollar in early 1997 to about 3.2 in 2026. Inside Malaysia the loss is hidden, because local prices move gently. Outside, it shows up the moment you pay an overseas tuition bill, a foreign hospital, a holiday, an imported phone or anything priced in dollars.

[7 list]
- Imported goods: fuel, food ingredients, machines, medicines and electronics are often priced in dollars. A weaker ringgit raises their cost in ringgit.
- Education and health abroad: a family saving for a child's degree in the US or Britain is really saving in dollars or pounds, whatever its account says.
- Travel and work options: the same salary buys fewer days abroad, and moving to a richer country costs more.
- Assets priced globally: shares of global companies, gold and Bitcoin all cost more ringgit when the ringgit falls.

[8 callout] (Be fair to both sides) Currencies swing both ways. The ringgit weakened to about 4.80 per dollar in early 2024, its weakest since the 1998 crisis, then strengthened to about 4.08 by October 2026. Short moves reverse. For a lifetime of savings the long trend is what counts, and since 1997 the passport ruler has shrunk.


### Why the home price tag looks so calm

[10 list]
- Subsidies and price controls. The government absorbs part of the cost of fuel, electricity and some foods. In September 2026, for example, protection on household electricity bills was widened from 600 to 800 kWh a month, which helped pull the utilities index down. The cost does not disappear. It moves onto the government's budget, and later onto taxes, debt or a subsidy cut.
- The basket. Official inflation tracks an average household's local shopping. It does not include overseas school fees or the cost of moving abroad.
- Local wages and rents set many prices, and they move slowly.


### How Washington reaches a Malaysian wallet

[12 steps]
- The Fed raises rates or the Treasury sells more bonds, so dollar yields rise.
- Global money leaves smaller markets to earn more in dollars, more safely. Foreign holders sell local bonds and shares.
- The local currency falls. Imports get dearer, so inflation is imported even though nothing changed at home.
- The local central bank must choose: raise its own rates to defend the currency, which hurts local borrowers and businesses, or let the currency fall and accept dearer imports.
- Either way, households pay for a decision made in Washington. When the Fed eases or the dollar weakens, the flow reverses, which is part of why the ringgit strengthened in 2025 and 2026.


### The rule no small economy can escape

[14 diagram] trinity: The impossible trinity. A country can hold only two corners at once. Each side of the triangle is a choice with a cost.

[15 p] Economists call it the impossible trinity. A country cannot at the same time fix its exchange rate, let money flow freely in and out, and set its own interest rates. It must give one up. The US keeps free flows and its own rates, and lets the dollar float. Malaysia in September 1998 chose differently: it imposed capital controls and pegged the ringgit at 3.80 to the dollar, keeping control of its rates. The peg lasted until July 2005. Singapore chose a third path: it keeps money flowing freely and steers its exchange rate, and accepts that its interest rates largely follow world rates.


### Captive savings: the EPF and the home government

[17 p] The EPF is one of the largest pension funds in Asia, and also the biggest single holder of Malaysian government bonds, with about 28% of them according to the fund's statement for December 2023. Its policy is to place about 70% to 80% of new money inside Malaysia. About 39% of its assets were overseas in late 2025, and in 2024 the overseas part, about 37% of assets, earned about half of all investment income.

[18 insider] This is the same captive-demand playbook Level 2 chapter 1 showed in Washington, where stablecoins are required by law to hold Treasury bills. A government with a large pool of compulsory savings has a reliable buyer for its debt at home. That keeps its borrowing cheap and its markets steady. The cost is that workers' retirement money is concentrated in the same economy that pays their wages, in the same currency.

[19 p] To be fair, the EPF has done well in ringgit, and the leak is slower than many fear. The ringgit's fall against the dollar since 1997 works out to roughly 1.7% a year, so a 6% ringgit dividend is still very roughly 4% a year in dollar terms. The leak is real but quiet. It matters most for people whose future spending will happen abroad.


### When the local economy hits bottom

[21 p] The real test comes in a crisis. In 1997 and 1998 the ringgit fell from about 2.50 to about 4.88 per dollar at its worst in January 1998, roughly halving what Malaysian savings were worth in world terms within months. Stocks crashed, companies with dollar debts were crushed, and the government reached for capital controls. Malaysia recovered, but the lesson stayed: in a crisis, people with only local assets and a local passport have the fewest exits. People with income or savings in a strong currency, or skills that are paid in one, have options.

[22 p] Malaysia remains deeply tied to the outside world. Its trade is larger than its whole economy, it is a key link in the world's chip supply chain, and its exporters earn dollars. That openness brought decades of growth. It also means a US recession, a tariff war or a Fed shock reaches Malaysian jobs within months. The same holds for most economies outside the few big hubs.


### Why money and talent crowd into a few places

[24 diagram] money-reach: New dollars ripple outward. The closer to the center, the earlier they arrive and the more they buy when they do.

[25 p] Chapter 16 describes the Cantillon effect: whoever receives new money first spends it before prices rise. At world scale, new dollars are created in New York and Washington and land first in US banks, US markets and a handful of hubs such as London, Singapore, Hong Kong, Dubai and Zurich. Commodity exporters and factory economies receive them later, through trade. Households in smaller economies receive them last, often after their currency has already moved.

[26 list]
- Capital goes where returns, rule of law and exits are best.
- Talent follows capital, because that is where the jobs, the strong-currency salaries and the investors are.
- Capital follows talent, because investors want to be near founders, engineers and deal makers.
- The loop feeds itself. Each hub's lead makes the next arrival more likely. That is why successful businesses and entrepreneurs end up in the same few cities out of more than 190 countries.

[27 p] Malaysia feels this loop directly. About 1.8 million Malaysians live abroad, and roughly 60% of them are in Singapore, according to a 2024 New Straits Times report citing World Bank work. The World Bank's chief economist Indermit Gill pointed to the currency gap as one reason educated Malaysians move there.


### Singapore: anomaly, strategy, or someone else's plan?

[29 p] Singapore is an island with no farmland, little water and no natural resources, separated from Malaysia in August 1965. Within two generations it became one of the richest places on earth. Some people decode this as a plan by American money managers to plant a financial outpost in Asia. Here is the record, run through this project's decoding method.

[30 table]
| What happened | Who drove it | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| 1960 to 1961: Dutch economist Albert Winsemius leads a UN mission, reports in 1961 and becomes Singapore's long-time economic adviser | Singapore's government, with UN help | A home-grown plan with outside advice |
| 1968: Singapore abolishes the tax on interest paid to foreign depositors. Bank of America opens the first Asian Currency Unit, starting the Asian Dollar Market | Singapore's government and a US bank, together | Singapore made itself the dollar's hub in the Asian time zone, and US banks were glad to use it |
| 1971: the Monetary Authority of Singapore is set up. From 1981 monetary policy steers the exchange rate instead of interest rates | Singapore | A deliberate choice inside the impossible trinity |
| 1974 and 1981: Temasek and GIC are founded to invest national savings around the world | Singapore | National savings sent abroad by design, unlike most countries |
| 1990: an agreement gives US forces access to Singapore facilities. Changi Naval Base later hosts US aircraft carriers | Singapore and the US | A security umbrella traded for strategic usefulness |
| Location on the Strait of Malacca, one of the busiest sea lanes on earth | Geography | An advantage no plan could create |

[31 callout] (The decode verdict) Planted by American money managers: D3, folklore, about 10% likely as stated. No evidence shows Washington designed Singapore. Strategically welcomed by them: D1, decoded-strong, about 80% likely. Singapore's leaders deliberately made their island useful to the dollar system and to US security in Asia, and the US, its banks and its navy had every reason to support that. It was Singapore's plan, built to fit the American system. Small size let the plan be run tightly, and geography made it pay.

[32 p] That is why Singapore is hard to copy. It needed a strategic location, leaders able to make long-term choices, rule of law trusted by foreign money, English as a working language, and the willingness to plug into the world's dominant system rather than fight it. Most countries have one or two of these. Very few have all of them at once.


### Tying it together

[34 steps]
- A decision in Washington moves dollar yields.
- Global money flows toward or away from smaller markets, and their currencies move.
- Home inflation stays calm, partly because subsidies absorb the shock, while the passport price tag changes.
- Compulsory savings such as the EPF recycle most local money into the home government and home market, in the home currency.
- Money reaches the hubs first. Talent follows money, and money follows talent.
- In a crisis, people with only local assets and local options have the fewest exits.
- A few places, like Singapore, escaped by choosing to become hubs themselves. That took geography, discipline and decades.

[35 myth] Myth: My country's inflation is only 2%, so my savings are safe.
Reality: Safe on the home ruler. Measure them also on the passport ruler: what they buy in dollars, in a foreign degree or in the cost of moving. Both rulers matter, and the second one moves with decisions made abroad.

[36 insider] This is general education, not advice. The lesson people usually draw is to know which ruler their future spending will use. If your life will be lived at home, the home ruler matters most. If your children may study or work abroad, part of your savings is really owed in another currency. Skills that are paid in global terms are the most portable asset a person can own.

[37 check] Q: The Fed raises rates and the ringgit falls 10% against the dollar. Malaysian inflation stays at 2%. Has anything changed for a family saving for a child's degree in the United States?
A: Yes. The degree is priced in dollars, so it now costs about 11% more ringgit. The home price tag stayed calm, but the passport price tag rose.

Rules: Measure your savings with two rulers: what they buy at home and what they buy abroad. / Money reaches the hubs first. Talent follows money, and money follows talent. / No country can fix its currency, open its doors to money and set its own rates all at once.

## L1.16 Eye-openers: what most people never notice (id rb-13)

Kicker: Sixteen quiet facts about money that, once seen, cannot be unseen.

[0 p] Each chapter so far has hidden a few surprises. This chapter gathers them in one place, with a few new ones. Each is something that sits in plain sight in the official numbers, yet most people go their whole lives without noticing it. Read them slowly. For each one, ask yourself where it touches your own money.


### About your own money

[2 insider] 1. Your bank deposit is a loan you made to the bank. The balance in your account is the bank's debt to you. You are an unsecured lender, protected by deposit insurance up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Above that, you are trusting the bank's books. That is why depositors ran from Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023.

[3 insider] 2. Banks create money when they lend. They do not lend out your savings. A new loan creates a new deposit at the same instant, as the Bank of England explained in 2014. In the United States, reserve requirements have been zero since March 2020. Bank lending is limited by capital, demand, profit and the interest rate, not by a fixed multiple of reserves.

[4 insider] 3. Treasury bills are the cash of the rich. In 2026 the average US savings account paid about 0.38%, while a three-month Treasury bill paid about 4.25%. On $50,000 that is about $190 a year against about $2,125. Wealthier savers and companies keep their cash in bills and money market funds. Many ordinary savers leave it in accounts that pay almost nothing, and the bank keeps the difference.

[5 insider] 4. Most money is not cash at all. Paper notes and coins are a small share of the money supply. Most money is bank deposits, which are entries in ledgers. When people say the government prints money, what usually grows is ledger entries at banks and at the Fed.


### About the government's money

[7 insider] 5. Inflation is a silent tax. No law is passed and no vote is taken, yet inflation moves buying power from people holding money and fixed-rate claims to people who owe money, and the largest debtor is the government. In 2026 the average interest rate on US government debt was about 3.2 to 3.5%, while nominal income had grown about 5.7% a year over the decade. That gap quietly shrinks the debt relative to the economy, paid for by bondholders and savers.

[8 quote] "There is no subtler, no surer means of overturning the existing basis of society than to debauch the currency." (John Maynard Keynes, The Economic Consequences of the Peace, 1919)

[9 insider] 6. Seigniorage: issuing money is profitable. A $100 bill costs a few cents to print and buys $100 of goods. More importantly, the Fed buys interest-paying bonds with money it creates, earns the interest, and in normal years sends its profits to the Treasury. Stablecoin issuers have found a private version: they take your dollar, buy Treasury bills and keep the yield.

[10 insider] 7. The Fed can lose money and keep running. From late 2022, the Fed paid more interest on bank reserves than it earned on its bonds. Instead of asking for a bailout, it recorded the losses as a deferred asset, an IOU to itself to be repaid from future profits before it resumes sending money to the Treasury. A central bank that creates its own currency cannot run out of it. It can only lose credibility.

[11 insider] 8. Interest on the national debt now exceeds defense. In fiscal year 2026 the US spent about $1,052 billion on net interest and about $965 billion on national defense. Interest was higher in 2024, 2025 and 2026. Interest took about 19.5% of all federal revenue. About 36% of the government's marketable debt reprices within a year, so if rates stay high, the bill keeps climbing.

[12 insider] 9. US gold is still booked at $42.22 an ounce. The Treasury owns about 261.5 million ounces of gold and records it at the 1973 legal price, about $11 billion in total. At market prices it is worth about $1.08 trillion. Revaluing it would raise about one year of interest payments, once. It is a one-time gain, not a fix for deficits that recur every year.

[13 insider] 10. Workers carry most of the federal tax load. In 2026 about 86% of federal receipts came from individual income and payroll taxes, and only about 7% from corporate taxes, down from about 11% two years earlier as companies expensed huge investments in AI. As machines take a larger share of income, this tax base is under quiet pressure.


### About the world's money

[15 insider] 11. Sanctions run through dollar plumbing. Most international payments pass through dollar accounts at US banks. That gives Washington the power to cut a country, bank or person out of the system. When Western governments froze about $300 billion of Russia's reserves in 2022, every central bank noticed. Central banks then bought gold at a record pace, about 863 tonnes in 2025, because gold held at home cannot be frozen by someone else.

[16 insider] 12. The Cantillon effect. In the 1700s the banker and writer Richard Cantillon noticed that new money does not reach everyone at once. Those who receive it first spend it before prices rise. Those who receive it last face higher prices first. This project's data refine the idea: in 2020 and 2021, checks sent directly to households lifted the bottom half's share of wealth from 1.7% to 2.4%. But owners of assets captured most of the dollar gains. Since late 2019 the top 10% took about 67.5% of the $78 trillion rise in household wealth.

[17 insider] 13. Stablecoin issuers keep the interest. Under the GENIUS Act, holders of compliant US stablecoins cannot be paid interest by the issuer. The issuer invests the reserves in Treasury bills and keeps the yield, often sharing it with distributors. At a 4.25% bill rate, the whole market's float is worth about $11.7 billion a year. Each $1,000 left idle in a stablecoin gives up about $42.50 a year.

[18 insider] 14. Mortgage rates are set more by the bond market than by the Fed. The Fed sets a short-term rate. Mortgage rates follow long-term Treasury yields, which reflect inflation fears, debt supply and foreign demand. In October 2026 the Fed's rate was 3.75 to 4.00%, while the 10-year Treasury yield was about 5.13% and the 30-year about 5.42%. In late 2024 the Fed cut rates and long-term yields rose anyway.

[19 insider] 15. Safe assets are only safe in a given season. Long-term US Treasury bonds have essentially no risk of default, yet they lost close to half their market value from 2020 to 2023 as rates rose. Silicon Valley Bank failed in 2023 largely because it held safe bonds bought in a different season. Safety is a property of an asset in a season, not of the asset alone.

[20 insider] 16. Platforms are the new toll booths. Producing goods has not become cheaper in dollar terms since 2019, but the cut taken by platforms has grown. Advertising rose from about 23.5% to about 41.9% of Amazon's seller services revenue between 2019 and early 2026. Etsy's take rate rose from 16.5% to 24.2%. None of this shows up as a separate line in the consumer price index.

[21 check] Q: Pick the eye-opener that most affects you this month, and say why.
A: Any is fine. For many readers it is number 3: moving idle savings from a near-zero account to a Treasury bill or government money market fund can earn many times more for the same safety. For others it is number 14, because mortgage rates do not simply follow the Fed.

[22 check] Q: Why can the Fed lose money without failing?
A: Because it creates the currency its debts are paid in. It records losses as a deferred asset to be covered from future profits. Its real risk is a loss of credibility, not running out of money.

Rules: Read the official numbers yourself. The biggest secrets in money are published. / Ask who receives new money first. They usually gain most. / Never confuse free of default with free of risk.

## L1.19 The titled world: why abundance runs on credit, and who keeps the balance (id rb-titled)

Kicker: Divide the world's wealth by everyone and it looks like plenty. Here is why most of that plenty is a price set on future credit, why the base layer was titled long ago, and what role each big country plays in keeping a crowded system from tipping over.

[0 p] This is the last chapter before the Rulebook, and it pulls the whole book together around one thought experiment. Take everything the world owns, divide it by everyone alive, and ask: if there is so much, why do so many people feel they are locked out?


### The thought experiment: divide it all

[2 p] UBS estimates the world's personal wealth at about $470 trillion at the end of 2024. Divided by about 8.2 billion people, that is roughly $57,000 for every man, woman and child, or about $230,000 for a family of four. On paper the world is rich. Yet the same report finds that about 42% of adults own less than $10,000, while about 1.5% own more than $1 million.

[3 diagram] divide-wealth: The equal share on paper against what adults actually hold. Brackets from the UBS Global Wealth Report 2025, end of 2024.


### Now divide the base layer instead

[5 p] The $57,000 is a nominal number. It is a price, measured in dollars at today's interest rates, not a quantity of things. To see the real pie, divide the base layer instead.

[6 diagram] base-divide: Each person's equal share of the world, first in paper, then in real things. Cropland from FAO (2023), energy from the Energy Institute (2025).

[7 p] Each person's share of the world's cropland is about 0.2 hectares, roughly a quarter of a football pitch, to grow all of their food. Their share of all the energy humanity used in 2025, about 600 exajoules, is about 20,000 kWh a year, the same as 23 old 100-watt bulbs burning day and night, to run every farm, factory, truck, home and data center on their behalf. Savills' value for all the world's farmland works out to about $5,800 a person. Measured in things, the world is far less abundant than $57,000 suggests. Measured in claims, it looks rich, because claims can multiply faster than farmland or power plants.

[8 insider] This is why the price of the base layer tends to climb in a credit-driven world. Each new round of credit creates more claims chasing the same farmland, the same city plots and the same energy. The claims multiply and the base layer does not, so its price rises. Owners of the base layer get richer without making anything new.


### Why the pie is smaller than it looks

[10 list]
- Most wealth is a price, not a pile. About two thirds of the world's net worth is real estate, and Savills values all the world's property at about $393 trillion at the start of 2025. Those values come from the last sales, as chapter 17 showed with market caps. If everyone tried to sell at once to share out the money, the price would collapse.
- Prices are future income divided by the interest rate. When rates fell from the early 1980s to 2021, the same buildings and companies became worth far more without producing more. McKinsey found that global net worth rose from about $156 trillion in 2000 to about $514 trillion in 2020, while for every $1 of new investment, liabilities grew almost $4, about $2 of it debt. Most of the new wealth was revaluation, not new things.
- Every financial asset is someone else's debt or someone's future income. Global debt reached about $365 trillion in mid-2026, about 310% of world output, according to the Institute of International Finance. A bond, a deposit or a loan is a claim on a borrower's future earnings. Count the claim as wealth and you must also count the debt.
- What can really be shared each year is output, not prices. The world produces roughly $115 to $120 trillion of goods and services a year, about $14,000 per person, and most of that already pays for wages, rents, interest and taxes.

[11 diagram] inverted-pyramid: A wide stack of claims balanced on a narrow base of real things. The higher the layer, the more it depends on future credit.

[12 p] So the abundance we see is partly real, in machines, knowledge, networks and food, and partly a bet on future production, financed by credit and priced at today's interest rates. That is the owner's insight in plain terms: much of the world's apparent abundance is priced on future credit. When rates rise, as they did in 2026, the bet is repriced and the paper abundance shrinks, even though not one building or factory disappears.


### Money is debt, and debt is money

[14 p] There is a deeper reason the paper pie and the real pie differ. Almost all modern money is somebody's debt. Chapter 6 showed that a bank loan creates a deposit. Your bank balance is the bank's debt to you. A dollar bill is a liability of the Federal Reserve, which holds mostly government bonds against it, and those bonds are the government's debt, to be paid from future taxes. Money is debt, and debt is money. If every loan in the world were repaid tomorrow, most of the money would vanish with them.

[15 diagram] money-debt-mirror: Every dollar someone owns is matched by a debt someone else owes. Only real things, gold and Bitcoin sit outside the mirror.

[16 list]
- To grow the money supply, the system must grow debt. That is why total wealth and total debt keep rising together.
- Paying debt down shrinks money. When too many people do it at once, as in 1929 to 1933, money disappears and prices fall.
- Add up the whole world and every financial asset cancels out against someone's debt. What remains, the world's true net worth, is the value of its real things: land, buildings, machines and the rest of the base layer.
- The exceptions are things that are nobody's IOU: gold, Bitcoin, land and goods. That is exactly why people run to them when they start to doubt the debts.

[17 insider] So the $57,000 is not $57,000 of stuff. Much of it is deposits, bonds and pension claims, each matched by a debt somewhere else, owed by a company, a bank or a government that must pay it from future income. Net out the IOUs and what is left is the base layer plus the machines and buildings on top, priced at whatever today's interest rates allow.


### A world already titled

[19 p] Underneath the claims sits the base layer: land, water, minerals, energy, ports, sea lanes, the best city plots, radio spectrum. Almost all of it was claimed long ago, recorded in a title registry or held by a state. Chapter 14 asked how a new player gets onto a board where no one gives out starting money. The harder truth is that the squares are already owned. Newcomers do not arrive on an empty board. They arrive as tenants.

[20 p] When the base layer was up for grabs, it was often fought over with armies. European powers divided Africa among themselves after the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885. Japan pushed into Southeast Asia in 1941 after the United States cut off its oil. Iraq invaded oil-rich Kuwait in 1990. Today the contest is mostly fought with money instead: tariffs, sanctions, export controls on chips and rare earths, and who gets to price oil, finance mines and hold the world's savings. The wars over title turned into wars over price and access, though the older kind has not disappeared.


### The generational seat at the table

[22 p] Within countries, the same pattern runs between generations. In the United States, households headed by baby boomers, born from 1946 to 1964, hold about 52% of household net worth in mid-2026, while millennials and Gen Z together hold about 11%, according to analyses of the Federal Reserve's distributional accounts. Households headed by someone under 40 held about 6.6% in early 2026, down from about 12% in 1989. Older people always own more than younger ones, but the gap has widened, because the assets the older generation bought were revalued upward by falling rates, and the young must buy the same assets at the higher price, usually with credit.

[23 callout] (Picture this) A Monopoly game where the first players bought every street while it was cheap. Late players start with only cash and pay rent on every lap. The bank keeps lending the late players money so the game can go on, because a game in which nobody can pay rent ends for the owners too.

[24 insider] That last line is the key to the whole system. Owners need newcomers. Rents, mortgages, share prices and pension promises only hold if new buyers and tenants keep paying. So the system cannot lock newcomers out completely. It lets them in through wages, credit and a little inflation, on terms that keep the owners' titles valuable. This is the quiet balance the money managers of the world are actually running.


### Four levers that keep the balance

[26 steps]
- Credit: lend newcomers the price of a seat. It keeps asset prices up and lets the young buy in, at the cost of decades of payments to lenders.
- Inflation: quietly shifts value from savers and lenders to borrowers, who are often younger. It shrinks old debts and lifts wages in money terms. Chapter 9 showed why indebted systems choose it.
- Taxes and transfers: pensions, health care, child accounts and income support move part of the owners' income to people without assets. Chapter 14 and the Desk looked at what a universal income can and cannot carry.
- New frontiers: new industries create new things to own before they are titled. Railways, oil, the internet and now AI, compute, energy and space are where newcomers can get title early.

[27 p] When all four levers fail at once, history offers darker resets. The historian Walter Scheidel argued in The Great Leveler (2017) that across recorded history only four forces have reliably shrunk inequality on a large scale: mass-mobilization war, transformative revolution, state collapse and deadly pandemics. The levers above exist, in large part, to keep those four away.

### Sovereign wealth funds: the countries' investment arms

[33 p] Chapter 18 drew every country as a company with an investment arm. That arm is the sovereign wealth fund: a state-owned fund that turns a country's surplus, usually from oil, gas or trade, into ownership of assets around the world. Global SWF counted sovereign funds holding more than $15 trillion at the end of 2025. There are well over a hundred of them, but about a dozen giants hold most of the money, so twelve is a good number to remember.

[37 list]
- Stabilizer: a shock absorber that saves in boom years and spends when oil or export prices crash.
- Saver: turns a resource that will run out into a permanent stake in the world's companies, for generations not yet born.
- Reserve investor: earns more on a country's surplus dollars than Treasury bills would.
- Developer: builds new industries at home so the country is not stuck selling one raw material. Malaysia's Khazanah Nasional plays this role.
- Strategic arm: buys stakes, ports, chip plants, football clubs and game studios abroad, which bring influence and access as well as returns.

[38 insider] Read these funds through this chapter's lens. They convert a base layer that will run out, oil and gas, into titles on the rest of the world: shares, buildings, ports and companies. Norway turned its oil into about 1.5% of every listed company on earth. A small country cannot hold the deep-market crown, but it can buy a slice of the board itself. It is also how much of the petrodollar from chapter 5 comes back to the US and Europe, not only into Treasuries but into their companies.

[39 p] The fund idea also touches the universal income debate. Alaska's Permanent Fund, built from oil revenue and worth about $89 billion, pays every eligible resident a yearly dividend: $1,200 in 2026, made up of $1,000 plus a $200 energy relief payment. It is the closest thing in the United States to a citizen's share of a titled base layer. The United States has historically had no national fund of this kind. A February 2025 executive order asked for a plan for one, and whether it becomes real is a tell worth watching.

[40 check] Q: Why would an oil country rather own a slice of the world's companies than spend all its oil money at home?
A: Because oil runs out and its price swings. Spreading the money across the world's companies turns a temporary resource into permanent, diversified income, and avoids flooding the home economy with cash it cannot absorb, which would cause Dutch disease.


### Decoding the balance: a plan, or incentives?

[42 callout] (The decode verdict) A secret global plan by money managers to keep newcomers out forever: D3, folklore, about 10% as stated. Policy that consistently protects asset prices, because owners vote, lend, fund campaigns and hold the pension savings: D1, decoded-strong, about 80%. After 2008 and again in 2020 central banks moved quickly to support asset prices. Nobody needs to coordinate. Each actor's incentive points the same way: keep the titles valuable, keep the newcomers paying, and keep the four horsemen away.

[43 insider] This is why the future this desk studies matters so much. Robots, AI and cheap energy could enlarge the base layer itself. If newcomers can own a share of that new capacity before it is titled, the gap can close. If the same owners title it first, the gap widens. Who gets title to the new base layer of energy, compute, data and orbit is the ownership question of this generation.


### The whole book in one chain

[45 steps]
- Money is a trusted promise (chapter 1). Its anchor moved from metal to the dollar to government bonds (chapters 2 to 6 and Level 2 chapter 1).
- Banks create most money as loans, so the system needs ever more credit (chapter 6 and Level 2 chapter 2).
- The seasons decide who wins, borrowers or savers, and debt decides which season governments choose (chapters 7 and 8).
- Nations compete for the deep-market crown, and smaller currencies live by its rules (chapters 11 and 12).
- The ruler we measure with keeps shrinking (chapter 13).
- Newcomers start on a board with no free money, and new rails are laid on old anchors (chapters 14 and 15).
- Paper values are claims on the future and can fall fast (chapters 16 and 17).
- Every country runs like a company on that leaderboard, with taxes as its subscription fee and hubs competing for its talent (chapter 18).
- Underneath it all, money is debt, the abundance on paper rests on credit, and the base layer was titled long ago. The system's real job is balancing owners and newcomers without calling the horsemen.

[46 myth] Myth: If the world's wealth were shared out, everyone would be comfortable.
Reality: Only on paper. Most of that wealth is the price of homes and companies at today's interest rates. Sell it all to divide it and the price collapses. What could really be shared is each year's output, about $14,000 per person, and most of it already pays for wages, rents, interest and taxes.

[47 check] Q: Global personal wealth is about $470 trillion. Why can't it simply be handed out as $57,000 to each person?
A: Because most of it is the price of existing assets, valued at the last trade and at today's interest rates, and much of it is matched by debts. Selling it all to share it out would crash the price. What is really available each year is output, about $14,000 per person.

[48 check] Q: Why does a system that favors owners still lend money to newcomers?
A: Because owners need newcomers to keep paying rent, interest and share prices. If newcomers could pay nothing, the owners' titles would lose their value. Credit keeps the game going.
