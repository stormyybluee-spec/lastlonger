## 1. Definitions and the Ponzi test

**What Ponzi actually ran (1919 to 1920).** Charles Ponzi's Securities Exchange Company in Boston promised 50% in 45 days (100% in 90 days), claiming to buy international postal reply coupons cheaply abroad and redeem them in the US. About 30,000 people put in more than $10 million in seven months. By August 1920 the firm owed about $3 million more than its assets (Mass Moments, citing the period record). The Boston Post's July 1920 reporting set off the run. Reports at the time and since say he bought almost no coupons, and that the coupon market could not have carried the volume (one widely repeated estimate: 160 million coupons would have been needed against about 27,000 in circulation [secondary, unverified against a primary]).

**The defining features**, as regulators and courts use them (SEC investor alerts define a Ponzi scheme as paying existing investors with funds from new investors rather than from profits [memory, unverified wording]):
1. No real earning asset, or one far too small for the promised return.
2. Old investors are paid from new investors' money.
3. The truth is hidden. Investors believe a story about the source of returns.
4. It must keep growing. It collapses when inflows slow, because there is nothing underneath.
5. Promised returns are fixed and unusually high.

**Scoring the money and government debt system against each feature**
| Feature | Fit for credit money and government debt | Evidence for the analogy | Evidence against |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. No real asset | Weak fit | Fiat money has no metal backing. Government bonds rest on future taxes, not a vault | Bonds are claims on a tax base, which is a claim on real output. The US produces roughly $30T a year [memory, unverified, R11A has growth data]. Ponzi's firm had almost no earning asset |
| 2. Old paid from new | Partial fit | Governments roll maturing bonds into new bonds. The UK paid interest on perpetual consols for generations and redeemed the 4% Consolidated Loan of 1927 only in February 2015 (218 million pounds), about 88 years later (gov.uk, 2014-10-31). Most advanced-economy debt is refinanced, not paid down | Interest is paid mostly from taxes, not from new lenders. A Ponzi pays all returns from new money. When r is below g, debt can be rolled forever with the debt ratio shrinking. Blanchard (2019 AEA address) finds safe rates on public debt were below growth on average and in most decades in US history |
| 3. Hidden | Weak fit | Inflation's transfer is hard for a saver to see. Keynes (1919): "by a continuing process of inflation, governments can confiscate, secretly and unobserved, an important part of the wealth of their citizens" | Budgets, central bank balance sheets (H.4.1 weekly), the inflation target and CPI are published. The 2% target is announced, not hidden. Ponzi's books were secret |
| 4. Must grow or collapse | Partial fit | Credit money needs new borrowing to keep the money stock growing. Debt-to-GDP keeps rising in many countries. Minsky's "Ponzi finance" is a borrower whose income cannot cover interest | A Ponzi has a fixed end date. Government debt systems survive centuries (UK continuous since 1694 [memory, unverified]). When debt shrinks, the result is recession or deflation (rb-07), not a vanished fund. The test is r against g plus the primary balance, not "inflows" |
| 5. Fixed high return | Fails | None | Bond yields are set in auctions and markets. The 2% target is a low, published number. Holders of long bonds lost close to half their market value in 2020 to 2023 (rb-08 block 302), the opposite of a guaranteed payout |

**Where the analogy bites.** Two places. (a) Rollover: a government that never repays principal and relies on new lenders shares a structural feature with a Ponzi, and becomes fragile in the same way when r rises above g for long enough, which is the Minsky borrower at national scale. (b) Hidden transfer: mild inflation moves value from cash savers and lenders to debtors without a vote on each transfer. Keynes said so himself in 1919.

**Where it fails.** No fixed promised return, a real tax base under the claims, public books, and survival over centuries. The better word is "a confidence system", like any bank or insurer: it works while people trust the issuer to keep money scarce enough. Fraud is a crime of deception. A published 2% target is a policy you can disagree with, not a con.

**Minsky's own Ponzi finance** (rb-07 block 19) is the honest version of the critique: some borrowers, and at times some governments, cannot pay interest from income. That is a risk class, not a description of all money.

**Overcorrection guard.** The theft version is right that mild inflation is a tax nobody votes on item by item, and that asset holders gained most in dollars from 2009 to 2021. It is wrong that the tax is secret, that it is fixed by design to go to the rich (2020 to 2021 sent it to households), and that hard money would remove first-comer gains.

## 3. Who gets new money first and who pays (Cantillon, 2008 to 2023)

**QE 2008 to 2014, UK.** Bank of England (2012, "The distributional effects of asset purchases", Quarterly Bulletin Q3): QE raised household wealth by over 600 billion pounds by May 2012, about 10,000 pounds per person if spread evenly. But the top 5% of households held 40% of financial assets outside pensions, while the median household held about 1,500 pounds of such assets against an average of 175,000 pounds for the top 5%. The Bank argued most people would have been worse off without QE.

**Bank of England staff 2018 (Bunn, Pugh, Yeates, Working Paper 720).** Easing from 2008 to 2014 had a small effect on income and wealth Gini coefficients. In pounds the gains varied widely, in percent they were broadly similar. Households near retirement gained most through wealth, and the young gained most through income (jobs and wages). Working Paper 880 (2020, Bunn, Haldane, Pugh) finds most people better off in well-being terms, the young more than the old.

**ECB (Lenza and Slacalek 2018, WP 2190, published 2024).** QE lowered income inequality in the euro area through the jobs channel, because many lower-income people found work. The wealth Gini effect was negligible: rich households gained from stocks, middle households from house prices.

**US, the distribution afterward (Fed Distributional Financial Accounts).** The top 1% share of US net worth was 31.7% in Q3 2025, a record since the series began in 1989, and 32.5% in Q2 2026 (release of 2026-09-18, per press reports). The bottom 50% held about 2.5% in Q3 2025, down from about 3.5% in 1989 (third-party chart of FRED data, verify series WFRBSB50215). BASE (rb-13 block 16) already has: bottom 50% share rose from 1.7% to 2.4% in 2020 to 2021, and the top 10% took about 67.5% of the $78T wealth rise since late 2019.

**Reading the evidence in both directions.**
- For Cantillon: asset owners gained most in dollars. Shares of wealth at the top rose over the QE years and hit records in 2025 to 2026. Saez links the top 1% share mainly to the stock market (press report, June 2026).
- Against a simple Cantillon story: the central bank studies (BoE, ECB) find small Gini effects in percent, and a jobs channel that helps the bottom. Counterfactual matters: without easing, unemployment would have been higher, and job loss hits the bottom hardest. The studies are by the banks that ran QE, a conflict of interest to flag (scope rule).
- Both can be true: small change in percentage shares, large change in dollar gaps. That is the gross against percent split from the blind-spot list.

**2020 to 2021 direct payments, money that reached households first.** Three rounds of Economic Impact Payments, $600 to $1,400 per adult, totalled about $850 billion (Economic Security Project). Census Supplemental Poverty Measure: the first two rounds lifted 11.7 million people above the poverty line in 2020, including 3.2 million children (CBPP). The third round kept close to 9 million out of poverty in 2021 (EPI). The expanded child tax credit cut child poverty, by 4.0 to 7.1 points depending on how benefits are counted (Columbia CPSP 2025). It expired in December 2021.

**Who paid through inflation, 2021 to 2023.** The burden did not fall in a straight line by income.
- In 2021 middle-income households had the highest inflation, because they spend more on cars and fuel (Minneapolis Fed 2024).
- From mid-2022 rising rents put low-income households highest (Minneapolis Fed 2024). Cleveland Fed (2025) finds the bottom 40% stayed above headline inflation into late 2022 and beyond.
- Over the long run the gap is small: BLS R-CPI-I shows about 0.28 points a year between the lowest and highest income fifths (Monthly Labor Review 2024). Boston College CRR estimates June 2022 inflation at similar levels across groups, middle incomes highest at 9.4%.
- Hidden hypothesis tested: "inflation always hits the poor hardest". Partly true from mid-2022 on rents, false for 2021. Bigger difference is balance sheets: fixed-rate mortgage holders gained (rb-08), renters and cash savers lost.

**Regime label.** 2008 to 2014: QE through asset prices, gains to holders, small Gini change. 2020 to 2021: fiscal checks plus QE, bottom half share rose, top share also rose in dollars. 2022 to 2023: inflation tax arrives, renters and low-income households pay most from mid-2022.

## 4. The new-players question: houses, Bitcoin and hard money with a growing population

**Home ownership at the same age, by generation.**
- US, age 30. Harvard JCHS: about 41% of early millennials owned at 30, against 50.5% of Gen X and 57.8% of early boomers. The gap narrows from 9.5 points at 30 to 4.8 points at 34. Redfin (2022 data): 43% of 30-year-olds against about 52% of boomers and 49% of Gen X at that age. Apartment List 2025 (Census data) gives a lower 33% for millennials against 42% Gen X and 48% boomers, a different method. St. Louis Fed (2023): among 30-year-olds without a degree, 49% of boomers owned against 24% of millennials.
- UK, age 30. Resolution Foundation (2018): about 63% of boomers and about 60% of Gen X owned by 30, against 42% of millennials. Millennials paid about 44,000 pounds more rent than boomers by 30. Data are 2017 to 2018 vintage. Needs a newer pull.
- Malaysia. Khazanah Research Institute uses the median multiple (median house price over median annual household income, 3.0 or below counts as affordable). 2019: RM289,646 over RM70,476 (RM5,873 a month) = 4.1, "seriously unaffordable". Above 3.0 every year since 2002. From 2012 to 2014 the median house price rose from RM170,000 to RM270,000 while incomes grew about half as fast. New launches priced below RM200,000 fell from over 50% of the market in 2009 to under 20% in 2020. Emir Research put 2020 at 4.72 (not KRI). No KRI figure for 2025 or 2026 found. Malaysia by generation at the same age: no source found this round (gap).

**How much is credit and low rates, and how much is building limits.**
- Rates and credit camp. Miles and Monro (Bank of England Staff Working Paper 837, 2019, Economic Policy 2021): UK real house prices rose about 160% from 1985 to 2018, and the fall in the risk-free real rate explains most of it. A persistent 1 point change in real rates moves real prices about 20% over many years. Press summary: "explained by low interest rates alone".
- Building limits camp. Glaeser and Gyourko (NBER w8835, "The Impact of Zoning on Housing Affordability", 2002, and the Manhattan paper with Saks, 2005): in high-cost US areas prices sit well above construction cost, two to three times in Manhattan, and land-use rules are the dominant explanation. In the cheap interior, supply keeps prices down. Adam Smith Institute reply to Miles and Monro: real rates were similar across rich countries, so rates alone cannot explain why the UK rose more. Both camps agree that low rates do more damage where supply cannot respond.
- Reading. "Printing raised house prices" is partly right through the rate channel, since QE and low policy rates pushed down long real rates. It is incomplete without supply: the same rates produced very different price rises in places that could build and places that could not. The young are hurt by the combination, not by money creation alone.

**Bitcoin concentration as a test of "hard money locks out latecomers".**
- Makarov and Schoar (NBER, "Blockchain Analysis of the Bitcoin Market", October 2021): the top 10,000 individual investors held about 5 million of about 19 million coins in circulation, about 27% (often rounded to a third). The top 1,000 held about 3 million. The top 10% of miners held 90% of mining capacity. The authors say concentration is likely understated.
- Lost coins: Chainalysis (June 2020) estimated 2.78 to 3.79 million BTC lost, coins unmoved for five years or more, with Satoshi's coins nearly half of the high figure. Glassnode (November 2022) used a ten-year cutoff and estimated about 2.55 million. Lost coins make the usable supply smaller still, a further gain for existing holders.
- Reading. Supports the claim that a fixed-supply money favours early holders. Lessens it a little: ownership has spread since 2021 through funds and exchanges (not measured here), and a Bitcoin standard is hypothetical. Hard money does not remove first-comer gains, it fixes them in place.

**Hard-money episodes with a growing population.**
- 1873 to 1896 gold standard: falling prices and debtor distress while US population and output grew (BASE rb-02 blocks 19 to 23, R11A has the data).
- The 16th-century price revolution: New World silver entered Europe during a period of population growth and prices rose several-fold over about a century [memory, unverified, R11A to confirm]. Lesson: even metal money inflated when supply jumped, and those who held land gained against wage earners.
- Takeaway for the chapter: under hard money, newcomers gain if output grows faster than population and prices fall gently (good deflation), and lose if they must borrow (rb-defl). The owner's question becomes: are newborns borrowers or savers? Mostly borrowers in their first decades, so hard money tilts against them.

**Japan.** Bank of Japan flow of funds: cash and deposits about 1,126 trillion yen at end of March 2026, about 47% of household financial assets of 2,386 trillion yen (computed from press reports of the BoJ release). Share was 50.9% at end 2024 and is falling as equities and investment trusts grow. Japan had decades of deposit-heavy saving with low growth and mild deflation (rb-08). But causation runs both ways: weak demand and deflation made cash rational to hold, and the cash hoard then gave the government a captive buyer for its bonds.

**China.** People's Bank of China urban household survey (October 2019, over 30,000 households): real assets nearly 70% of household assets, housing about 59.1% (IMF working paper 2022/196 citing the PBOC), and 96% of urban households own a home. The China Household Finance Survey finds over 70% in tier 3 cities. The property slump since 2021 shows the cost: when most savings sit in one illiquid asset, a fall in its price cuts spending.

**Does "non-circulating wealth stalls progress" hold?**
- Partly right. Keynes's paradox of thrift and the "liquidity trap" make the same point: if everyone tries to hold money at once, spending and incomes fall. Japan and China show that a nation of savers can end up with weak home demand.
- Partly wrong. A house is not money sitting still. The seller received the money and spent or lent it. A deposit is lent on by the bank or backs a bond that funds spending somewhere (BASE rb-titled block 14). What stalls is the flow of new spending when everyone wants to save at once, not the existence of stored wealth. Saved wealth that funds investment is how progress is paid for.
- Regime label: a problem in slumps and at zero rates, mostly not in normal years.

**Attempts to force circulation.**
- Silvio Gesell, The Natural Economic Order (1916) proposed money that loses value each month unless stamped [memory, unverified date]. Wörgl, Austria, 1932 to 1933: the town issued stamped "labour certificates". Of 32,000 schillings printed, about 8,000 circulated. Advocates report the notes changed hands far more often than schillings and that local unemployment fell, while the Mises Institute disputes the gains. The Austrian National Bank ended it in September 1933 because it held the sole right to issue money, and unemployment returned. Sources differ on the monthly fee (1% is most cited) and the end date (September or November 1933).
- Negative policy rates, a modern stamped money on bank reserves: the ECB deposit rate went below zero in 2014 and reached minus 0.5% in 2019, the Bank of Japan used minus 0.1% from 2016 until March 2024 [memory, unverified, check against ECB and BoJ releases]. Banks mostly did not pass negative rates to household deposits, so the push to spend was weak on ordinary savers.
- Mild inflation is the softest version of the same idea: a small, steady fee on holding cash (rb-08 block 280 already uses the owner's "leaking bucket").
