# Round 11 frame: "Printing money is a scam, theft or a Ponzi scheme"

Cold framer (Move 1) with pre-mortem. Written 2026-11-15, blind to REPORT.md, earlier rounds, site pages and the ledger.
Stack: Fundamentals (PLAN 1.1). Product: the first Level 3 Rulebook chapter. Tier: Standard. Unseen mode: off (this is a mechanism and belief audit, not a secret-deal question). The "who decides" dimension touches power, but it is answered from laws and documents, not inference.

Ground rule for every lane: each premise below, including the owner's lean, is a hypothesis to falsify. The overcorrection guard runs both ways. The chapter must not land on "printing is a scam" and must not land on "printing is good". The expected shape of a fair verdict is a grid: which kind of printing, in which regime, judged against which definition, costs whom and helps whom.

---

## 1. Question types and hidden hypotheses

### 1.0 The theme
Restated: "Is money creation a scam, theft or a Ponzi scheme, or is it a structure that lets a growing world build wealth? Weigh it through the household, the company and the government."
Type: belief audit (popular claim) wrapped around a mechanism question (how does new money reach people, and what does it do to them).

Hidden hypotheses in the theme:
| # | Hypothesis | Why it is not yet a fact |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | "Everyone is saying printing is a Ponzi scam" | Reach is a claim. The Sweep lane must size it (views, books, polls), and check who says it and what they sell |
| T2 | "The government imposes it" | Most new money in normal years is made by commercial banks lending (PLAN 2B: Fed share 4% to 13% per decade, 1971 to 2007). The government plus the central bank dominated only in 2008 to 2014 and 2020 to 2021. "Imposed" must be tested per channel and per regime |
| T3 | "Printing provides wealth for everything living on the earth" | Money is a claim on goods, not the goods. Creating it can mobilise idle workers and machines when there is slack, and can destroy wealth when there is none (hyperinflation). Credit booms also predict crises. This is the owner's strongest lean and the one most likely to be flattered |
| T4 | "Nobody has wandered through why it may not be a scam" | Central banks, Keynesian and post-Keynesian economists, MMT writers and historians of the gold standard have written this case at length. The chapter should cite them, not claim novelty |
| T5 | "Nothing is perfect, so weighing options is the only choice" | Fair, but it can hide a choice already made. The chapter must show the alternatives concretely (gold, currency board, fixed peg, rule-based fiat, Bitcoin-style fixed supply), each with its record |

### 1.1 Question 1: post-war rebuild
Restated: "If gold had been money after WW2 and the US held 75% of the world's gold, could war-torn countries have rebuilt? Was credit money necessary for the rebuild?"
Type: what-if (counterfactual) resting on a history fact check and a mechanism question (what financed reconstruction).

| # | Hidden hypothesis | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Q1a | The US held 75% of the world's gold | Share of official monetary gold, 1945 to 1950. Denominator matters: official gold outside the Soviet bloc, all official gold, or all above-ground gold including private holdings. Desk memory (to verify): about 20,000 tonnes in 1949, roughly 60% to 75% of official gold outside the Soviet bloc |
| Q1b | "By the Second World War gold is money" | The world was not on a gold-coin standard in 1945. Most countries had left gold in 1931 to 1936. Bretton Woods (1944) tied only the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce, for official holders. Domestic money was already credit money |
| Q1c | With gold as money, war-torn countries would have had "no money" | A country can issue its own domestic currency without gold. West Germany's 1948 currency reform created the Deutsche Mark with no gold behind it. The binding limit was foreign exchange to buy imports, not domestic money |
| Q1d | The rebuild took about 50 years, or would have | Western Europe passed pre-war output by about 1950 to 1951 and Japan by about 1953 to 1955 (memory, verify with Maddison). The question's time scale may be off by a factor of ten |
| Q1e | Trading resources could not have done it | Exports, the European Payments Union clearing system, Marshall aid, debt relief (London Debt Agreement 1953) and Korean War procurement for Japan all mattered. Which was largest must be sized |
| Q1f | Most wars start from resource scarcity or an empire wanting money | A contested causal claim from international relations. The chapter should not lean on it. At most, label it as one school |
| Q1g | The rebuild disproves a gold anchor | Anomaly for the owner's lean: the fastest growth in modern European history (1950 to 1971) happened under a gold-anchored system with capital controls. That is a hybrid: gold at the top, elastic national credit money below, discipline from the peg. Each camp can claim part of it |

### 1.2 Question 2: new players on an old board
Restated: "With a growing population, is it fair that inflation trims the advantage of those who already hold wealth, so newborns are not locked out? Would a deflationary hard money (Bitcoin in 100 to 200 years) lock out the young? Were the young priced out of houses because of printing or despite it? Does wealth sitting in houses stop money circulating?"
Type: belief audit plus mechanism question (distribution of inflation and of new money), with a forecast embedded (a future Bitcoin standard).

| # | Hidden hypothesis | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Q2a | Inflation strips the wealthy of advantage | Inflation taxes holders of nominal claims (cash, deposits, bonds, fixed pensions) and favours debtors and owners of real assets. The rich hold mostly real assets and often use leverage. Doepke and Schneider (2006) found surprise inflation moves wealth from rich old bondholders to young middle-class mortgage debtors in the US. So the owner is partly right for the young with a mortgage and wrong for the young renter with savings in cash |
| Q2b | The Monopoly analogy fits | In Monopoly the bank pays every player $200 when they pass Go, an equal transfer. Real new money mostly enters through loans to people with income and collateral, and through asset purchases from bond holders. The analogy describes a citizen dividend, not today's system. 2020 stimulus cheques are the nearest real case |
| Q2c | Hard money locks out the newborn | Under a stable or falling price level, productivity gains reach wage earners through lower prices. Compounding still works for savers. Lock-out comes from who owns the assets, which can happen under any money. Test: wealth concentration under the classical gold standard (Gilded Age) against Bretton Woods (compression) against post-1980 fiat (rise again). If inequality moves without the money regime moving, money is not the main driver |
| Q2d | "If it is worth more tomorrow, why spend today" stops an economy | Partly true in a debt deflation (Fisher 1933, US 1929 to 1933). Weak as a general rule: prices of electronics fall every year and people still buy them. Test 1873 to 1896, a period of falling prices and positive real growth |
| Q2e | Boomers' house prices priced out the young, because of printing | Rival causes: falling interest rates 1981 to 2021, land and zoning limits, household formation, incomes, tax rules, and credit directed to mortgages. Printing acts through low rates and mortgage credit, so the claim is partly a channel claim. Test house price to income ratios and home ownership by generation at the same age, in the US and Malaysia |
| Q2f | Decentralised money has a forgotten bad past, and hacks take units from citizens | Both sides have a record: US free banking 1837 to 1863 (wildcat notes), Scottish free banking 1716 to 1844 (relatively stable), crypto exchange hacks (Mt Gox 2014), and centralised seizures (US gold order 1933, Cyprus deposit bail-in 2013). The chapter must show both |
| Q2g | Money always has a scoreboard, so it flows upward, and empires used force or creative governance to recirculate it | The historical tool for this was usually debt cancellation, land reform or taxation (Mesopotamian clean slates, Solon in 594 BC, jubilees), not printing. That is a different instrument for the same goal. Distinguish them |
| Q2h | Money "stored in houses" does not circulate and stalls a civilization | When a house is bought, the money goes to the seller and keeps moving. What is stuck is credit directed at existing land rather than new production. Test the share of bank lending that is mortgages over time (Jorda, Schularick and Taylor "The Great Mortgaging" 2016) and whether that share links to slower productivity |
| Q2i | Credit money as digital accounting is "now the better method" | A ranking claim. Needs criteria first: price stability, growth, crisis frequency, fairness across ages, resistance to seizure and hacks, accountability. Then scores per regime |
| Q2j | "The feeling that printing is theft sounds just" | Keep this. A fair chapter has to say where the feeling is right |

### 1.3 Question 3: the sword
Restated: "A collector pays 100k for a custom sword. The money becomes income for the smith and suppliers. The sword is not a store of value: stolen and pawned it fetches far less. Is this a fair way to explain why money needs mild inflation?"
Type: belief audit of an analogy (does the mechanism in the story match the mechanism it is meant to explain).

| # | Hidden hypothesis | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Q3a | The sword story explains mild inflation | The gap between 100k paid and the pawn price is about liquidity and how specific the good is (the bid to ask gap of a one-off item), not about the price level. It explains why money is more liquid than goods, and that one person's spending is another's income. Neither point requires inflation |
| Q3b | Money "becomes" the sword | The money did not vanish. The smith holds it. What changed is that the buyer swapped a liquid claim for an illiquid good. If the 100k was a new loan, new deposits were created and the smith now holds them. That is the place to teach loans create deposits |
| Q3c | Mild inflation is needed | The documented reasons central banks give for about 2% rather than 0%: room to cut rates above zero, wages that rarely fall in money terms, and price indexes that overstate inflation by an estimated 0.5 to 1 point. These must be stated and tested, and the costs (inflation tax on cash, bracket creep, the arbitrary choice of 2%) set beside them |
| Q3d | A better analogy exists | Candidates for the lanes to test with cold readers: a game where the bank must issue chips as more players join, or a market where prices of a fixed stock of coins must fall as goods multiply |

## 2. The strongest form of both sides

### 2.1 The scam case, steel-manned
| Strand | The strongest claim | Evidence a fair chapter must show |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation tax | Inflation is a tax no parliament voted for (Friedman's "taxation without legislation"). It falls on cash, deposits paying below inflation, fixed pensions and wages that lag | Dollar size of the inflation tax on US currency and on low-interest deposits by year. First pass in section 6. Who holds cash by income and age (Fed SCF) |
| Who gets new money first (Cantillon effect) | Banks, bond holders, borrowers with collateral and asset owners get new money before prices rise. Wage earners get it last | Fed Distributional Financial Accounts: asset shares by wealth group before and after 2008 and 2020. Stock ownership concentration (top 10% share of equities). Channel table by regime |
| Asset inflation | QE and low rates lifted stock, bond and house prices more than wages, widening the gap between owners and renters | Price to income ratios, equity valuations, house prices against wages 2008 to 2021, Malaysia included |
| Debasement history | Every fiat or debased money has lost most of its value over long runs | Roman denarius silver content falling over the third century, US CPI since 1913 and since 1971, Malaysia ringgit against the dollar since 1973 and against its own CPI |
| Hyperinflations | Printing to pay the state has ended in ruin many times | Hanke and Krus list of hyperinflations (about 56 episodes, memory, verify): Germany 1923, Hungary 1946, Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela 2016 to 2019. With the causes each shows (war, lost output, foreign debt, collapsed tax base) |
| Debt that must roll | Government debt is never repaid in total, only rolled. If interest runs above growth, it compounds faster than the economy and must be inflated away or defaulted on. This is the formal economic meaning of "Ponzi" (the no-Ponzi condition) | Effective interest rate on US federal debt against nominal GDP growth, 1946 to 2026. Interest cost as a share of revenue. Malaysia's federal debt and debt service charges |
| No exit and no consent | A citizen cannot opt out of the national currency for taxes, contracts and wages. Rules on who issues money are set by a few people | Legal tender laws, central bank independence laws (Federal Reserve Act, Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009), who appoints the decision makers |

### 2.2 The structure case, steel-manned
| Strand | The strongest claim | Evidence a fair chapter must show |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic money for a growing world | Output and population grow. A fixed money stock means prices must fall, and falling prices raise the real weight of every debt | World population and real GDP since 1820 (Maddison). Gold stock growth against output growth under the classical gold standard |
| Debt deflation under hard money | Gold-standard rules forced money to shrink in crises, turning recessions into depressions | US 1929 to 1933: prices down about 25%, money stock down about a third (Friedman and Schwartz). Eichengreen and Sachs (1985): countries that left gold earlier recovered sooner |
| Lender of last resort | A central bank that can create money stops bank runs from spreading (Bagehot 1873) | Frequency of banking panics in the US National Banking era (1863 to 1913, no central bank) against after 1934 deposit insurance. Banking crisis dates by regime (Reinhart and Rogoff, Jorda, Schularick and Taylor) |
| Post-war reconstruction on credit and aid | National credit money, aid and debt relief rebuilt Europe and Japan within a decade | Marshall Plan size by country against GDP, the 1948 Deutsche Mark reform, the European Payments Union, the 1953 London Debt Agreement, Japan's Dodge Line and Korean War procurement |
| Credit funds new firms | Banks create purchasing power for entrepreneurs before they have savings (Schumpeter) | Business lending share of bank credit, small firm credit, Malaysia's development finance (SME lending) |
| Mild inflation lubricates | A low, steady rate makes real wage adjustment easier and keeps rates above zero for crises | Incidence of nominal wage cuts, frequency of policy rates at zero since 2008, measurement bias estimates |
| Transparency today | Unlike coin clipping, modern targets are published and measured monthly. The rate is announced in advance | Inflation targets and actual outcomes by year, the 2021 to 2022 overshoot included |

### 2.3 Where both sides are wrong in their simple form
- The scam side treats all money creation as state printing. Most is bank lending in normal years.
- The structure side treats inflation as harmless at 2%. Over 35 years, 2% removes half the purchasing power of cash, and the overshoot years (2021 to 2023) were not announced in advance.
- Both sides treat the gold standard as one regime. The classical gold standard (to 1914), the interwar gold exchange standard (1919 to 1939) and Bretton Woods (1944 to 1971) behaved very differently.
- Commodity money also inflated: the Spanish silver inflow (the price revolution of the 1500s) and gold discoveries after 1848 and 1896 raised prices.

## 3. Answer spec: what a complete expert answer must contain

### 3.1 Definitions fixed before any judgment
| Word | Definition the chapter will judge against | Variants to keep separate |
|---|---|---|
| Printing | Any creation of new money. Kept in three kinds: (a) central bank creating reserves or cash (QE, lending to banks), (b) commercial banks creating deposits by lending, (c) monetary financing, the central bank paying for government deficits directly or in effect | Physical cash printing is a small part. Each kind gets its own verdict |
| Scam | A scheme that gains by deception: the victim would refuse if they knew the truth | Is the 2% target hidden? Is an overshoot deceptive? Is the public told who gains? |
| Theft | Taking property without consent or legal right | Legal taking with no individual consent (like a tax) is not theft in law. Say whether the chapter means law, ethics or the reader's feeling |
| Ponzi | A scheme that pays earlier investors from new investors' money, has no real return underneath, needs ever-growing inflows and collapses when they stop | The formal economics version: debt is "Ponzi" if it can only be serviced by new borrowing forever, which is sustainable only while the interest rate stays below the growth rate. Give the verdict under both |

### 3.2 Required contents
1. The four definitions above, set out before any verdict, with a verdict grid: kind of printing (a, b, c) by word (scam, theft, Ponzi) by regime.
2. The channel table (PLAN Move 5): every way money is created and destroyed, signed and sized for a normal year and a stress year, with who receives new money first in each channel.
3. The regime split, each with price level change, growth, banking crises, inequality direction, and who gained and lost:

| Regime | What to record |
|---|---|
| Classical gold standard to 1914 | Long-run price stability, deflation 1873 to 1896, panics in the US, growth rates |
| 1919 to 1939 | Hyperinflations (Germany, Austria, Hungary), the gold exchange standard, the Great Depression and the order of leaving gold |
| Bretton Woods 1944 to 1971 | Dollar pegged to gold, capital controls, rebuild, high growth, US gold share falling, the Nixon close of the gold window |
| 1971 to 2007 | High inflation in the 1970s, the Volcker shock, the long decline in rates, the 1997 Asian crisis and Malaysia's 1998 peg and capital controls |
| 2008 to 2019 | QE, zero rates, asset price rise, low consumer inflation |
| 2020 to 2026 | Pandemic money creation, 2021 to 2023 inflation, rate hikes, QT, the late 2026 state (rates and debt service from data, not memory) |

4. The three lenses, each with gains and losses:

| Lens | Gains from credit money | Losses from credit money | Malaysia angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household | Mortgage access, lower real debt burden when inflation surprises, employment kept up in recessions, deposit safety through a lender of last resort | Inflation tax on cash and savings, house prices against wages, late receipt of new money, renters against owners | EPF (KWSP) savers as holders of nominal claims, ringgit purchasing power, house price to income ratio |
| Company | Credit to start and grow before profits, stable demand in downturns, debt eroded by inflation | Misallocated credit to property and zombie firms, input cost volatility, uncertainty in high inflation | Exporters gain from a weaker ringgit, importers lose, SMEs depend on bank credit |
| Government | Seigniorage, flexibility in war and crisis, debt eroded by inflation, rescue of banks | Loss of trust, higher rates demanded by lenders, debt that must roll, temptation to finance deficits | Bank Negara's policy follows the Fed, reserves held in dollars, limited room to print without currency fall |

5. The alternatives scored on the same criteria (price stability, growth, crisis frequency, fairness across ages, seizure and hack risk, accountability): classical gold, Bretton Woods hybrid, currency board (Malaya until 1967), fixed peg (Malaysia 1998 to 2005), rule-based fiat with a target, fixed-supply crypto.
6. The owner's three questions answered directly, each with what the premise gets right and wrong.
7. The sword analogy judged, with a better analogy offered if one tests better with cold readers.
8. Where the scam feeling is right, stated in plain words, with numbers.
9. What is still unknown and what would change the verdict (for example, if interest on US debt stays above growth for years).

## 4. Blind-spot list (dimensions the answer flips on)
| Dimension | How the verdict can flip |
|---|---|
| Creditors against debtors | Inflation is a transfer from one to the other. "Theft" from a saver is relief to a borrower |
| Old against young | Old households hold more bonds and cash, young households hold more mortgage debt. But the young renter loses on rents and house prices |
| Asset owners against wage earners | Asset inflation favours owners even when consumer inflation is low (2010s) |
| Reserve-currency country against others such as Malaysia | The US earns seigniorage worldwide and exports its policy. Malaysia imports Fed decisions, holds dollar reserves and cannot print freely without the ringgit falling. "Printing" means different things in Washington and in Kuala Lumpur |
| Mild against high inflation | 2% and 200% are different animals. The verdict must state its threshold |
| Expected against surprise inflation | Expected inflation is priced into wages and rates. Surprise inflation does the transferring |
| Who decides | Elected treasury, independent central bank, private banks, a protocol. Accountability differs |
| Normal against crisis years | Bank lending dominates normal years, the central bank dominates crises (PLAN 2B) |
| Nominal against real | Prices up 8 times since 1971 tells nothing without wages and output |
| Stock against flow | The inflation tax is a flow on a stock of cash. The debt problem is a stock that rolls |
| Gross against net | A household with savings and a mortgage may be a net winner |
| Domestic against foreign holders | About half of US currency may be held abroad (memory, verify), so part of the US inflation tax is paid by foreigners |
| The law against practice | Bretton Woods convertibility existed on paper. In practice the US discouraged conversion in the 1960s |

## 7. Pre-mortem: the three likeliest ways the headline will be wrong
| # | How it goes wrong | Check that catches it | Stage that runs it |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | The desk flatters the owner's lean. The headline becomes "printing is not a scam, it is a structure that builds wealth", and the scam case appears only as a weak foil. Signs: the inflation tax and Cantillon numbers missing or buried, hyperinflations shrunk to a footnote, "Ponzi" dismissed as rhetoric without testing the formal interest-against-growth version | Symmetry test: the hard-money voice and the MMT voice each confirm in writing that their strongest case is stated with numbers. Completeness auditor counts evidence rows for each side in section 2 and blocks if either side is missing a strand. Word audit for "myth" and "wrong" | Red team RT3 (hard-money critic and the literal reader), then the Judge as completeness auditor |
| P2 | One definition picked from several. "Printing" collapses into central bank QE (or into bank lending) and "Ponzi" into its loose meaning, so the verdict is true for one kind and false for another. This is the Round 6 habit | The verdict grid of section 3.1 (kind of printing by word by regime) must be filled with no blank cells before any prose verdict. The headline must name which kind it covers | Ledger lane builds the grid, completeness auditor checks it |
| P3 | A true headline at one level hides a loser at another. "Mild inflation is broadly fine" is true in aggregate and hides cash holders, renters, wage earners whose pay lags, and non-reserve countries like Malaysia. Or "credit money rebuilt Europe" is true and hides that the rebuild ran under a gold-anchored peg with capital controls. This is the Case 2 and Round 8 habit | The three-lens table must show gains and losses in every cell, with a Malaysia column. The regime table must be filled from data. The historian voice checks the rebuild story | Data lane and Ledger lane, then red team RT2 (economic historian and accountant) |
