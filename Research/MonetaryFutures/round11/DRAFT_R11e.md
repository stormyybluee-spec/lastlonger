## L3.1 Is printing money a scam? The charge, the defence and the toll (id rb-print)

Kicker: You have heard it a hundred times: printing is theft, the dollar has lost 97%, the whole thing is a Ponzi. This chapter puts that charge on trial at full strength, then puts hard money on trial too, and ends with the question that matters more than either: who pays the toll, and who decides.

[0 tldr]
- The charge is partly true. Cash does melt: a dollar buys what 3 cents bought in 1913, and a ringgit buys what 18 sen bought in 1971. From 2021 to 2025 people holding US cash lost an estimated $488 billion of buying power (our estimate), and no one voted on it.
- It is not a scam in the plain sense today, since the target and the price index are published. It is not a Ponzi by its features, though by the strict test the US borrows to pay its interest. The two numbers to watch are the interest rate against growth, and the deficit before interest. The honest name is a toll, paid by cash holders, renters and late savers.
- Hard money has a toll too. It falls on borrowers and the young: from 1929 to 1933, as US prices fell by a quarter, debts grew a third heavier. A fixed money does not remove the first-comer advantage. It hands early holders the whole rise in value.
- After 1945 America held 60% to 70% of reported official gold, less than the 75% some people claim. Europe and Japan passed pre-war output by 1949 and 1951, in about five years, not the fifty some people expect. Work and home saving paid for most of it, not gold or new money.
- Both systems tilt the board, toward different people. The useful questions are who pays, how much, who gets new money first, and what number would show failure.
- This is a debate between serious people, and nothing here is advice.

### Part 1 of 4. The charge sheet

[5 p] Picture a courtroom. In the dock sits the money in your pocket. The charge is read out. This money is printed from nothing. It loses value every year. The people who print it get rich first. And the whole system only survives by borrowing more. Guilty of theft, says the crowd. A scam. A Ponzi.

[6 p] A fair trial starts by making the charge as strong as it can be. So first, three meanings of printing, because the crowd mixes them. A central bank creates money when it buys bonds. Ordinary banks create it when they lend. And a government creates it when it spends more than it taxes and the central bank or banks buy the debt.

[7 table]
| The charge | The evidence | How big |
|---|---|---|
| Cash melts | Prices rise almost every year, so idle money buys less | A dollar keeps 2.9 cents of its 1913 buying power, and 12 cents of its 1971 buying power. A ringgit keeps 17.6 sen of its 1971 buying power |
| It is a tax nobody voted on | Inflation takes buying power from anyone holding cash. Nobody voted on the 2% | An estimated $488 billion lost by US cash holders, 2021 to 2025 (our estimate). About half of dollar notes by value are held abroad |
| The first to get new money win | When central banks bought bonds, asset prices rose, and those assets are owned very unevenly | Bank of England: after its bond buying, UK household wealth was up just over 600 billion pounds by May 2012, and the top 5% of households held about 40% of financial assets outside pensions |
| Savers were pushed under water | Interest on safe savings was held below inflation for years | US Treasury bills lost about 1.7% a year after inflation from 2009 to 2021 |
| You cannot opt out | Taxes and wages are set in the national money | No number needed |
| It only works by borrowing more | Governments repay old bonds by selling new ones. The US has run a deficit before interest every year since 2002 | The average interest rate on US marketable government debt rose from 1.43% in December 2021 to 3.57% in October 2026 |

[8 insider] (Even Keynes said so) The strongest witness for the prosecution is the economist most people think defended printing. John Maynard Keynes wrote in 1919 that by a continuing process of inflation, governments can confiscate, secretly and unobserved, an important part of the wealth of their citizens. He was describing the wild inflations after the First World War. But the mechanism he named is the same one, only slower. Its far tail is hyperinflation, prices rising 50% or more a month, found 56 times by 2012 and 58 by 2019 in one count.

[9 check] Q: Inflation runs at 3% for a year. Who loses, and does the government collect what they lose?
A: Anyone holding cash or deposits that pay less than 3% loses buying power. Partly, yes. A banknote is a debt of the central bank that pays no interest, while the bonds it holds do pay interest. In normal years that profit, called seigniorage, goes to the government, though the US central bank has sent none since late 2022 because it now pays banks more interest than it earns. Borrowers gain too, because their debts shrink in real terms, and the government is the biggest borrower. Banks that pay savers less than they earn keep part of the gap.


### Part 2 of 4. The cross-examination

[11 p] Now test the charge, one word at a time. A scam wins by lying. Theft takes without consent or legal right. A Ponzi scheme has a precise meaning, and it comes from one man.


### Is it a Ponzi?

[13 p] In 1920 Charles Ponzi of Boston promised 50% in 45 days. About 30,000 people handed him more than $10 million in seven months. He had almost no real business. He paid early investors with money from later ones, and when a newspaper asked questions the inflow stopped and it all collapsed. Line the money system up against his scheme.

[14 table]
| What makes a Ponzi | Ponzi's scheme | Money and government debt |
|---|---|---|
| No real asset underneath | Yes. Almost nothing | No. Bonds are claims on future taxes |
| Old investors paid from new ones | Yes. All of it | Partly. Old bonds are repaid with new ones, and since 2002 even the interest is in effect borrowed |
| The truth is hidden | Yes. The books were secret | No. The inflation target, the central bank's holdings and the budget are published |
| It must grow or collapse | Yes. It lasted seven months | Partly. The money supply needs new lending to grow, but Britain repaid a 1927 war loan only in 2015, so it rolled that debt over for 88 years |
| A fixed, high promised return | Yes. 50% in 45 days | Partly. The return is modest and set by buyers, who can refuse. Long bonds lost heavily after 2020 |

[15 p] So by its features it is not a Ponzi. That is the first of two answers. Economists also have a strict version, and here the prosecution scores a point. A borrower runs a Ponzi game in that sense when it pays all its interest with new borrowing. The US does: it has spent more than it taxed, even before interest, every year since 2002 (IMF). Doing this is not the same as collapsing. Whether that can go on depends on two numbers: the interest rate against how fast the economy grows in dollars, price rises included, and the deficit before interest, which is spending minus taxes with the interest bill left out.

[16 p] Think of a household with a loan. Test one asks: does your pay grow faster than the interest rate on the loan? Test two asks: before the loan costs, do you spend more than you earn? Pass both and the loan stays small against your pay. Fail, and the loan outgrows your pay until something stops it. For a country that something is higher taxes, spending cuts or inflation.

[17 table]
| The strict test | What it asks | The US today | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test one: the rate against growth | Is the interest rate on the debt below the growth of the economy? | Rate 3.57% against growth of 5.9% in October 2026, lifted by inflation | Passes |
| Test two: the deficit before interest | Is the gap between spending and taxes small enough? | About 3.6% of income in 2024. With debt a little over one year of income, the most that keeps debt steady is about 2% to 2.6% (our arithmetic) | Fails |

[18 p] Test one does not always pass. The rate was above growth, which is the bad case, in only 5 or 6 of the last 25 years, counting from 2001. Over the longer stretch from 1962 it was the bad case in 24 of 64 years. So by the strict test today's answer is a warning, not a collapse. Watch both numbers.


### Did the dollar really lose 97%?

[20 p] Yes, if you kept it under a mattress since 1913. That is a claim about cash that earns nothing. A saver who kept money in short US government bills since 1971 saw one dollar grow to almost eleven, while prices rose about eight times. That saver came out ahead by about half a percent a year before tax, which tax could erase. But not in every decade: bills lost to inflation through the 1970s and again from 2009 to 2021. So the charge lands hardest on people who hold plain cash and accounts that pay nothing. About half of dollar notes by value are held abroad.


### Who gets the new money first?

[22 table]
| How the money is created | Who touches it first | Who waits or pays |
|---|---|---|
| A bank lends | The borrower, then whoever sold them the house or goods. About 41% of US bank lending is against property | Later buyers, who meet higher prices |
| The central bank buys bonds | The seller of the bond, then owners of shares and houses as prices rise. Since 2022 the central bank has been shrinking its holdings, so this has run in reverse | Savers, partly, as interest rates fall, and renters |
| The government sends cheques | Households. The US sent about $850 billion in 2020 and 2021. The first two rounds, about $400 billion, lifted 11.7 million people above the poverty line in 2020 (Census) | Holders of dollars, through part of the 2021 to 2023 inflation, which had several causes |

[23 p] The crowd says the rich always get it first. In dollars that fits bond buying and much bank lending, though central bank studies found only small changes in inequality in percentage terms. It does not fit the cheques of 2020 and 2021, which reached ordinary households first. Then part of the bill arrived as inflation. In 2021 it hit middle earners hardest, through cars and fuel. From mid 2022 it hit the poorest hardest, through rent.

[24 insider] (The verdict so far) Scam? Not in the plain sense today. A con depends on a lie, and the target, the purchases and the price index are published, the US 2% target since 2012. Theft? Not in law, since Congress set the goal of stable prices, but in effect a transfer nobody voted on. Ponzi? Two answers. By its features, no: nobody is lied to and the debt is backed by taxes. By the strict test, a warning: the US borrows to pay its interest, and its deficit before interest is now too large. The honest word is a toll on a road the law makes you use, paid by some far more than others.

[25 table]
| Kind of printing | Scam? | Theft? | Ponzi? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Banks lend | No. Borrower and bank both agree to the deal | No. Each loan is matched by a promise to repay | No, though chains of borrowers can become one, as in 2006 to 2008 |
| Central bank buys bonds | Not in law. Partly in feeling, since few were told asset owners gain most | Not in law. In effect a transfer to asset owners | No promised return |
| Government spends new money | It can be, when the state hides it | Closest to Keynes's charge when inflation runs high | Closest fit when the rate beats growth |

[26 check] Q: A friend says the government is running a Ponzi because it never repays its debt. What two numbers would test that?
A: The interest rate on the debt against the growth of the economy, and the deficit before interest. While growth is higher and the deficit before interest is small, the debt can be rolled over without growing against income. If the rate stays above growth for years outside a recession, or the deficit before interest stays large, the friend has a point. One small correction to the friend: each US bond is repaid in full when it falls due. It is the total that keeps rolling.


### Part 3 of 4. The alternative goes on trial

[28 p] A fair court does not stop at the charge. If printing is the crime, what is the alternative? Usually the answer is hard money, which no government or bank can create at will. Think of gold, whose stock grows only as fast as it is mined, or a coin with a fixed supply. The table keeps the eras apart. Then hard money goes in the dock, starting with a question readers often ask.

[29 table]
| Era | The money rule | US prices | Who lost most |
|---|---|---|---|
| To 1914 | Gold (US paper dollar until 1879) | Fell about 2% a year, 1866 to 1897 | Debtors. US panics in 1873, 1893, 1907 |
| 1919 to 1939 | Gold restored, then dropped | Fell a quarter, 1929 to 1933 | Savers in hyperinflations, then debtors and workers |
| 1944 to 1971 | Dollar tied to gold for central banks | 1.8% a year in the 1950s, 2.9% in the 1960s | Bondholders, slowly |
| 1971 to 2007 | Paper money, floating | 8.0% a year in the 1970s, then 2.3% to 4.5% | Savers in the 1970s |
| 2008 to 2019 | Paper money, near-zero rates | 1.7% a year | Savers, while asset owners gained |
| 2020 to 2026 | Paper money, then higher rates | 4.5% a year, 2020 to 2025 | Cash holders and renters |


### Question 1. Could a world on gold have rebuilt after the war?

[31 p] People who ask this question often assume three things that need fixing. First, some say America held 75% of the world's official gold. The share peaked at about 70% in 1948. Second, some say rebuilding takes fifty years. It took about five. Third, gold was not money for ordinary people in 1945, only for central banks. The rest of the world held about 40% of the gold, and some ruined countries almost none: Italy held $24 million worth. Gold kept flowing to America until 1949.

[32 p] Yet Western Europe's industry passed its 1938 output in 1949, and Japan's real spending its pre-war level by 1951. Work and saving inside each country paid for most of it, not gold and not new money.

[33 table]
| What rebuilt Europe and Japan | The detail |
|---|---|
| Aid | The Marshall Plan gave about $13 billion from 1948, most of it as grants. It came to about 2.5% of the yearly income of the countries that received it, on average |
| A new currency and tight money | West Germany launched a new currency in June 1948. Old savings were converted at 6.5 new marks for every 100 old ones, which wiped out most savings. That was a real loss for savers, but the reform stopped the inflation and got trade moving again |
| Credit between neighbours | From 1950, after the 1949 milestone, the European Payments Union let countries trade on credit and settle later, so they did not need scarce dollars for every deal |
| Debt forgiven | In 1953, after the recovery, West Germany's foreign debt was cut by about half, and repayments were capped at 3% of what it earned from exports |
| Work and exports | Most investment was paid for by each country's own work and saving. Aid was under a fifth of it |

[34 p] Here is the twist. The world was tied to gold only at the top: the dollar for foreign central banks, other currencies to the dollar. So this is a story of work and credit inside a gold anchor, and hard-money writers note that US inflation stayed near 2% to 3% under it. The question is right on one point. The ruined countries lacked the gold and dollars to buy food and machines abroad, and American aid filled the gap. So gold did not do the rebuilding, and a shortage of gold did not stop it. Work, saving, aid and credit did the job. That is a point for neither side. Gold was not the engine, and its lack was not the brake. Whether a world fully on gold could have done it cannot be tested.


### Question 2. The old board and the new players

[36 p] Imagine a game of Monopoly that has run for centuries. The streets are owned. Every year new players are born and sit down with nothing. Which money gives them a fairer start? In Monopoly every player gets $200 for passing Go. Real new money is not shared like that. It enters as loans to people with income or property. And inflation strips the rich of little, since shares and property tend to keep pace with prices. The top 1% share of US wealth hit a record in 2025.

[37 p] Take hard money first. Until 1879 the US dollar was paper with no gold behind it. The government kept that paper scarce so the dollar could go back to gold, and from 1879 to 1897 it was on gold. Across 1866 to 1897 prices fell about 2% a year while output grew roughly 3.5% to 4% a year. So falling prices and fast growth can live together, and people still buy phones that get cheaper every year. But falling prices make every debt heavier, and the young are borrowers before they are savers. At 2% a year falling prices, a loan grows about 50% heavier over 20 years.

[38 p] The country had panics in 1873, 1893 and 1907. It had no central bank, and many banks could not open branches, which left them weak. Britain, also on gold, had a central bank that rescued banks in 1866 and 1890. From 1930 to 1933 the US money supply fell nearly 30% and prices about a quarter, making debts a third heavier. Economists still argue whether the gold rules or the central bank's choices did more harm.

[39 p] Now a modern fixed money. Bitcoin is a digital coin whose supply is capped at 21 million. A 2021 study found the top 10,000 investors, leaving out exchanges and miners, held a quarter to a third of all coins. For a rough comparison, the top 1% of US households, over a million homes, hold about 32% of US net worth. So a fixed money has not shared wealth more evenly so far. But with fixed coins and growing output, each coin buys more every year, so latecomers pay early holders to get in. Credit money can lend to the newcomer, which helps them start and leaves them in debt. A fixed money hands early holders the whole rise in value.

[40 p] Yet the same cheap loans that help one newcomer start can shut the next one out. Cheap loans can push house prices up faster than pay, though later marriage and other changes matter too.

[41 table]
| Who owned a home at age 30 | Early baby boomers | Generation X | Early millennials |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | about 58% | about 51% | about 41% |
| United Kingdom | about 63% | about 60% | about 42% |

[42 p] In Malaysia the median house cost 4.1 times the median household's yearly income in 2019. The usual line for affordable is 3, and Malaysia has been above it every year since 2002. Why are houses so dear? A Bank of England study found that falling interest rates, measured after inflation, explained most of the UK rise. US research points to limits on building. Malaysia has not been tested this way. Both systems tilt the board, toward different people. Hard money favours people who already hold coins or lend, and it hurts borrowers and the young. Credit money favours people who already own houses and shares, and it makes newcomers borrow to catch up. Neither starts the new player level.


### How societies have reset the board

[44 p] When wealth piles up in a few hands, many societies have looked for ways to deal the cards again. History offers six tools.

[45 table]
| Tool | An example | Who paid |
|---|---|---|
| Cancelling debts | Rulers of ancient Babylon are reported to have cancelled some debts by decree | Lenders |
| Land reform | Japan, 1946 to 1950, under US occupation: land farmed by tenants fell from 6.3 million acres to 1.6 million | Landlords |
| Taxing the top | Very high top income tax rates in America after 1945 | High earners |
| Inflation with capped interest rates | America and Britain, 1945 to 1980: low real interest rates wore down debt by about 3% to 4% of national income a year | Savers and bondholders |
| Agreed debt relief | West Germany, 1953: half its foreign debt cancelled | Foreign creditors |
| War and collapse | Japan, 1938 to 1945: the top 1% went from about 20% of income to about 6% | Owners of capital |

[46 p] Notice that all five peaceful tools on the list use the state's power over land, money, debt or tax, and each has a named loser. Credit money adds one quiet tool, inflation with capped interest rates. The others work under any money.

[47 check] Q: A fixed-supply money ends the inflation toll. Why does that not make the game fair for a child born in 100 years?
A: Because the coins are already owned, and if the economy grows each coin gains value. A child earns coins from holders, as children earn dollars today, but any coins they borrow grow heavier while they repay. The toll on cash holders goes, and a toll on newcomers and borrowers takes its place.


### Part 4 of 4. The sword, the 2%, and the verdict from three seats


### Question 3. The sword

[50 p] The defence calls one last witness: a sword. A collector pays 100,000 ringgit for a custom one. The blacksmith and the suppliers receive the 100,000 ringgit as income. The collector now owns a sword. If it were stolen and turned up in a pawn shop, it would fetch a fraction of the price. The defence says money is like this too. Once you spend it, some of its value seems to vanish. Is that why money is made to lose a little value each year?

[51 p] Short answer: no, the sword does not explain the 2%. But it teaches two true things. First, the money was not destroyed. It became the blacksmith's income and went on moving, because one person's spending is another person's pay. And if the collector borrowed, the bank created the 100,000 when it lent. Second, a sword is not money. You cannot sell it quickly, at a fair price, to a stranger. Being easy to pass on is the first job of money.

[52 p] Here is why it does not explain the 2%. The sword loses value because it is one object that is hard to resell. Inflation is different. It is money itself buying less of everything. Central banks aim for about 2% for four other reasons.

[53 table]
| Why a little inflation and not zero | In plain words |
|---|---|
| Pay is hard to cut | A boss who cannot cut wages can skip a raise. With a little inflation that works as a quiet pay cut, and fewer people lose their jobs in bad years. A 1996 study estimated that zero inflation would add 1 to 3 points of unemployment, though others dispute it |
| Room to cut interest rates | If inflation is 2%, normal interest rates sit a few points above zero, which leaves room to cut in a slump, since rates cannot go far below zero |
| The price index overstates inflation | Price indexes miss some quality gains. A 1996 US review found an overstatement of about 1.1 points a year. Later fixes cut it to near 0.8, so a measured 2% is nearer a true 1% |
| Fear of falling prices | Deflation makes debts heavier and people wait to spend, as in the 1930s |

[54 p] So mild inflation is a small fee on money that sits still, like a slow leak in a bucket. It is a design choice with a cost, and the cost falls on whoever holds the bucket longest.


### Three seats at the table

[56 p] The jury has heard both sides. Whether printing looks like a scam depends on which seat is yours. There are three main seats, household, company and government, and the household seat splits into saver, home owner, renter and worker.

[57 table]
| Seat | Under credit money with mild inflation | Under hard money |
|---|---|---|
| Household that saves in cash | Loses. At 2% a year, cash loses half its buying power in 35 years | Gains when prices fall, but has less protection if a bank fails |
| Household with a home loan | Gains. Inflation shrinks the debt | Loses. The debt grows heavier as prices fall |
| Household that rents | Loses when cheap credit lifts house prices and rents | Mixed. Cheaper houses, but less credit to buy one |
| Worker | Gains in slumps, when easier money saves jobs. Loses when prices outrun pay, as in 2021 and 2022 | Loses in slumps. In normal years gains through lower prices, without needing a raise |
| Company | Can borrow before it has profits, and inflation shrinks its debt. A Malaysian exporter gains when the ringgit weakens | Credit is scarce and dear, and debts grow heavier |
| Government | Can fight a crisis, earns the profit on printing notes in normal years, and its debt melts a little each year. The danger is the temptation to overspend | Hands are tied, which blocks abuse and limits rescue, though Britain rescued banks on gold in 1866 and 1890 |

[58 p] A Malaysian sits in one more seat. Income per person in Malaysia grew about six times after inflation from 1971 to 2025, while the ringgit lost about 82% of its buying power. Bank deposits beat inflation in each decade from the 1980s to the 2010s, though not in the 1970s or 2021 to 2025. Malaysia has tried harder money too: a currency board until 1967, and a ringgit fixed to the dollar from 1998 to 2005. Bank Negara sets its own rate, but dollar rates press hard on the ringgit.


### The verdict

[60 steps]
- The jury's finding: not a scam in the plain sense. The US target has been published since 2012, though few people are ever shown who pays. Not theft in law, but in effect a transfer nobody voted on.
- A real toll. It falls on cash holders, renters and people who save late, and perhaps half of the toll on dollar notes falls on people outside America.
- Not a Ponzi by its features. By the strict test the US borrows to pay its interest, and today its deficit before interest is too large. Watch both numbers.
- Hard money is not innocent either. Its toll falls on borrowers and the young, and it limits rescue in a panic.
- So the real argument is not printing against no printing. It is who pays the toll, how big it is, who decides, and whether you can see it. It would change if high inflation ran on, or US debt rates stayed above growth.

[61 p] One more claim deserves a straight answer. Some say credit money has provided wealth for everyone alive. Is that true? Not as stated. World income per person grew 1.6% a year from 1971 to 2024. The share living on under $3 a day fell from 43% in 1990 to about 10%. But growth was faster, 3% a year, in the 1960s under the gold link, partly from post-war catch-up. American output per hour rose 175% from 1971 to 2025 but median family income only 62%. Growth happened under credit money, which does not prove credit money caused it or decided who shared it.

[62 curiosity]
- Q: If printing is theft, who exactly is the victim, and how much did they lose?
  A: Holders of money that pays nothing. On US cash alone, about $488 billion from 2021 to 2025, perhaps half of it lost by holders abroad. Deposits that pay below inflation may add more, but current deposit rates were not measured for this chapter. (builds on rb-moneymake)
- Q: Who gets new money first?
  A: It depends on the door. Bank loans reach borrowers and house sellers first. Bond buying reaches asset owners first. Government cheques reach households first. (builds on rb-moneymake)
- Q: Is Ponzi the right word?
  A: Not by its features. By the strict test the US does borrow to pay its interest. That works while growth beats the rate, as it did in 19 or 20 of the 25 years to 2025 but in only 40 of the 64 years from 1962, and while the deficit before interest stays small. (builds on rb-07)
- Q: Did hard money keep the board level?
  A: No. The US gold era had deflation that squeezed borrowers and repeated banking panics, partly blamed on its banking rules. How wealth was shared then could not be tested in this research. (builds on rb-02)
- Q: What rebuilt Europe and Japan after 1945?
  A: Work and saving inside each country above all, helped by aid and credit between neighbours. Debt relief came later, in 1953. Gold was flowing to America, not from it, until 1949. (builds on rb-03)
- Q: Why 2% and not zero, and does the sword explain it?
  A: Sticky pay, room to cut rates, a price index that overstates inflation, and fear of deflation. The sword explains something else: spending is income, and a thing you cannot resell quickly at a fair price is not money. (builds on rb-08)
- Q: Would a fixed money give a child born in 2126 a fair start?
  A: Not by itself. The coins would already be owned, and the child would start as a borrower. Fairness would still depend on tax, inheritance and credit. (builds on rb-defl)


### Five checks when someone says it is a scam

[64 steps]
- Which printing do they mean: bank loans, central bank buying, or government spending paid for with new money?
- Who pays, and how much? Ask for a number.
- Compared with what? Name the alternative and its own losers.
- Who got the new money first this time?
- What number would prove them right? For a Ponzi claim it is the interest rate against growth, plus the deficit before interest. For a theft claim it is inflation against the published target.

[65.1 p] Each side concedes something. Some critics, such as the free-banking economists Selgin and White, accept that money demand grows as an economy grows. Economists of the MMT school add that a government borrowing in its own money cannot be forced to default, so its real limit is inflation, not lenders.

[66 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): When anyone could print: free banking, and money you guard yourself
[66.0 p] America tried letting banks print their own notes from 1837 to 1863. A study of 709 such banks in four states found that about 48% closed, but only about 15% failed to pay their notes in full. The authors found that losses came mainly from falls in the state bonds that backed the notes, rather than from fraud. Some historians argue that Scotland ran a similar system for over a century with few failures, in part because most Scottish bank owners were liable for losses without limit.

[66.1 p] Money you guard yourself moves the risk from an institution to you. About $3.4 billion of crypto was reported stolen from January to early December 2025, including about $1.5 billion in one exchange hack. A stolen key is rarely insured. A bank deposit can be frozen or inflated away, but in many countries it is insured up to a limit. Losses from bank failures and bank fraud were not sized for this chapter, so the comparison is one-sided. The lesson from both stories is the same: the question was never central against decentral alone. It is who bears the losses and who checks the issuer.

[67 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Does stored wealth stall the world? And attempts to force money to move
[67.0 p] Here is a sharp question: if most wealth sits in houses and savings that do not circulate, how does a society advance? In the US each dollar of money was spent about 2.2 times a year in 1997, and only 1.2 times in 2020. Money did slow down, and by 2026 it was back to about 1.4. Japanese households hold about 47% of their financial wealth in cash and deposits.

[67.1 p] But a house is not money sitting still. The seller received the money and spent or lent it. What stalls an economy is everyone trying to save at the same moment, which is a problem in slumps, not in normal years.

[67.2 p] People have tried to force money to move. In 1932 the Austrian town of Woergl issued notes that lost value each month unless stamped. Supporters say they changed hands far faster than normal money. The central bank shut the experiment down in 1933. Negative interest rates, used in Europe and Japan in the 2010s, were the modern version, and banks are reported to have mostly spared ordinary savers. Mild inflation is the gentlest form of the same idea.
