## L3.5 Who wrote the rules of money, and for whom? (id rb-law)

Kicker: Every country has a body that issues its money, and a law that says what that body is for. A reader says it exists to let output and progress grow, that it inflates a little on purpose for the public good, and that it is kept under control. This chapter reads the laws, follows the money, and visits the places that tried other rules.

[0 tldr]
- Central banks were first built to pay for wars and stop bank panics. Growth was written into the law later, and often behind stable prices. Malaysia's law does name growth: it asks for stability that helps the economy grow.
- Mild inflation is on purpose, and it is published. When inflation runs above what lenders expected, or rules hold interest rates down, a large winner is the government as a borrower.
- The people who run the money answer to the governments that appoint them, and they must publish what they do. Voters elect the lawmakers who write these laws but are rarely asked directly. When asked, they mostly kept what they had.
- Countries that tied their own hands usually got steadier prices, some for decades, and paid for it in a crisis. Countries that kept their hands free could rescue and steer, and some were ruined by it.

### The claim on trial

[4 p] A central bank is the body that issues a country's money and stands behind its banks. In Malaysia it is Bank Negara. In America it is the Federal Reserve. The reader who wrote in has a view about what such a body is for. Here it is in four parts.

[5 steps]
- Part 1. The body that issues money exists so that output, meaning everything a country makes in a year, and progress can grow.
- Part 2. It lets prices rise a little each year on purpose, and that serves the public.
- Part 3. It runs under stable, controlled governance.
- Part 4. These are rules the public has chosen.

[6 p] A fifth test comes at the end. Some countries wrote very different rules. What happened to them?


### A country without its own money

[8 p] Start close to home, in Malaya in the early 1950s. The country had banknotes, but it had no central bank. Its money was issued by a currency board. The board followed one hard rule. It issued a Malayan dollar only in exchange for British pounds, which it kept in reserve. In the early 1950s it had almost no power to issue more. The same board also issued Singapore's money.

[9 p] That rule kept the money sound. A Malayan dollar was as good as the pounds behind it. But there was not much money to go round.

[10 table]
| Place | Money for each person, early 1950s (Bank of England, 1962) |
|---|---|
| Federation of Malaya | About 17 pounds |
| Britain | About 120 pounds |

[11 p] The Bank of England put most of that gap down to a young money economy, where money and banks were still new to many people. But the rule played a part. Money at home could grow only when more pounds came in from abroad. And the board could do very little to help. It did not lend, and it could not make loans easier to get when times were bad. Only from December 1961 could it issue some money against crop loans. In 1962 the Bank of England named two standard complaints about old-style boards like this one: reserves locked up abroad, and what a passive board could not do.

[12 quote] "to exert positive influence on credit conditions" (What old-style currency boards in general could not do, in the words of the Bank of England, 1962)

[13 p] The same passage said this led to "painful deflationary correctives in times of depression". In plain words: a board could not cushion a slump, so the pain came through falling prices and wages. Malaya wanted an issuer that could manage credit and free its reserves. Bank Negara began work in 1959. But for eight more years the board still issued the notes, because the places that shared it hoped to keep one currency. Bank Negara was open, but it did not yet issue the money. That plan failed after Singapore left Malaysia in 1965. On 12 June 1967 Bank Negara became the only issuer of Malaysia's money.

[14 p] That is part of the reader's claim in one story. A new country wanted an issuer that could manage its own credit, and it wrote a law to get one. But the final step in 1967 came from a failed plan to share a currency. And Singapore took the other road. It kept a currency board until 2002, and its economy still grew fast, by most accounts. The question is how far that story holds for the rest of the world, and what each road has cost.


### Test 1. What was the issuer created to do?

[16 p] The simplest test is to read the laws. What did each central bank say it was for?

[17 table]
| Central bank | When | What its law or charter says it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Bank of England | 1694 | To raise 1.2 million pounds for a war, lent to the government at 8% |
| America's Federal Reserve | 1913 | Its founding law speaks of an elastic currency, meaning money that can stretch in a panic, and of better supervision of the banks |
| America's Federal Reserve | 1977 | A new line was added: "maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates". Money should grow with the economy's power to produce |
| Bank of England | 1998 | Stable prices first. Then, as long as prices are stable, support for the government's aims for growth and jobs |
| European Central Bank | Treaty as in force today | Stable prices are the main aim. Anything else comes only if prices are safe |
| Bank Negara Malaysia | 2009 | Stability "conducive to the sustainable growth of the Malaysian economy". Conducive means helpful. Stable prices are the main aim of its interest rate decisions. Jobs are not named in sections 5 and 22 |

[18 p] Read down the last column. The oldest central bank was built to pay for a war. America's was built to stop bank panics. Growth and jobs were written in later. Britain, Europe and Malaysia put them after stable prices. America's 1977 law lists jobs and prices side by side. Malaysia's law is closer to the reader's picture than most. It says the point of stability is growth.

[19 p] New countries after 1945 had the reader's idea too. By one count, at least 25 developing countries set up or rebuilt central banks in those years, with powers to steer credit toward development. The economist Gerald Epstein argues that, for many central banks, financing the government and directing credit were among the reasons they were set up. He also grants that it sometimes went badly.

[20 insider] (What test 1 does to the claim) Part 1 holds in part. The first central banks were built for war and for panics, not for progress. Growth entered the law later, often behind stable prices. But many central banks set up after 1945 were given powers to steer credit toward development, and Malaysia's law today names growth as the point of stability.

[21 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Steering credit: where it helped and where it hurt
[21.0 p] Several countries that grew fast after 1945 used their banks to steer money toward chosen industries. In Japan, by one account, savings collected through the post office were passed by the finance ministry, of which the central bank was then a part, to the industries seen as best for growth. Epstein concludes that central banks helped development most in late developers, where they were part of a government's plan for industry. He also grants that these tools in some cases had bad results in the end.

[21.1 p] South Korea shows both sides. In the 1970s it is reported to have steered bank lending to heavy and chemical industries. A Korean government research body later said this lending left large firms with weak finances and small firms short of credit, and most cheap policy loans were ended in June 1982. Several Latin American countries that used similar tools, Argentina among them, later had very high inflation.

[21.2 p] Malaysia still uses a milder form. Six development banks, such as Bank Rakyat, Agrobank and SME Bank, work under their own law and are supervised by Bank Negara. In January 2026 Bank Negara is reported to have added 2.5 billion ringgit to its fund for small firms.

[21.3 p] Words in a law are not the same as practice. A study of 72 countries, as others report it, found that in poorer countries how often the governor was replaced said more about inflation than what the law said.

[21.4 p] One more study is often quoted here, though it tests something narrower than the reader's claim. It asked whether central banks kept apart from politicians do better. Across 16 rich countries from 1955 to 1988, they had lower inflation. It is reported to have found no clear effect on growth or jobs, one way or the other. It did not cover poorer countries.

### Test 2. Is mild inflation on purpose, and who gains?

[23 p] It is on purpose. In January 2012 America's central bank announced that it would aim for prices to rise 2% a year, and said this was the rate "most consistent over the longer run" with its duties. Bank Negara has no number in its law. Nobody hides this. The earlier chapters called it a toll. So who collects?

[24 p] There are two collectors. When inflation is a surprise, the second can be much bigger than the first.

[25 p] The first is the profit on issuing money. A central bank pays almost nothing to create money and earns interest on what it buys with it. Part of the profit goes to the government, as each country's law decides.

[26 table]
| Central bank | Year | Paid to its government |
|---|---|---|
| America's Federal Reserve | 2021 | 109 billion dollars, its largest regular payment in 2011 to 2022 |
| America's Federal Reserve | 2023 to 2025 | Nothing. It was paying out more interest than it earned. Before it can pay the government again, it must earn back a shortfall of 243.5 billion dollars, as at the end of 2025 |
| Bank Negara Malaysia | 2024 | A recommended dividend of 5.25 billion ringgit, about 40% of a profit of 13.16 billion |

[27 p] The second collector is the government as a borrower. When prices rise, a debt fixed in money is easier to pay back, because each unit of money buys less, and in most countries the biggest debtor is the state. But lenders know this. If they expect 2% inflation, they ask for interest that covers it, and nobody is cheated. If prices then rise 4%, the extra 2 points is a surprise. The lender is paid back in money that buys less than expected, so the lender loses and the borrower gains. That works like a hidden tax on the lender. So the state gains most when inflation beats what lenders expected, or when rules hold interest rates below inflation. Both have happened.

[28 table]
| A loan of 100 for one year | Lenders expect prices to rise 2%, so they ask 2% interest | Prices really rise 4% |
|---|---|---|
| What the borrower pays back | 102 | 102 |
| What 102 buys, in last year's prices | About 100. The lender is no worse off | About 98. The lender has lost about 2, and the borrower has gained it |

[29 p] This is a made-up example with round numbers. The same thing happens to a government's debt, on a much larger scale.

[30 table]
| Case | What happened | How much lighter the state's debt became |
|---|---|---|
| America and Britain, about 1945 to 1980 | Interest rates were kept below inflation, often by caps and by rules that made banks hold government bonds | By one estimate, each year the debt got lighter by an amount equal to 3% to 4% of everything the country made that year |
| America, 2021 | Prices rose much faster than lenders expected | 3.3% of output in one year. A staff study at the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis called it a 6.5% tax on holders of government bonds of a year or more. About 38% of those bonds, leaving out the central bank's own, were held abroad |

[31 table]
| Who gains from a little inflation | Who pays |
|---|---|
| The government, whose debts grow lighter, most when inflation is a surprise | Anyone who lent to the government at a fixed rate, at home or abroad |
| Borrowers with fixed loans, such as home owners | Savers who hold cash or accounts that pay little |
| Taxpayers, who may need to pay less tax to pay the interest on the debt | People on fixed incomes |

[32 p] So does it serve the public? It depends which part of the public. A taxpayer and a saver are often the same person, gaining with one hand and losing with the other. The earlier chapters gave the reasons for a small target: room to cut interest rates, and a cushion against falling prices. Those reasons are real. But the gain to the state as a borrower is real too, mostly when inflation is a surprise or rates are held down, and it is less often explained.

[33 insider] (What test 2 does to the claim) Part 2 holds in part. Mild inflation is on purpose and it is published. It has public reasons. When it beats what lenders expected, or rates are held down, it also moves a great deal of wealth from savers and lenders to the state. That transfer is less often explained.

In America, Britain and Malaysia the law says who appoints the leaders, for how long, and what they must publish. The table below shows the main rules in two of them. [38 p] So there is control. But notice who holds it. Appointing a governor is not the same as running the bank: Malaysia's law says the Bank sets monetary policy on its own. In each country the central bank answers to the law and to the government of the day, and to the public mainly through what it must publish. Britain's governor must write a public letter whenever inflation misses the target by more than one point. No voter chooses a governor directly.

[39 p] Is that distance from politics good or bad? The record points both ways.

[40 p] Here is what can happen when a government bends the issuer to its own needs, though other things went wrong in each place too. In January 2009 Zimbabwe's central bank announced a banknote for 100 trillion dollars. On the black market it was worth about 33 American dollars. People often queued for hours outside banks to draw out barely enough to buy a loaf of bread. Farm seizures and drought had also hurt.

[41 p] Venezuela's own central bank reported inflation of 130,060% for 2018, and outside estimates were higher. Oil prices and sanctions also hurt. Turkey is reported to have removed three central bank governors between 2019 and 2021, and its inflation is reported to have reached about 85% in 2022, a year of high energy prices.

[42 p] And here is what can happen when the issuer is left alone. America's central bank was independent in the years before the crash of 2008. An official inquiry is reported to have found that it failed in its legal duty to set standards for home loans, though four of its members disagreed. So it was free of politicians and still made a serious mistake.

[43 insider] (What test 3 does to the claim) Part 3 holds in part. The governance is controlled, by governments and by rules on what must be published. It is not controlled directly by voters. Keeping the issuer apart from politicians has gone with lower inflation in rich countries, and it has not prevented serious mistakes.


### Test 4. Did the public choose these rules?

[45 p] Laws about money were passed by elected lawmakers. America's central bank law passed the Senate by 43 votes to 25 in December 1913. But lawmakers are not the same as the public, and some of the biggest changes had no vote at all. Chapter 03 told how the dollar's link to gold was ended by a president over one weekend.

[46 p] How often has the public been asked directly? Rarely. Here are the cases this chapter could find.

[47 table]
| Where and when | The question | The answer |
|---|---|---|
| Switzerland, 1999 | Accept a new constitution, which also ended the old tie between the franc and gold? | Yes, by about 59%, as reported. This vote changed a rule |
| Denmark, 2000 | Give up the krone for the euro? | No, by about 53%, as reported |
| Sweden, 2003 | Give up the krona for the euro? | No, by about 56%, as reported |
| Switzerland, 2018 | Stop banks from creating money when they lend, and leave that to the state? | No, by about 76%, as reported. About one voter in three took part |

[48 p] Look at the four answers. Three times, voters kept what they already had. Once, in Switzerland in 1999, they accepted a change. The Danes and Swedes kept their own money. In 2018 the Swiss kept the right of banks to make money when they lend. That is not quite the same as choosing the system. It may only mean that people are careful with something as serious as money, and prefer the rules they know.

[49 insider] (What test 4 does to the claim) Part 4 holds at one remove. Voters elect the lawmakers who passed the money laws, but some big changes had no vote at all. When the public was asked directly, which was rare, it mostly kept what it already had.

[54 p] Argentina's end is worth seeing up close. In December 2001 the government partly froze bank deposits. People could not get at their own savings. In the last days of that month, middle-class people in Buenos Aires took to the streets, banging pots and pans in protest. The fixed rule had kept their money sound for ten years. Now the rate was fixed and many debts were in dollars. The money could not give way, so output and jobs gave way instead.

[55 p] Malaysia has tied its own hands too, by choice, as earlier chapters told. On 2 September 1998, in the middle of the Asian crisis, Bank Negara fixed the ringgit at 3.80 to the US dollar and limited money leaving the country. The fixed rate lasted until 2005. How much it helped is still argued over. But it shows that a country with its own central bank can tie its hands for a while, and untie them later.

[56 p] The newest experiment is in El Salvador, which already used the US dollar. So it tested something else: whether a government can bring in a new kind of money from the top down. On 7 September 2021 it made Bitcoin legal money beside the US dollar, and gave 30 dollars' worth of bitcoin to everyone who downloaded the state's phone wallet. By early 2022 about half of all households had done so. More than 60% of the early users made no payment at all once the 30 dollars was spent. Only one firm in five accepted Bitcoin, and about 5% of all sales were paid in it. The government says the law drew tourists and investment. On the first day a reporter found a market trading in dollars as usual.

[57 quote] "We don't want that bitcoin." (Rosa Martha Perez, 68, who marched against the law in San Salvador on the day it took effect (AFP, 7 September 2021))

[58 p] In 2025, as a condition of a loan of 1.4 billion dollars from the International Monetary Fund, El Salvador changed its law so that private firms no longer had to accept Bitcoin, and taxes are paid only in dollars. The law could make Bitcoin legal money, but it could not make people use it.

[59 p] Put these cases beside Malaya's currency board, Singapore's, and Malaysia's fixed rate of 1998, and a pattern appears. Hong Kong's link has held for over forty years. Argentina's ended in 2001 after output fell nearly 20%. It is a pattern, not a law. Each case has its own history, so no single case proves the rule.

[61 insider] (What test 5 does to the claim) Test 5 gives the reader's claim some support and a real warning. A country with tied hands has little power to steer credit or rescue its banks, which was one reason Malaya wanted a central bank. Tied hands have also lasted for decades, in Hong Kong and Singapore. But free hands are only as good as the people and rules that guide them. Zimbabwe had free hands too.

[62 check] Q: A friend says central banks should be abolished and money should follow a fixed rule. Give one point for and one against, each with a case.
A: For: a fixed rule can end wild inflation. Argentina's fell from 27% a month to under 10% a year in about two and a half years. Against: a fixed rule cannot bend in a crisis. Argentina's output then fell nearly 20% and deposits were frozen, and Hong Kong's output fell 5% in 1998 while its rate held, in a crisis across the region.

[65 p] Here is the claim again, rebuilt so the record can carry it.

[66 steps]
- The first central banks were built to fund governments and to stop panics. Many later ones, in countries trying to catch up, were also given powers to steer credit toward growth. Malaya's began work in 1959 and became the only issuer in 1967, when a shared currency failed.
- A free issuer has more room than a tied one to rescue banks, soften a slump and direct credit. Each can be done well or badly. Tied hands have also lasted for decades.
- The price is a toll, mild inflation, and it is published. When it beats what lenders expected, or rates are held down, much of the gain goes to the state as a borrower.
- The issuer answers to the government that appoints it and to rules on what it must publish. Voters choose the lawmakers who write those laws but are rarely asked directly. When asked, they have mostly kept what they had.

[67 p] So was the reader right that the issuer exists for progress? For Malaysia, more than for Britain or America. And was the reader right that it is under control? Under control, yes: by governments, by the law and by what must be published. How much direct say voters should have is a fair question, and this chapter leaves it open.

[68 insider] (What would change this verdict) This view would change if any of three things were shown. First, that countries with free central banks grow faster over the long run than countries with tied hands, with other causes set aside. Second, that directed credit has succeeded more often than it has failed. Third, that the public, asked plainly, would choose a different system. The first two are not settled in this chapter, and the third has been tested only a few times. This chapter also did not test the rules for ordinary banks, which make most money.

[69 deeper] GO DEEPER BOX (collapsed by default): Three ideas about why the issuer exists
[69.0 p] Economists disagree about why a central bank should exist at all. One old view says it is there to lend freely in a panic, so that sound banks do not fall with weak ones. A second says it must be kept apart from politicians, because a government facing an election will always be tempted to print. A third, held by free-banking economists, says it should not exist: banks issuing their own notes in competition would be kept honest by each other. Canada is often named in that argument. It had no central bank until 1935, by the Bank of Canada's own account, and none of its banks failed in the Depression, though its economy fell about as far as America's. Economists also credit its few large banks, with branches across the country.

[69.1 p] One plan is not known to have been tried on a whole country. It would stop banks from creating money and leave that to the state alone. A 2012 working paper by two economists at the International Monetary Fund, which does not speak for the Fund, found output gains close to 10% in a model. Swiss voters rejected a version of it in 2018, and the head of the Swiss central bank is reported to have called it an unnecessary and dangerous experiment.
