## Evidence for and against H1, H3 and H5

### H1 (the issuer exists to let output and progress grow)

For:
- Epstein (2005) shows that financing governments and supporting sectors with direct credit were "among the reasons for their existence" for many central banks, and the New York Fed helped about 25 developing countries set up central banks with wide credit powers after 1945 (verified).
- The Bank of England (1962) named the core weakness of the Malayan currency board: it could not "exert positive influence on credit conditions" and forced "painful deflationary correctives in times of depression" (verified). Canada founded its central bank in the Depression to answer a call for action, after its banks had survived without one (secondary).

Against:
- The classic case for the modern issuer is price stability and avoiding panics, not growth. Alesina and Summers found independence lowered inflation with no visible gain in growth (secondary).
- Adolph (2013) and Posen (1995) argue central banks reflect the interests of the financial sector that backs them (secondary). Several founding motives (lender of last resort, government finance) concern stability and the state's needs more than progress.

### H3 (the governance is controlled and answerable)

For:
- Where the central bank was kept apart from the treasury, inflation was lower across 16 rich countries in 1955 to 1988 (secondary, St Louis Fed verified).
- Hong Kong's rule-bound board held its link through the 1997 to 1998 attack with no change in the rate (verified).

Against:
- Capture is common and its costs are huge: Zimbabwe 231 million percent (2008), Venezuela 130,060% (2018, central bank figure), Turkey 85.51% (October 2022) after governors were dismissed over rates, Argentina 211.4% (2023) (secondary to verified).
- In developing countries legal independence says little. The turnover of governors is the better guide (Cukierman, Webb and Neyapti 1992, secondary), and independence may follow inflation rather than cause low inflation (St Louis Fed, verified).

### H5 (alternatives exist and were tried)

For:
- Hard rules ended high inflation fast: Argentina from 27% a month (February 1991) to single digits a year by mid-1993 with about 6% growth a year to 1998, Ecuador from 90% (February 2000) to single digits from 2003 (verified and secondary).
- Free banking produced stable banks: no Canadian bank failed in the 1930s, and Scottish failure rates were lower than England's in 1809 to 1830 on White's figures (verified).

Against:
- Hard rules pay for stability with output when shocks hit: Argentina lost nearly 20% of output in 1998 to 2002 with unemployment over 20% and a deposit freeze. Hong Kong shrank 5% in 1998 with prices falling from late 1998 (verified).
- The newer alternatives have little record: no modern country runs full reserve banking (Swiss voters rejected it, 75.7% no), and Bitcoin as legal tender drew little use (20% of firms accepted it, about 5% of sales) and was rolled back under the IMF programme (verified).
