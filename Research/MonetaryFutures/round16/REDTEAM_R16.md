### RT-01. The Malaya story leaves out why the change came in 1967 (blocks 12, 13, 53, 58)

What is wrong. The draft says the board made bad times worse, "So Malaya built a central bank", and by block 53 that tied hands are "why Malaya untied its own". But Bank Negara began in 1959 and the board kept issuing for eight more years. The 1967 end came when the plan for one shared currency with Singapore and Brunei broke down after Singapore left Malaysia in 1965. On 12 June 1967 all three began to issue their own money, still exchanged at par until 1973. Singapore then kept a currency board until 2002, with full backing for its notes, and grew fast. Brunei's dollar is still exchanged one for one with Singapore's. A historian of Malaysia would say the draft turns a political split into a story about growth. The reader's claim is still partly true for Malaya, but the neighbours show that tied hands and fast growth went together too.

Evidence.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaya_and_British_Borneo_dollar (currency union ended 12 June 1967, three new currencies)
- https://www.bnm.gov.my/web/guest/significant-milestones-in-the-malaysian-foreign-exchange-market (sole issue 12 June 1967, interchangeability ended 8 May 1973)
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei%E2%80%93Singapore_Currency_Interchangeability_Agreement
- https://www.mof.gov.sg/news-resources/newsroom/merger-of-the-board-of-commissioners-of-currency-singapore-bccs-with-the-monetary-authority-of-singapore-mas-/ (currency board merged into MAS in 2002, full backing kept)

Fix, block 12.
OLD: In plain words: when times were bad, a board made them worse. So Malaya built a central bank, which began work in 1959. On 12 June 1967 it became the only issuer of the country's money.
NEW: In plain words: when times were bad, a board could not soften the blow. Malaya built a central bank, which began work in 1959. But the board kept issuing the money for eight more years, because Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei hoped to share one currency. That plan failed after Singapore left Malaysia in 1965. On 12 June 1967 each of the three began to issue its own money, and Bank Negara became the only issuer of Malaysia's.

Fix, block 13.
OLD: That is the reader's claim in one story. A new country wanted an issuer that could steer credit toward its own growth, and it wrote a law to get one.
NEW: That is the reader's claim in one story, though not the whole story. A new country wanted an issuer that could steer credit toward its own growth, and it wrote a law to get one. Its neighbour chose the other road. Singapore kept a currency board until 2002 and grew fast all the same. Brunei's dollar is still exchanged one for one with Singapore's.

Fix, block 53.
OLD: A country with tied hands cannot steer credit or rescue its banks, which is why Malaya untied its own.
NEW: A country with tied hands has little power to steer credit or rescue its banks, and that was one reason Malaya wanted a central bank of its own. Singapore and Brunei kept their hands tied and still did well.

Fix, block 58.

NEW: Malaya set one up in 1959. The shared currency board ended in 1967, when the plan for one currency with Singapore and Brunei failed.

### RT-02. "The biggest winner is the government" ignores that lenders price in expected inflation (blocks 0, 23, 26, 29, 56, 58)

What is wrong. A steady, expected 2% does not transfer much wealth. Lenders who expect 2% ask for interest that covers it. The state gains in two cases: when inflation turns out higher than expected (2021, the draft's own example), or when rules hold interest rates below inflation. The 1945 to 1980 figure from Reinhart and Sbrancia is mainly about the second case. Their paper is about "financial repression": caps on interest rates and rules forcing banks to hold government bonds. The draft writes "low interest rates and rising prices together" but never says the low rates were held down by rules. So it presents the effect of capped rates and surprise inflation as the effect of a 2% target. A central banker would call this the single biggest error in the chapter. The St Louis Fed post the draft cites is itself about "surprise inflation" and defines the surprise as the gap between expected and actual inflation.

Evidence.
- https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2022/mar/how-much-are-we-taxed-surprise-inflation (title and method are about surprise inflation. The 3.3%, 6.5% and 38% figures are confirmed. The 38% excludes debt held by the Fed)

Fix, block 0.
OLD: Its biggest winner is the government as a borrower, and that is rarely said out loud.
NEW: When inflation runs higher than lenders expected, a large winner is the government as a borrower. That part is less often explained.

Fix, block 23.
OLD: There are two collectors, and the second is much bigger than the first.
NEW: There are two collectors. In a year when inflation is a surprise, the second is much bigger than the first.

Fix, block 26.
OLD: When prices rise, every old debt becomes lighter, and the biggest debtor in any country is the state. From 1945 to 1980, low interest rates and rising prices together shrank the debts of America and Britain by an amount equal to 3% to 4% of everything those countries made, every year.
NEW: When prices rise, old debts at fixed rates become lighter, and in most countries the biggest debtor is the state. But lenders know this. If they expect 2% inflation, they ask for interest that covers it. The state gains most when inflation turns out higher than lenders expected, or when rules hold interest rates down. From 1945 to 1980, rules that kept interest rates low, together with rising prices, shrank the debts of America and Britain by an amount equal to 3% to 4% of everything those countries made, every year.

Also in block 26, the 38% sentence: OLD: About 38% of those bonds were held abroad.
NEW: About 38% of those bonds, leaving out those held by the central bank itself, were held abroad.

Fix, block 29.
OLD: It also moves a great deal of wealth from savers and lenders to the state, and that transfer is rarely named.
NEW: When inflation runs above what lenders expected, or interest rates are held down, it also moves a great deal of wealth from savers and lenders to the state. That transfer is less often explained.

Fix, block 56, row 2, third cell.
OLD: From 1945 to 1980 inflation and low rates cut American and British debt by 3% to 4% of output a year
NEW: From 1945 to 1980 inflation, with interest rates held down by rules, cut American and British debt by 3% to 4% of output a year. In 2021 surprise inflation cut America's by 3.3%

Fix, block 58.
OLD: The price is a toll, mild inflation, whose largest gain goes to the state as a borrower.
NEW: The price is a toll, mild inflation. When it runs above what lenders expected, much of the gain goes to the state as a borrower.

### RT-05. Test 1 judges purpose by founding words and gives development central banking a thin hearing (blocks 17, 18, 19)

Block 19 says Alesina and Summers "cuts against Part 1", but a finding that independence did not change growth does not test whether central banks foster growth. That leans against the reader.

### RT-13. Omissions that change the verdict (after block 60)

What matters most for the verdict.
1. Commercial banks. Block 1 says most money is made when banks lend, but the chapter tests only the central bank. Who writes the rules for bank lending (in Malaysia, Bank Negara also regulates the banks) is part of "who wrote the rules of money".
2. The US dollar's reach. When America's central bank raises rates, money tends to leave countries like Malaysia and the ringgit comes under pressure. So part of Malaysia's monetary rules are written in Washington, which matters for Part 3 and Part 4.
3. The IMF. It appears only at the end of El Salvador. For small countries its loan conditions can rewrite money laws, as El Salvador shows. Malaysia's choice in 1998 not to take an IMF programme is a local story worth one sentence.
4. Islamic finance. Malaysia runs Islamic and conventional banking side by side, both under Bank Negara, with a Shariah Advisory Council that rules on Islamic finance matters (verify the current law). This is a real part of who writes the rules of money in Malaysia. It can be described neutrally as an institution.
Less critical: the central bank digital currency debate. It belongs in a later chapter unless the verdict turns on it.

Fix: add a short go-deeper box after block 60.
OLD: The first two are not settled in this chapter, and the third has been tested only a few times.
NEW: The first two are not settled in this chapter, and the third has been tested only a few times. Two more forces shape Malaysia's money and are not tested here. Most money is made by commercial banks, and Bank Negara writes the rules they lend under, for Islamic and conventional banks alike. ## 2. Eight voices: the single most important miss for each

- A central banker. Block 26 and the summary in block 0 treat a published 2% target as a quiet transfer to the state. Expected inflation is priced into interest rates. Only surprise inflation, or rates held down by rules, moves wealth on a large scale. See RT-02.

For this school, a state that issues its own money is limited by inflation and real resources, not by its debt, so "who gains from inflation" is the wrong frame.
