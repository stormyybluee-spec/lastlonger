## 1. Summary

Counts (each VF id counted once, each red team point or note row counted once): 129 rulings. Accept 104, accept in part 21, reject 4. The four rejects are red team memory or unsourced items (Hong Kong 6%, a smaller Ecuador fall, a modern monetary theory paragraph, a household inflation scene). Section 3 holds 83 edits and 8 new blocks. Hand work: delete tables 32, 41 and 46, block 19, and the Gallup row of the sources table.

What changes most:
1. The Malaya opening stays a scene but its facts are fixed. The Bank of England's two quotations are now said to describe old-style boards in general. The rubber planter and tin prices are gone. The board's 1961 crop finance power is in the text. The 17 against 120 pounds goes in a table, with the Bank of England's own main cause, a young money economy, beside the rule. The 1967 step is tied to the failed shared currency, and Singapore's currency board to 2002 is shown as the other road.
2. Test 2 now says how the state gains: expected inflation is priced in, and large gains come from surprise inflation or rates held down by rules. A worked case (2% expected, 4% actual) explains it. "Most of the profit" and the Fed's "most ever" are corrected.
3. The governance table moves into a go-deeper box with the exact Malaysian Act wording (section 15 on the King's appointment, the 12.5% limit and its repayment, the Cabinet under section 72 against the Bank's autonomy under section 22). Blanks read "Not checked in this chapter".
4. Test 5 is fairer to tied hands: a short main-path table, a full table in a box with rival causes, and a new Malaysian scene, the 1998 fixed rate of 3.80. El Salvador is framed as a top-down new money, not a test of tied hands.
5. Main path rises from about 3,470 to about 3,890 words (kicker included). New evidence went into three go-deeper boxes. New main-path text carries a mechanism or a scene.

How the four findings now read:
- Part 1, the issuer exists for growth: holds in part. True of many later central banks, not of the first. Decided by the Bank of England's war loan of 1694 and Malaysia's 2009 law.
- Part 2, mild inflation on purpose for the public good: holds in part. On purpose and published. Large gains to the state come from surprise inflation or capped rates. Decided by 3% to 4% a year in America and Britain about 1945 to 1980 under held-down rates, and 3.3% in 2021 from surprise inflation.
- Part 3, stable controlled governance: holds in part. Controlled by governments, laws and publication rules, not directly by voters. Decided by terms and removal grounds in law, Bank Negara's accounts before Parliament and Britain's 12 public letters (Zimbabwe no longer decides it).
- Part 4, the public chose the rules: holds at one remove, through elected lawmakers. Decided by the 1913 Senate vote and four direct votes, three that kept the system and one (Switzerland 1999) that changed a rule.
The closing line becomes an open, honest question: how much direct say voters should have.
