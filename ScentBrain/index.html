02 · VIRTUAL NOSE

Smell it on paper first

No AI can smell. What it can do is the paper review a senior perfumer does before opening a bottle. It works out which materials a nose will actually notice, what survives the wash and the dryer, what reacts with the base, what leaks from a capsule, and where a formula breaks its own brief. Paste any formula with CAS numbers and the audit runs instantly. "Senior panel review" then asks Claude to critique it as three senior perfumers and propose a fix, and that fix is audited the same way. Before you rely on the numbers, run the data check with Gemini.

InputFormula, accords and capsule oilOne material per line: name, CAS, %

Start an accord with @CODE name. Start a formula with = name, and add the word "capsule" for a capsule oil. A line that names an accord code, such as CH-1 accord | 28, is expanded automatically. Write dilutions like Geosmin, 1% in IPM.

What the paper review catches

Before anyone smells

  • Potent traces that break the brief (a melon or musty note at 0.05% can be louder than 10% of a musk)
  • Notes that rinse away or leave through the dryer vent
  • Aldehydes that react with amino silicone and yellow the liquid
  • Capsule oils that leak because the materials dissolve in water
  • Overdoses, banned materials, allergens to declare
What it cannot catch

Only a nose can

  • Whether the accord is beautiful, and whether it reads as chrysanthemum tea rather than "a nice floral"
  • How one supplier's natural oil differs from another's
  • How a specific detergent base flattens or bends a note
  • Musk anosmia: some people barely smell certain musks
Fastest route to world class

Measure the target, not just describe it

Ask the OEM for a headspace GC-MS of your reference drink and of the lab tea. That lists the molecules that actually make the smell. Paste those into the brain as a target accord, and every formula can be scored against the real thing instead of a description.

04 · THE ENGINE

From one line to a perfumer-grade formula

The goal is to skip you. When you type a raw brief, the brain should reach what a senior perfumer team would hand you without you having to push, paste a review or ask twice. This section is the brain's operating manual. Every brief runs through it, at one of three depths.

Case 1 · why the engine exists · Chrysanthemum brief v2, 3 Nov 2026

A complete-looking brief with ten paper failures

Another chat wrote nine chrysanthemum tea formulas that read as finished. Only after you asked for a senior review did ten problems surface: geosmin 15 times over a safe level, camphor and marine notes the brief itself banned, candy notes, colour formers that would block shipping, leaky capsules, a tea core too small to survive a dryer, and Creative and Accurate versions that smelled the same after drying. None were hidden. Each one was sitting in the numbers. The lesson: a formula is not done when it looks complete. It is done when it has been framed, measured, attacked and scored.

Handoff · using the brain from another chat

How your R&D chat should use this page

  1. Give the other chat this page's link and ask it to read the page with its Artifact tool. Section 04 and PLAN.md are its operating manual.
  2. Paste each draft formula into the Virtual Nose (one material per line: name, CAS, %), with the real product context and planned label claims.
  3. Fix every fail, then run the Full review before anything goes to the OEM.
  4. Save the Full review's predictions to the ledger. When the panel results arrive, record hits and misses.
  5. When a real brief catches the brain out, bring it back to this chat as a new held-out test.

Scorekeeper · test bank and skip-me score

Does the brain catch faults on its own?

The exam runs test formulas through the automatic checks with no help and grades them against answer keys. Case 1 is the training case the senior rules came from. Tests 2 to 5 are held out: a rose softener, a white-musk extrait, a durian joke brief and a "fragrance-free" detergent, with faults planted without writing any new rule. Tests 6 to 8 were written blind by an independent agent that never saw the rules. The brain caught 9 of their 17 faults on the first run. The misses were fixed as general rules and the tests now guard against regressions. The skip-me score counts findings you had to trigger yourself. The goal is zero.

The held-out keys live in this page's code but are never sent to any Claude agent, so the Standard and Full reviews cannot see them. A missed finding is a real gap: fix the engine generally, never just for the test. Add a new held-out test every time a real brief catches the brain out.

05 · SELF-STUDY

What the world's best sellers teach

The brain studied the commercial icons of perfumery and the science of why people like a smell. The aim is to learn the grammar, not copy the sentences: find the familiar frame people already love, then break new ground on top of it, the way technology does.

The headline finding · measured on the icons themselves

Hits are about 70% familiar and 30% new

Each icon was turned into a smell profile on the brain's 24 dimensions, then compared with the closest icon launched before it. The median hit sat at a similarity of 71. Half of all hits fell between 57 and 80. Only genre-openers such as Cool Water (34), Shalimar (36) and Angel (48) launched far from everything before them, and they are the rare exceptions. Close followers above 84 (Acqua di Giò, Invictus, Good Girl) sold too, helped by reach. White Diamonds sits at 92 to No. 5 and still sold over $1.5bn on a star's name, a reminder that scent is not the only driver. This is the familiar-surprise principle, measured.

Method: profiles authored from public note pyramids, and early icons had few predecessors to be compared with, so they are estimates of character, not formulas. Treat the numbers as directions, then calibrate with panel data in the prediction ledger.

Why people love a smell: the science

Sourced brief from a research agent, 3 Nov 2026, 35 searches, snippets only. U marks figures that are uncertain or single-source.

1 · The molecule matters more than the culture

41% molecule, 54% person, 6% culture

Across 9 to 10 cultures, from hunter-gatherers to city dwellers, the molecule explained 41% of liking, the individual 54%, and culture only 6%. Vanillin ranked most pleasant everywhere, then a fruity ester (ethyl butyrate), then linalool. Cheesy, sweaty isovaleric acid ranked last. Arshamian et al. 2022

2 · Pleasantness is written in chemistry

Bigger, more complex, no sulfur

Pleasantness is the main axis of smell perception, and it lines up with molecular properties well enough to predict odours people have never smelled (Khan 2007). Larger, more complex molecules tend to be liked, sulfur ones disliked (Keller and Vosshall 2016).

3 · Most advanced yet acceptable

New, but still typical

People like new designs as long as novelty does not break typicality (Hekkert 2003, testing Loewy's MAYA). Hit Makers calls it "familiar surprise". The icon data below agrees: hits launched at a median similarity of 71 to the closest earlier hit.

4 · Repetition builds liking, up to a point

Aim for pleasant, then let wearing do the rest

Repeated exposure made every odour more familiar, but raised liking most for neutral to mildly pleasant ones, barely for strongly pleasant or unpleasant ones (Delplanque 2015). One sniff on a blotter is the wrong test for a new idea.

5 · Everyone is blind to something

Layer musks and ambers

Some people cannot smell certain musks (OR5A2, OR4D6 variants) or androstenone (2% to about 50% depending on the test). Retail figures for Iso E Super (20 to 25%) and Ambroxan (about 20%) are U. Almost nobody is blind to every musk, which is why skin bases layer three or more.

6 · Names and colours change the smell

Story is part of the formula

The same molecule was far more unpleasant labelled "body odour" than "cheddar" (de Araujo 2005). White wine dyed red was described as red by tasters (Morrot 2001). Name, colour and liquid shade steer what people think they smell.

7 · Freshness is also a feeling

The trigeminal nerve

Cooling (TRPM8) and tingle read as freshness, but the same pathways turn into irritation at higher doses (TRPA1), and mixtures add up. A mild cool or pepper cue lifts a fresh idea. Too much reads as medicine or burn.

8 · Region changes the volume, not the liking

Intensity norms differ

China leans fresh and light (over 50% prefer fresh, 3% "strong", U). The Gulf favours oud and musk at high strength. India buys on longevity. Humid Southeast Asia amplifies sweet and heavy notes. Japan's "kōgai" (scent pollution) backlash logged 928 softener complaints from 2014 to 2020.

9 · Laundry taught the world "clean"

Sion's hidden advantage and risk

White musks such as Galaxolide make "clean" the most widely learned smell in Asia and the US. That makes it the strongest familiar anchor a laundry brand can use, and also the most saturated. Scent boosters show demand for lasting laundry scent. The Japan backlash shows the ceiling.

Studying is not copying

The anti-copy rule

The compass flags any formula above 90 similar to an icon as a near copy. The brain never reproduces a commercial formula, uses a commercial perfume's name to sell, or builds "smells like" products. Icons are studied the way architects study buildings: for structure, proportion and what made people stay.

06 · COMPOSER LAYERS

How a brief becomes a formula

Eight layers, run in order. Layers 2 to 4 are where a language model adds the most. Layers 1, 6 and 7 are deterministic code, because safety, regulation and dosing must never depend on a model's mood.

07 · THREE HEADS

Scent 1, 2 and 3

Your umbrella, kept and sharpened. Every brief always returns all three, in this order, so a person learns the brand's grammar after one use.

Scent 1 · Creative

The brain's own reading

Finds the dominant feeling, then adds a deliberate contrast. Firecrackers get a milky, nostalgic side. Chocolate gets hot metal. This is where new, unexplored scents come from.

  • Starts from 10 picks, prefers bridge accords
  • Twist strength set by the frame (0.6 default, 1.3 in Unexplored)
  • Extra input later: a Twist dial and a "surprise me" seed
Scent 2 · Accurate

As close as wearable allows

Follows the brief literally, through the translation ladder. Strange briefs stay strange, but the beauty floor stops them smelling like the real thing when the real thing is foul.

  • Starts from 8 picks, direct hints weighted highest
  • Fidelity score is the main metric
  • Never uses the Wearer Lens, so it stays a pure reading
Scent 3 · Luxury

Couture direction

The same idea in finer materials: orris, sandalwood, rose absolute, oud accord, ambergris-type. Longer dry-down, smoother edges, more base.

  • Starts from 12 picks, cost bias toward naturals
  • Wearer Lens applies here: climate, moment, projection
  • Highest concentration option per format
Decision · the personality test

No onboarding quiz. A 3-tap Wearer Lens instead.

The brief is the product, so it comes first and stands alone. A personality test is a proxy. It guesses what you might like from who you might be. The Wearer Lens only asks things that physically change how a perfume performs: the climate you live in, when you wear it, and how far it should travel. It is optional, comes after the brief, and only steers Scent 3 and the dose. A fun "vibe quiz" can still exist as marketing content and data collection, but it never overrides what someone typed.

08 · TRANSLATION RULES

The ladder, the floor and the gate

Most briefs name things that have no smell, or a bad one. The brain climbs a four-rung ladder until it reaches something a perfumer can build.

RUNG 1 · LITERALIt has a good smell

Build it directly from materials or a bridge accord.

Soybeans → soy milk & green bean accord
RUNG 2 · EVOCATIVEIt smells bad

Keep its signature at trace level and wrap it in something beautiful.

Feces chocolate → indolic trace, patchouli, cocoa
RUNG 3 · ASSOCIATIVEIt has no smell

Smell the things around it: places, materials, weather.

DEFCON 3 → hot metal, aldehydes, pepper
RUNG 4 · ABSTRACTIt is pure idea

Map by synesthesia: temperature, brightness, texture, tempo.

DMT → ozone, ritual smoke, electric violet leaf
Rule · Beauty floor

Odd is allowed. Foul is not.

Malodor materials (indolic, fecal, sulfurous, smoky tar) have hard caps in the palette. If the harsh share of a formula passes 45%, the validator moves 15% of the formula into musk and ambergris-type comfort. Creative may go to 55%.

Rule · Ethics gate

Some briefs are declined

Sexual violence, abuse of children, atrocities against real groups, self-harm and hate are never turned into a product. The brain says so plainly and offers the feeling the person may have been reaching for, such as danger, menace or night.

Rule · Persona

Read the aesthetic, never the person

"Smells like Kanye West" is read as a public aesthetic. The brain never claims to know how a real person smells, and the person's name never goes on a bottle or in marketing without a licence (right of publicity).

Rule · Themes

War and drugs as art

War, nuclear and drug briefs are accepted as artistic themes. Copy stays solemn and never glorifies. Drug briefs get the experience, never the substance: no claims of effects and no cannabis-derived ingredients.

Rule · Proposer and disposer

The model never has the last word

Claude or any future model proposes. Local code checks every material ID against the palette, applies caps, enforces the beauty floor, fixes the sum to 100 and converts to grams or millilitres. Nothing a model writes can push a material past its cap.

Rule · Regulation

IFRA comes before taste

The caps on this page are working placeholders. Before launch, every cartridge needs an IFRA certificate from its supplier for the 51st Amendment (fully in force since Oct 2025, check for a 52nd), per product category: detergent, softener and fine fragrance each have different limits. Allergen labelling (EU) follows from the final formula.

11 · LEARNING LOOP

How the brain gets smarter

Today the brain is rules plus a language model. Every order turns it into something no competitor has: a dataset of free-text briefs paired with formulas and with what people actually thought when they smelled them.

The flywheel
  1. Person types a brief and picks a frame and format
  2. Brain returns three formulas and stores the full trace
  3. Person chooses one (the choice itself is a label)
  4. Machine or lab dispenses. Sample ships.
  5. Person rates it on fidelity, beauty and wear after 7 days
  6. Perfumer reviews the outliers, best and worst
  7. Rated pairs train the next version of the brain
Model versions
  • v0 · now Rules, lexicon and palette on this page, plus Claude for reading the brief.
  • v1 · 1k ratings Retrieval: similar past briefs and their best-rated formulas are fed to the model as examples.
  • v2 · 10k ratings A learned preference model scores candidate formulas before anyone smells them.
  • v3 · house A molecular layer built on an odor map (OpenPOM, trained on about 5,000 molecules) to design new accords and check that a formula lands where the brief points.

Research intake

What the brain reads

  • Pyrfume: 40+ odorant datasets, one format, licence per dataset
  • GoodScents + Leffingwell: about 5,000 molecules, 138 descriptors (the "5,000 scent database")
  • DREAM mixture challenges: 650 to 700+ rated mixtures
  • Classic texts: Arctander, Calkin & Jellinek
  • Patents for laundry substantivity rules
  • IFRA Standards library and supplier certificates
Do not scrape

Licence first

Fragrantica, Parfumo (about 231,000 perfumes) and Basenotes are consumer databases with terms that forbid scraping. Use them for human research and taste, or license the data. Leffingwell carries a restricted licence. Your own rated genomes are the only data you fully own.

Evaluation

How we know it works

  • Blind triangle tests: can people tell Scent 2 matches its brief?
  • Beauty: 9-point hedonic scale, target mean 6.5+
  • Wear: longevity on skin and on dry fabric at 24 h
  • Perfumer veto rate below 10% before v2 ships

From wash to fragrance house

Stage 1Sion Wash

Custom-scented detergent

The brain runs in detergent and softener formats only. Formulas are blended by hand from 24 laundry accords. Every order creates a Scent Genome record.

  • Pick a fragrance supplier who will build and IFRA-certify 24 laundry accords
  • Stability test each accord in your base: 4 weeks at 40 °C, colour and pH
  • Launch the brief box on the site with the three-scent result
  • Collect the first 1,000 ratings
Stage 2The machine

64-cartridge dispenser

The brain sends a dispense program: cartridge ID and grams or millilitres, at 0.01 ml resolution. Fine fragrance formats open up. Retrieval from rated genomes switches on (v1).

  • Gravimetric dosing (weigh, do not just pump) for accuracy below 1%
  • Cartridge freshness tracking: citrus and aldehydes oxidise
  • Batch log per bottle for recalls and repeat orders
Stage 3The house

Sion fragrance house

A full organ, in-house perfumers, captive accords designed with a molecular model (v3). The brain becomes the house's creative director: it drafts, the perfumer signs.

  • Signature collection built from the most-loved briefs
  • Licensed collaborations replace unlicensed persona briefs
  • Open the brief box to other brands as a B2B service
